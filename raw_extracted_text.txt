ABSTRACT
Graphic organizers can pick out and organize key information to release students' cognitive effort 
from their lower-order thinking for their higher-order thinking (dispositions, cognitive processes, 
skills), but studies showed mixed results. Hence, this meta-analysis of 4824 students in 66 
empirical studies with 72 effect sizes determine the overall effect size of graphic organizers on 
higher order thinking and moderators that explain the different results. Use of graphic organizers 
showed an overall positive link with higher order thinking (g = 0.778). The effect size is stronger 
(a) with mind maps, tapering for argument maps and concept maps; (b) in secondary school, 
tapering for primary school and university; (c) for cognitive processes and dispositions than 
outcome skills; (d) for divergent thinking than convergent thinking; (e) in countries with higher 
GDP per capita, and (f) in later publication years. Gender, academic subject and study duration 
showed no moderation effects. These results inform teachers' judicious use of graphic organizers 
to boost their students ’ higher order thinking.
1. Introduction
In our information-rich modern world, education has shifted from understanding it, recalling it, and applying it ( lower-order 
thinking , LOT) to analyzing it, evaluating it, and creating it ( higher-order thinking [HOT]; Bloom, 1956 ; Zohar and Ben David, 2009 ). 
Students with better higher-order thinking (domain general) than others often show more learning motivation ( Purnama & 
Nurdianingsih, 2019 ), understand ideas more deeply ( Thamrin & Widodo, 2019 ), show greater autonomy and initiative ( Smith & 
Darvas, 2017 ), and have better learning outcomes in school (e.g. Tanujaya et al., 2017 ).
Hence, visual tools that pick out key information and spatially organize it (e.g., charts, diagrams, maps; graphic organizers , GO) 
might release students' cognitive effort from their lower-order thinking to their higher-order thinking ( Eppler & Burkhard, 2008 ). 
However, studies showed mixed results. Graphic organizers boost students' higher-order thinking in some studies (e.g., Achor et al., 
2024 ; Min et al., 2023 ) but not others ( Carwie, 2009 ; Su & Long, 2021 ). Differences in participants, settings, or study designs might 
explain these results. Hence, our meta-analysis of 4824 students in 66 studies (a) determines the overall relation between graphic 
organizer and higher-order thinking and (b) identifies moderators that account for different results across studies. Understanding 
graphic organizer's effect on higher-order thinking and factors that boost its effectiveness can help teachers and their students navigate 
the information avalanche from generative artificial intelligence (e.g., large language models like ChatGPT; Kelly et al., 2023 ).

2. Conceptual framework
2.1. Higher-order thinking
Higher-order thinking is defined as comprising dispositions and cognitive processes that yield the skills of analysis, evaluation, 
inference, deductive reasoning, and inductive reasoning ( Hwang et al., 2021 ). Higher-order thinking dispositions drive higher-order 
thinking cognitive processes to yield its outcomes/skills. Consider this example. Ana saw dandelions growing in poisoned soil near 
factories, and was curious about how they thrived. Her open mind led her to many sources, including journal articles and a professor 
studying how plants remove contaminants. She read about plants that absorbed metals from poisoned earth. These concepts were hard 
to grasp, but she persisted. Unsatisfied with abstract ideas, she systematically tested them by examining dandelions in clean and dirty 
soil. The professor generously helped her use special equipment to find that dandelions in polluted soil had more heavy metals and 
more intricate roots. She assessed these complicated roots to be relevant rather than coincidence. Synthesizing this information about 
heavy metals and intricate roots, she creatively inferred that genetic mutations gave dandelions such roots, which helped them survive 
the poisoned soil. Reflecting on her results, she considered other possible causes, like soil pH and water availability, showing her 
critical thinking and creativity.
Higher-order thinking dispositions are orientations and attitudes, such as Ana's curiosity, open-mindedness, perseverance, and 
systematicity ( Lim, 2017 ). Her curiosity engages her to ask questions (how do dandelions grow in poisoned soil?), dig for answers, find 
new ideas, and explore them ( Rauscher & Badenhorst, 2021 ). Her open-mindedness let her see many sides of a story (heavy metals, 
intricate roots, soil pH) and adapt to such new information ( Arviani et al., 2023 ). Her perseverance keeps her going through difficult 
tasks and setbacks (e.g., difficult concepts) to eventually master skills ( Galita & Bonta, 2023 ). Her systematicity pushes her to organize 
her thoughts (dandelions, soil, growth), break down problems into simpler parts they can handle (clean vs. dirty soil), and plan and 
solve problems (compare dandelions in clean vs. dirty soil; Rauscher & Badenhorst, 2021 ). Hence, her curiosity and open-mindedness 
opened doors to new ideas, while her perseverance and systematicity pushed her beyond her initial efforts.
These dispositions motivated Ana's higher-order thinking cognitive processes of analysis, evaluation, synthesis, and inference 
( Thamrin & Widodo, 2019 ). She examined dandelions to understand their parts (heavy metals, intricate roots; analysis ). Then, she 
assessed the relevance of each part to solving her problem (relevance of roots; evaluation ). Next, she fit relevant parts together (relate 
heavy metals to intricate roots; synthesis ). Based on her organized information, she drew conclusions (genetic mutation aided survival; 
inference ).
These higher-order thinking cognitive processes helped her achieve her higher-order thinking outcomes of critiques/critical 
thinking, creativity, or problem solving ( Tikhonova & Kudinova, 2015 ). Analysis and evaluation especially aid critical thinking (why 
intricate roots?), and synthesis and inference particularly facilitate creativity (genetic mutation can aid survival). Nevertheless, any of 
these cognitive processes might support critical thinking, creativity or problem solving (as in Ana's example).
2.2. Graphic organizer
Graphic organizer is defined as a visual tool that pick out key information and organize it (e.g., charts, diagrams, maps; Eppler & 
Burkhard, 2008 ). We can organize information via a spatial metaphor : time is space; ideas are objects at locations; ideas with shared 
attributes look similar (color, shape, size, etc.); related ideas are nearby objects; causation is moving from one location to another, and 
so on ( Lakoff & Johnson, 2024 ). Applying this spatial metaphor, graphic organizers pick out key ideas, organize them and their re -
lations, and store them on a surface (e.g., paper, screen), which reduces the cognitive load ( Mayer, 2002 ) of understanding and 
recalling (lower-order thinking) to free brain resources for higher-order thinking ( Eppler & Burkhard, 2008 ; Lee, 2012 ). See example of 
graphic organizer of Ana's higher-order thinking in Fig. 1 .

2004 ; Hay, 2007 ; Romero García et al., 2017 ) and learning outcomes ( Dexter et al. 2011 ), especially for understanding texts (science: 
Roman et al., 2016 ; social studies: Meo, 2008 ; language learning: Imsa-ard, 2022 ). Hence, we propose this hypothesis. 
H-1 . Students using graphic organizers outperform other students on higher-order thinking.
However, some graphic organizers, especially poor ones, can hinder higher-order thinking by worsening cognitive load, split 
attention, or decrease coherence. Some highlight small details, jumble the order of facts, or mismatch attributes (e.g., color, size, 
shape) with their meanings ( Schnotz & Bannert, 2003 ). Students might fixate on a complicated graphic organizer (see Fig. 1 ) instead of 
listening to the teacher, which can burden their minds with unnecessary details ( excessive cognitive load , Sweller et al., 2011 ). Also, the 
information in the graphic organizer might not align with their learning goals and obstruct their understanding ( Schnotz & Bannert, 
2003 ). Even as their teacher lectures, students ’ thoughts might wander back to the graphic organizer. This can split their attention, 
break their focus, disrupt the steady flow of information ( Hittleman, 1985 ). Also, some students know the basic ideas but need the 
details from the lecture. These students can waste time on the graphic organizer, especially one tangled with tangential ideas (less 
coherence). These can slow learning ( redundancy effect , Sweller et al., 2011 ) or cause students to ignore the lecture altogether ( Schnotz, 
2014 ).
2.3. Potential moderators
To account for past studies ’ different results, we test for moderation effects of higher-order thinking measures, graphic organizer 
types, demographics, and study designs.
2.3.1. Higher-order thinking measures
2.3.1.1. Higher-order thinking components: dispositions, cognitive processes, and skills. Studies assessed higher-order thinking disposi -
tions, cognitive processes, or outcomes/skills. As higher-order thinking dispositions and higher-order thinking cognitive processes 
precede higher-order thinking outcomes, successful higher-order thinking outcomes require both suitable higher-order thinking dis -
positions and higher-order thinking cognitive processes ( Thamrin & Widodo, 2019 ; Tikhonova & Kudinova, 2015 ). By contrast, neither 
the dispositions nor the cognitive processes require higher-order thinking outcomes ( Thamrin & Widodo, 2019 ; Tikhonova & 
Kudinova, 2015 ). Hence, graphic GO-HOT links are likely larger when assessing higher-order thinking dispositions or cognitive 
processes rather than higher-order thinking skills. 
H-2 . The positive link between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking is stronger for dispositions or cognitive processes than 
skills.
2.3.1.2. Divergent vs. convergent higher-order thinking. Some researchers divide higher-order thinking into (a) logical thinking and 
assessment, including critical thinking ( convergent ) and (b) original, flexible, creative thinking ( divergent ; Lu et al., 2025 ; Zhan et al., 
2023 ). As graphic organizers organize ideas with a concise, structured representation of idea (e.g., hierarchies, networks, flow dia -
grams), they reduce cognitive load, especially LOT and convergent thinking ( Ezzeldin, 2017 ). This frees more resources for more 
difficult, divergent thinking ( Eppler & Burkhard, 2008 ), creation of new ideas ( Leeds et al., 2019 ), or deeper students ’ conceptual

2.3.2. Graphic organizer types
Graphic organizer types include types of maps (e.g., mind, concept, argument; Kansızo ˘glu, 2017 ), fishbone diagrams, Venn dia -
grams, flowcharts, and so on ( Scott, 2011 ). However, most studies of graphic organizers and higher-order thinking used mind maps, 
concept maps or argument maps (see Fig. 2 ). Mind maps are visual diagrams that organize ideas and information around a central 
concept, with branches representing related themes and subtopics ( Edwards & Cooper, 2010 ). Often used in brainstorming, students 
using mind maps identified more relations among ideas and used these relations to remember more, compared to other students 
( Farrand et al., 2002 ; Palaniappan et al., 2023 ). By contrast, concept maps often summarize key ideas and relations, so they are more 
complex and can become unwieldy compared to the single-concept focus of mind maps ( Shi et al., 2023 ). Indeed, students often learn 
more from mind maps than concept maps ( Abbas et al., 2018 ; Redhana et al., 2021 ), though their effects on critical did not differ 
significantly in Suardana et al. (2020) .
Whereas concept maps and mind maps organize ideas and their relations for greater understanding, argument maps structure the 
logic behind claims, evidence, warrants and backing to persuade others of a specific conclusion ( Davies, 2011 ). Argument maps boost 
students ’ critical thinking during structured writing ( Botley & Hakim, 2014 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) and problem-solving ( Hoffmann, 2005 ). 
As arguments are typically more structured than brainstorming, we expect the added structure of a mind map to be much more 
beneficial to brainstorming than an argument map to argumentation. Like mind maps, arguments have clear lines, so both are often 
more organized than concept maps ( Davies, 2011 ). Indeed, among students studying science, those using argument maps learned more 
than those using concept maps ( Kabatas ¸ Memis ¸ & Karakus ¸ , 2021 ). Hence, we propose the following hypothesis. 
H-4 . The positive link between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking is strongest for mind maps, weaker for argument maps, 
and weakest for concept maps.
2.3.3. Demographics
Demographics include national income, age, and year. Countries with higher national income ( gross domestic product [GDP] per 
capita) often spend more money to create better technology or train their teachers better ( Nelson, 2005 ). Better technology can yield 
better graphic organizers. Such graphic organizers can organize key ideas and their relations better to help students learn them more 
easily. Furthermore, better teachers can use graphic organizers more effectively to help their students learn more. Hence, graphic 
organizers might have stronger effects on higher-order thinking in richer countries than in poorer ones. 
H-5 . The positive link between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking is higher in countries with higher GDP per capita.
Age might moderate the effect of graphic organizer on higher-order thinking. As older students ’ brains are more mature (e.g., 
formal operations of secondary school students vs. concrete operations of primary school students; Carey et al., 2015 ), they likely have 
superior higher-order thinking and representational skills. So, they have more skills that graphic organizers can activate, and can better 
use graphic organizers. Moreover, unimportant graphic organizer details (e.g., images) more readily distract primary school students 
and hinder their graphic organizer use, compared to older students ( seductive-details effect , Rowland-Bryant et al., 2009 ). Compared to 
younger students, secondary school students often have stronger ( meta- ) representational skills ( diSessa and Sherin, 2000 ; Pande & 
Chandrasekharan, 2017 ; Uttal & Doherty, 2008 ), so they can use graphic organizers more effectively ( Hillmann, 1993 ). As univer -
sity students have mature representation skills, they can readily create their own spatial metaphors to pick out key ideas and organize 
them, reducing the comparative benefit of an external graphic organizer ( self-scaffolding , Bickhard, 2013 ). Hence, graphic organizer 
benefits might be highest among secondary school students and lowest among university students. Indeed, concept maps benefit 
secondary school students more than university students ( Nesbit & Adesope, 2006 ). 
H-6 . The positive link between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking is strongest for secondary school students, tapering off 
for primary school students, and lowest among university students.
Later years often bring more advanced technology ( Fraillon et al., 2014 ) and progress toward better teaching methods ( Compayre 
& Payne, 2015 ). Advanced technology can yield more effective graphic organizers. Also, teachers using better teaching methods can 
help their students use a graphic organizer more effectively. Hence, we expect studies published in later years to show larger effect 
sizes of graphic organizers on higher-order thinking. 
H-7 . The positive link between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking is larger in later publication years.
2.3.4. Other moderators
We do not expect gender, academic subject, or study duration to moderate the link between graphic organizer and higher-order 
thinking. Indeed, gender did not moderate the link between concept mapping and critical thinking in a prior analysis ( Barta et al., 
2022 ). Also, meta-analyses of mind mapping and cognitive outcomes showed no significant academic subject moderations ( Nesbit 
& Adesope, 2006 ; Shi et al., 2023 ) or study duration moderations ( Shi et al., 2023 ). Still, following other meta-analyses, we test for 
their possible moderation effects.

2.4. This study
Past studies of graphic organizers and higher-order thinking showed mixed results. So, this meta-analysis adjudicates the overall 
effect of the independent variable graphic organizer on the dependent variable higher-order thinking and tests for moderators (graphic 
organizer, higher-order thinking, demographics, etc.) that explain the different results.
3. Methods
3.1. Literature search and coding
3.1.1. Literature search
We identified relevant primary studies by searching seven databases: Google Scholar, Web of Science, Elsevier SDOL, EBSCO, 
Springer, PsycINFO and ProQuest Digital Dissertations. We used the following combination of search terms: ( “ diagram ” OR “ graphic 
organizer ” OR “ mind mapping ” OR “ concept mapping ” OR “ argument mapping ” OR “ cognitive mapping ” ) AND ( “ critical thinking ” OR 
“ higher-order thinking ” OR “ reasoning ” OR “ creative thinking ” OR “ problem solving ” OR “ argumentation ” OR “ argumentative 
thinking ” OR “ decision-making ” OR “ metacognitive thinking ” OR “ creativity ” ). If an original study was not publicly available, we tried 
to get it from a school library or by emailing the authors. We completed this search process in January 2025, retrieving 665 articles.
3.1.2. Inclusion criteria
We applied the following inclusion criteria to these studies: (a) empirically tested the relation between the independent variable 
graphic organizer use and the dependent variable higher-order thinking; (b) had a quasi-experimental, experimental, or pre-post 
design; (c) had sufficient statistical information to calculate the effect size (e.g., sample sizes, means, standard deviations) (See 
Fig. 3 ). After applying these criteria, 66 articles remained.
3.1.3. Quality assessment
Two authors independently assessed the quality of each study with Kmet, Lee, and Cook's (2004) fourteen-item checklist. These 
items helped assess the internal validity of quantitative research across multiple methodological dimensions. One such item is the 
clarity and completeness of the research question/objective. Each item was rated as “ yes ” (2 points), “ partial ” (1 point), or “ no ” (0 
points) for a maximum of 28 points. To accommodate the heterogeneity of quantitative study designs, some items can be designated 
“ not applicable ” (N/A). All 66 original studies had at least moderate quality (over 50%; M = 23.75 [85%]) and were included in this

3.1.4. Study feature coding
For each study, two authors coded its features ( Lipsey & Wilson, 2001 ): study name, publication year, sample size, effect size, GDP 
per capita, grade level, gender, higher-order thinking measures (disposition, cognitive process vs. skill; convergent vs. divergent), 
graphic organizer type (mind map, concept map, argument map), academic subject, and study duration (see Table 1 ). In one study ( Su, 
2018 ), the teacher created the graphic organizer and gave it to the students. In all other studies, the students created the graphic 
organizer. A common sequence in graphic-organizer-based learning activities across the studies involved teachers introducing the 
basic knowledge of the graphic organizer used in the intervention, students generating their own graphics, and teachers providing 
feedback. Krippendorff's (2011) α between the two authors' ratings is 0.813, showing acceptable inter-rater reliability.
Researchers measured higher-order thinking dispositions, cognitive processes, and skills in different ways. To measure higher-order 
thinking disposition, researchers in all 34 primary studies used Likert-type scales (strongly disagree to strongly agree) with questions 
such as “ I like to ask something that no one has imagined before ” (e.g. Chiu & Hwang, 2024 ). Meanwhile, researchers in all 17 studies 
measuring cognitive processes asked participants to solve open problems via writing. Then, they used scoring rubrics to capture these 
cognitive processes ( Lane, 1993 ). For example, Khrais & Saleh (2020) asked students to read a letter with flawed reasoning and then 
write an essay assessing the claims in the letter. Then, the researchers applied a rubric to assess participants' quality of reasoning 
(especially counter-arguments), relevance and clarity of critiques, and use of evidence. Lastly, researchers in all 57 studies used 
multiple-choice tests to measure higher-order thinking skills. These include standardized tests, like the California Critical Thinking 
Skills Test (CCTST, Facione, 1990 ). An example item is “ the happiness level in some countries can be higher or lower than in others 
with the same personal income level. The statement is: (a) True; (b) Probably True; (c) Insufficient Data; (d) Probably False; (e) False. ” 
Studies in the field of nursing in US used the computerized exams by Health Education Systems, Inc. ( Morrison et al., 2004 ). Other 
researchers developed and validated multiple-choice questions with high reliability to test students ’ higher-order thinking skills. (e.g., 
Min et al., 2023 ). We test whether these different measures of the outcome variable higher-order thinking (disposition, cognitive 
process, skill) moderate the relation between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking ( Chiu & Roberts, 2018 ).
3.2. Data processing and analysis
3.2.1. Effect size computation
For each study, we computed the effect size Hedge's g ( V ø llestad et al., 2012 ). If available, we used the sample sizes, means, and 
standard deviations in either (1) the post-test data of the experimental and control groups or (2) the pre-test and post-test data of the 
single experiment. Otherwise, we used χ
2
, t, or F values.
3.2.2. Homogeneity test
We computed Cochrane's Q and I
2 
to test for heterogeneity in the effect sizes across studies ( Huedo-Medina et al., 2006 ). Substantial 
heterogeneity (significant Q; I
2 
> 75) requires random-effects models and moderation tests ( Lipsey & Wilson, 2001 ).
3.2.3. Publication bias
To evaluate publication bias, we made a funnel plot ( Lin & Chu, 2018 ) and ran Egger et al. ’ s (1997) regression. A sharply 
asymmetric funnel plot or a significant, non-zero regression intercept indicates publication bias.
3.2.4. Data analysis
We used the meta , dmetar and metafor packages in R (version 2022.02.0 + 443) for our meta-analysis ( Schwarzer et al., 2015 ). A 
three-level meta-analytic variance components model ( Assink & Wibbelink, 2016 ) showed no significant variance between effect sizes 
within studies (level 2), so we used the traditional two-level meta-analysis. Two random-effects models calculated the effect sizes and 
the homogeneity test, followed by moderator tests. Wald tests determined whether differences in effect sizes were significant ( Hansen, 
2022 ).

5.1. Overall positive GO-HOT link
The positive link between graphic organizer and higher-order thinking supports hypothesis H-1 . This result fits the view that 
graphic organizers use spatial metaphors ( Lakoff & Johnson, 2024 ) to select key ideas/relations and organize them, which reduces the 
cognitive load ( Mayer, 2002 ) of lower-order thinking and lets more brain processes engage in higher-order thinking ( Eppler & 
Burkhard, 2008 ; Lee, 2012 ). This result also aligns with past studies showing how teachers used graphic organizers to help their 
students learn new concepts ( Akinsanya & Williams, 2004 ; Hay, 2007 ; Romero García et al., 2017 ) and understand accompanying texts 
( Imsa-ard, 2022 ; Meo, 2008 ; Roman et al., 2016 ).
5.2. Higher-order thinking
The GO-HOT link was much stronger for higher-order thinking dispositions and cognitive processes than for higher-order thinking 
outcomes/skills, supporting H-2 . This result coheres with the idea that higher-order thinking outcomes require both dispositions and 
cognitive processes, whereas neither of them require higher-order thinking outcomes ( Thamrin & Widodo, 2019 ; Tikhonova & 
Kudinova, 2015 ). Hence, this moderation result highlights the importance of distinguishing higher-order thinking outcomes from 
dispositions and cognitive processes in theoretical models, empirical studies, and meta-analyses. Whether higher-order thinking 
dispositions and cognitive processes largely co-occur or not requires future studies that capture both phenomena.
Furthermore, GO-HOT link was larger for divergent higher-order thinking than convergent higher-order thinking, supporting 
Hypothesis 3. This result fits the view that graphic organizers help organize ideas to reduce cognitive load, especially the need for 
lower order thinking skills and effort for convergent thinking ( Ezzeldin, 2017 ). This frees up brain resources for divergent thinking 
( Eppler & Burkhard, 2008 ), such as creation of new ideas ( Leeds et al., 2019 ).
5.3. Graphic organizer
Unlike past studies that examined only one type of graphic organizer (e.g., Shi et al., 2023 ), this meta-analysis showed the relative 
effects of different graphic organizers. Specifically, the GO-HOT link was largest for mind maps, much smaller for argument maps, and 
smallest for concept maps, supporting H-4 . These results offer evidence to fit the view that the simpler mind-map organization sub -
stantially boosts relations among ideas during brainstorming ( Davies, 2011 ), compared to the more demanding, complex argument 
maps and concept maps ( Shi et al., 2023 ). These results also align with those of past studies showing that students often learn more 
using mind maps rather than concept maps ( Abbas et al., 2018 ; Redhana et al., 2021 ).
Furthermore, the larger GO-HOT link for argument maps than concept maps coheres with the view that the former is simpler and 
easier to use ( Davies, 2011 ). Argument maps typically have a linear, logical or temporal structure, so they have simpler organizations 
than concept maps, which can have any structure and thus be unwieldy ( Davies, 2011 ). This result is also consistent with those of past 
studies showing that students learn more science using argument maps than concept maps ( Kabatas ¸ Memis ¸ & Karakus ¸ , 2021 ).
5.4. Demographics
Extending past studies, this meta-analysis showed that country, publication year, and age moderated the GO-HOT link. Studies in 
richer countries or later years showed larger GO-HOT links, supporting H-5 and H-7 . These results fit the views that richer countries 
spend more money to create better graphic organizers or train better teachers to use them more effectively to help their students learn 
more ( Nelson, 2005 ). Likewise, later years often bring (a) more advanced technology ( Fraillon et al., 2014 ) for better graphic orga -
nizers and (b) better trained teachers ( Compayre & Payne, 2015 ) who can better use them to improve their students ’ learning.

Extending past studies, this meta-analysis showed that the GO-HOT link was strongest among secondary school students, much 
weaker for primary school students, and weakest among university students, supporting H-6 . This result not only aligns with that of a 
meta-analysis showing that concept maps benefit secondary students more than university students ( Nesbit & Adesope, 2006 ) but also 
extends it by showing the relative effect size of primary school students. The weakest GO-HOT link among university students fits the 
view that their strong representation skills let them create their own graphic organizers, so they do not need an external graphic 
organizer for their higher-order thinking as much as younger students do. The results for the younger students cohere with the view 
that compared to primary school students, secondary school students use graphic organizers more effectively. Specifically, they are less 
distracted by unimportant graphic organizer details ( seductive-details effect , Rowland-Bryant et al., 2009 ) and their stronger ( meta- ) 
representational skills lets them use graphic organizers more effectively than primary school students ( diSessa and Sherin, 2000 ; 
Hillmann, 1993 ; Pande & Chandrasekharan, 2017 ).
Gender, academic subject, and study duration did not moderate the GO-HOT link. These non-significant results are consistent with 
those of earlier meta-analyses ( Barta et al., 2022 ; Nesbit & Adesope, 2006 ; Shi et al., 2023 ).
5.5. Implications
These results have implications for theory, methodology and practice. For theory, these results show that any comprehensive 
theory of higher-order thinking must include available tools (e.g., graphic organizer), dispositions, cognitive processes, and outcomes/ 
skills within an ecological model with individual ages, country contexts, and time. Similarly, a rigorous theory of graphic organizers 
must distinguish among its different types (e.g., mind map vs. argument map vs. concept map), their affordances, disadvantages, and 
conditions of use.
Methodologically, researchers must separately measure higher-order thinking dispositions, cognitive processes and skills. Also, 
they can test the degree to which higher-order thinking dispositions co-occur with cognitive processes. As these meta-analysis results 
showed differences across student ages, regions, and years, researchers should consider how the local ecology might affect their study 
and thereby design it accordingly.
Practically, this meta-analysis suggests that students can use graphic organizers to boost their higher-order thinking. Hence, 
teachers can use these results to inform their use of graphic organizers. As graphic organizers raise higher-order thinking dispositions 
and cognitive processes much more than higher-order thinking outcomes/skills, teachers can scaffold their students to help them 
improve their higher-order thinking skills. As graphic organizers were more effective for secondary school students and in richer 
countries, educators can support graphic organizer use for them first, before bringing them to other students. As the GO-HOT link was 
stronger in later years, students might benefit even more from using graphic organizers in the future.
6. Limitations and implications
This meta-analysis has three primary limitations: studies of few types of graphic organizers, sparse details in the original studies, 
measures, and language of publication. As we found studies of only three types of graphic organizers and higher-order thinking, these 
results might not apply to other types of graphic organizers (e.g., fishbone diagrams). Hence, future primary studies can test whether 
other types of graphic organizers affect higher-order thinking, which will flow into more comprehensive meta-analyses later.
Second, sparse details in the primary studies preclude many moderation tests. For example, many studies lacked details about the 
students (e.g., prior achievement) and their teachers (e.g., years of experience), so future studies should report this information, which 
can enrich future meta-analyses.
Third, these primary studies used (a) participants ’ Likert-type scales to measure their higher-order thinking dispositions, (b) their 
writing to measure their higher-order thinking cognitive processes, and (c) their multiple-choice test responses to measure their 
higher-order thinking skills. So, these different methodologies might affect the study results. Hence, future studies can apply the same 
methodology or multiple methods to measure all outcomes. Also, primary studies mostly used three graphic organizers: mind maps, 
concept maps, or argument maps. As future primary studies examine flowcharts, Venn diagrams, fishbone diagrams and other graphic 
organizers, future meta-analyses can test whether these effects hold for different graphic organizers.
Lastly, this meta-analysis only included English publications. Authors of future meta-analysis who can read other languages can 
include primary studies not published in English.
7. Conclusion
This meta-analysis of 72 effect sizes from 66 studies of 4824 students from 19 countries showed that graphic organizers were 
positively linked to higher-order thinking (g = 0.778). This link was stronger for higher-order thinking dispositions and conceptual 
processes than for higher-order thinking skills; for mind maps, tapering for argument maps, and concept maps; for divergent higher- 
order thinking than convergent higher-order thinking; in counties with higher GDP per capita, for secondary school students, tapering 
for primary school students and university students; and in later publication years.
