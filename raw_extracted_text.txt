ABSTRACT  
Education hubs represent the third generation of crossborder education activities, which emerge onto the land- 
scape of our more globalized world. As a new phenomenon in higher education, education hubs are appearing in 
different locations around the globe. In the literature on education hubs, most research studies have focused on 
Southeast Asia and Middle East. To address this gap in the literature, we seek to explore the emergence of China 
as an international education hub, especially with respect to its impact on development. Against the backdrop of 
China ’ s national strategy to develop its international competitiveness in education, we conduct case studies of 
three education hubs in China: (i) the International Collaborative Education Exemplary Zone by the International 
Campus of Zhejiang University (iZJU) in the Yangtze River Delta; (ii) the International Higher Education 
Demonstration Zone in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macau Greater Bay Area; and (iii) the International Education 
Innovation Pilot Zone in the Hainan Free Trade Port. Overall, our paper draws implication on how education 
hubs developed through university-industry-government collaboration can help the region to shift to a knowl -
edge and service-based economy, and gain a competitive edge within the country and beyond.   
1. Introduction 
With the concept of ‘universe ’ in the naming of ‘universities ’ , higher 
education has had an international dimension for centuries. Interna -
tionalization is changing the world of higher education, and globaliza -
tion is changing the world of internationalization. In higher education, 
globalization and internationalization are different but related. Global -
ization is defined as the broad economic, scientific, and technological 
trends that directly affect higher education ( Altbach, 2004, 2007 ). 
Internationalization is defined as the process of integrating an interna -
tional, intercultural, or global dimension into the purpose, functions, 
and delivery of higher education ( Knight, 2004 : p.11). Pushed and 
pulled along by the forces of globalization ( Altbach, 2009 ), interna -
tionalization includes the policies and practices that are undertaken by 
higher education systems and institutions to cope with globalization 
( Altbach and Knight, 2007 ). Globalization may be unalterable, but 
internationalization can involve many choices. Both as an actor and as a 
reactor to globalization, internationalization has been called upon to 
help contribute to tackle the complex global challenges faced by global 
society today ( de Wit and Altbach, 2021 : p.29). 
Internationalization has not only transformed higher education, but 
it has undergone fundamental changes itself. There are two pillars in the 
internationalization process of higher education: (1) ‘Internationaliza -
tion at Home ’ (IaH, Nilsson, 2003 ); and (2) ‘crossborder education ’ , that 
is, internationalization abroad. These two pillars are closely linked and 
interdependent. The IaH concept is defined as the purposeful integration 
of international and intercultural dimensions into the formal and 
informal curriculum for all students in domestic learning environments 
( Beelen et al., 2015 : p.69). Within the context of crossborder education, 
there are three generations of development ( Knight, 2011 : p.223). The 
first generation is academic mobility, which is about the movement of 
students and scholars around the world. The second generation is pro -
gram and provider mobility, which is about the movement of degree 
programs and higher education institutions between countries. Educa -
tion hubs build on the first and second generations of crossborder edu -
cation activities, and constitute the third generation of crossborder 
education initiatives, which demonstrate the responsive nature of 
internationalization to the complex realities of our more globalized 
world. 
In the literature on higher education, an education hub is defined as a 
planned effort to build a critical mass of local and international actors 
strategically engaged in education, training, knowledge production, and 
innovation initiatives ( Knight, 2011 : p.233). As a new phenomenon in 
the landscape of higher education, education hubs are appearing in

training to include the production and distribution of knowledge and 
innovation, with the primary objectives to help build a knowledge and 
service-based economy, and increase regional or global economic 
competitiveness.

3. Case studies: Education hubs in China 
As part of China ’ s regional development strategy, coordinated 
regional development is an objective which involves implementing both 
a strategy for overall coordination and a strategy that is focused on 
developing the main functional areas ( Du, 2014 ). There are four coor -
dinated development areas in China, including the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei 
Economic Circle ( Yu, 2015 ), the Yangtze River Delta Agglomeration,the 
Chengdu-Chongqing Economic Circle, and the Yangtze River Middle 
Reaches Urban Agglomeration ( Fig. 3 ). The regional integration of these 
four coordinated development areas are mutually reinforcing and jointly 
promoting high-quality growth in China.
16 
Based on their development 
experiences, the development of the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao 
Greater Bay Area (GBA) is also accorded the status of key strategic 
planning in China ’ s development blueprint. 
In the literature, higher education plays a critical role in regional 
development ( Harrison and Turok, 2017 ), with different drivers and 
dynamics at work, such as the ‘triple helix ’ model of uni -
versity-industry-government relations ( Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 
2000 ); the intersection of the learning region and local and regional 
economic development ( Shaw and Allison, 1999; Maskell et al., 2003 ); 
regional innovation systems ( Benneworth, Pinheiro, and Karlsen, 2017 ). 
In practice, the Chinese government has also engaged higher education 
institutions in regional development ( Wang and Vallance, 2015; Xie, 
Huang, and Jung, 2022 ). 
In this section, we conduct case studies of three education hubs in 
China, which include (i) the International Collaborative Education 
Exemplary Zone by the International Campus of Zhejiang University 
(iZJU). It is based in the Yangtze River Delta, which is one of China ’ s 
four coordinated development areas and is also one prioritized region in 
China ’ s 2035 Plan; (ii) the International Higher Education Demonstra -
tion Zone in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area; and 
(iii) the International Education Innovation Pilot Zone in the Hainan 
Free Trade Port. 
3.1. The iZJU International Collaborative Education Exemplary Zone 
(iZJU 国际合作教育样板区 ) 
Located in the Yangtze River Delta of China, Zhejiang University 
(ZJU) is among the top 50 universities in the world,
17 
and it is a member 
of the C9 League ( Huang, 2015 : p.207). At the forefront of higher edu -
cation, ZJU has been striving to become a pioneer of reform and an 
exemplar of development,
18

world-class university ( Xu, 2008 )
19

Xue, 2021 : p.14). To accelerate its efforts, ZJU began to build its inter -
national campus in 2013, which came into use in 2016, as a base for 
international collaboration. 
The International Campus of Zhejiang University (iZJU) is a pioneer 
of ‘Internationalization at Home ’ ( Qu and He, 2022; Qu and Dai, 2024 ) 
and an exemplar of international collaborative education in China. As a 
pioneer of ‘Internationalization at Home ’ , iZJU is China ’ s first campus 
for transnational higher education,
20 
with the unique characteristic of 
‘1 + X ′ , that is, iZJU can establish more than one Sino-foreign joint in -
stitutes on one campus.
21 
There are currently two Sino-foreign joint 
institutes at iZJU ( Table 2 ): Zhejiang University – University of Edin -
burgh Institute (the ZJU-UoE Institute, or ZJE), and Zhejiang University 
– University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign Institute (the ZJU-UIUC 
Institute, or ZJUI). 
22 
By partnering with world-class universities, iZJU 
seeks to combine the best practices of Eastern and Western education, so 
as to train globally minded and high-calibre innovators, and cultivate 
future leaders with a global vision. 
As one of ZJU ’ s eight campuses, iZJU has its own campus away from 
the main campus of ZJU in Hangzhou, but benefits from the knowledge 
spillovers among ZJU ’ s network model of campuses ( Fig. 4 : left panel). 
As a part of ZJU, iZJU has an organizational structure with two tiers: at 
the campus and the institute levels. At the campus level, the iZJU 
leadership team acts on behalf of ZJU to oversee the operation of the 
campus and the two Sino-foreign joint institutes, with a coordinating 
committee and an academic affairs committee. 
At the institute level, both of the two Sino-foreign joint institutes are 
operated by an iZJU-based academic and administrative team, a joint 
management committee (JMC), and an international advisory board 
(IAB), which together facilitate and coordinate the governance and 
management between ZJU and its foreign institutional partners, via a 
partnership model ( Fig. 4 : right panel). 
As an exemplar of international collaborative education, the location 
of iZJU in the Yangtze River Delta is one of the prioritized regions in the 
‘China ’ s Education Modernization 2035 Plan ’ in February 2019. The 
development of iZJU as an International Collaborative Education 
Exemplary Zone is a national strategy in the “ Outline of the Yangtze 
River Delta Regional Integrated Development Plan ” in December 
2019.

Among these major bay areas in the world, the Tokyo Bay Area is a 
metropolitan area that covers Tokyo and its seven surrounding pre -

San Francisco Bay Area consists of many cities along the industrial belt 
of Silicon Valley and covers 9 counties bordering the San Francisco Bay. 
It has a world-class university cluster and a university-industry-research 
cooperation model. The New York Bay Area has a dual-core-driven 
model for regional development, with New York as its financial center 
and Boston as its educational center ( Xie, Liu and McNay, 2023 ). 
Located in the southern coastal area of China, the GBA is a mega -
lopolis of a 11-city cluster, including nine cities in Guangdong Province, 
and the two special administrative regions of Hong Kong and Macau 
( Fig. 6 ). The GBA has a multi-core-driven model for regional develop -
ment, with Guangzhou as an education center, Shenzhen as an innova -
tion center, Hong Kong as a financial center, and Macau as a tourism 
center. Compared to the other three major bay areas, the GBA has the 
most extensive land area and the largest population, but it has the lowest 
per-capita GDP, and the lowest tertiary industry share ( Table 4 ). 
In 2019, the Chinese central government promulgated the “ Outline 
Development Plan for the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macau Greater Bay 
Area ” , which signified a new milestone in the development of the GBA. 
As a national strategy, the objectives of the GBA are to further deepen 
cooperation amongst Guangdong, Hong Kong SAR, and Macau SAR, 
fully leverage the comparative advantage of these three places, facilitate 
in-depth integration within the region, and promote coordinated 
regional development, which include the development of a talent hub 
and a knowledge-innovation hub. 
As higher education plays a strategic role in promoting innovation 
and entrepreneurship ( Chow, Hua, and Hung, 2020; Mok, 2022; Tang, 
2022 ), the development of a talent hub and a knowledge-innovation hub 
in the ‘Outline Development Plan for the GBA ’ is in accordance with the 
priority for the GBA in the ‘China ’ s Education Modernization 2035 
Plan ’ , which is to allow for greater mobility of professionals, and facil -

within the area.

4. Conclusion 
Internationalization is changing the world of higher education, and 
globalization is changing the world of internationalization (Knight, 
2004). Both as an actor and as a reactor to globalization, internation -
alization has not only transformed higher education, but it has under -
gone fundamental changes itself. There are two pillars in the 
internationalization process of higher education, which include ‘Inter -
nationalization at Home’ (IaH, Nilsson, 2003; Qu and Dai, 2024) and 
‘crossborder education’. These two pillars are closely linked and inter -
dependent. Within the context of crossborder education, there are three 
generations of development, which include academic mobility, program 
and provider mobility, and education hubs. Building on and extending 
the first and second generations of crossborder education activities, 
education hubs constitute the third generation of crossborder education 
initiatives, which demonstrate the changing and responsive nature of 
internationalization to the complex realities of our more globalized 
world. Based on the rationales of the activities, there are three different 
but linked models of education hubs, which include student hub, talent 
hub, and knowledge-innovation hub (Knight, 2011). 
As a new phenomenon in the landscape of higher education, edu -
cation hubs are appearing in different locations around the world, each 
with its own individual political, economic, and cultural context. In the 
literature on education hubs, most studies have focused on Southeast 
Asia and Middle East (Table 1), and relatively few studies have exam -
ined the case of China, which is both a source and a hub for international 
students (Wei, 2013; Ma, 2017; Wen, Hu, and Hao, 2018; Wen and Hu, 
2019; Tian and Liu, 2021). To address this gap in the literature, we 
explore the emergence of China as an international education hub, 
especially with respect to its impact on development. Against the 
backdrop of China’s national strategy to develop the country’s inter -
national competitiveness in education, we conduct case studies of three 
education hubs in China, which include (i) the International Collabo -
rative Education Exemplary Zone by the International Campus of Zhe -
jiang University (iZJU) in the Yangtze River Delta; (ii) the International 
Higher Education Demonstration Zone in the Guangdong-Hong Kong- -
Macau Greater Bay Area; and (iii) the International Education Innova -
tion Pilot Zone in the Hainan Free Trade Port (Table 7). 
Our first case study is the International Collaborative Education 
Exemplary Zone led by the International Campus of Zhejiang University 
(iZJU) in the Yangtze River Delta. It is a student hub, with the feature of 
‘Internationalization at Home’ for Chinese students (Qu and Dai, 2024), 
and the brand of ‘Study in Haining’ for international students. Moreover, 
it aims to become a knowledge-innovation hub and a talent hub, by 
cultivating a knowledge, innovation, and entrepreneurial ecosystem 
with the Juanhu International Science Park that is conducive to 
attracting international talent in education and science. The regional 
development model of iZJU is like a single-core-driven model with iZJU 
as the core, which leverages the strength of ZJU’s network model of 
campuses and iZJU’s partnership model with world-class universities 
(Fig. 4), unleashes the region’s technological and innovational capacity, 
and integrates the development of iZJU and the Haining city. It sets a 
role model for the transformation of other county-level cities in China. 
Our second case study is the International Higher Education 
Demonstration Zone in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macau Greater Bay 
Area (GBA). As higher education plays a strategic role in promoting 
innovation and entrepreneurship in the GBA (Chow, Hua, and Hung, 
2020; Mok, 2022; Tang, 2022), the objectives for the GBA are to be a 
talent hub that allows for greater mobility of professionals, and a 
knowledge-innovation hub that facilitates efficient exchange of higher 
education information and technology within the area. With over 200

The global advancement of the knowledge economy ( Altbach, 2013 ) 
and the growing internationalization of higher education has resulted in 
the emergence of a number of education hubs in different regions of the 
world. Our case studies of three education hubs in China show that these 
hubs face similar challenges and opportunities. For a student hub, a 
common challenge is how to develop an ecosystem that is conducive to 
attracting reputable international education providers; and a common 
opportunity is posed by the rising global demand for quality interna -
tional education nearer to home, that is, ‘Internationalization at Home ’ 
( Qu and Dai, 2024 ). For a talent hub, a common challenge and oppor -
tunity is the brain drain and brain gain for global talent mobility ( Mok, 
Zhang, and Bao, 2022 ). For a knowledge-innovation hub, a common 
opportunity is the dynamics of innovation from the ‘triple helix ’ model 
of university-industry-government relations ( Etzkowitz and Ley -
desdorff, 2000 ), and the ‘quadruple helix ’ of 
university-industry-government-society relations ( Kang and Jiang, 
2020 ), which is especially important for countries keen to shift to a 
knowledge and service-based economy, and gain a competitive edge in 
the region and beyond. 
In the aftermath of the COVID-19 global pandemic, the advancement 
in digital technology and innovation has driven a ‘virtual ’ internation -
alization of higher education, with universities expanding their digital 
footprints overseas, accelerating their distance education offerings, 
exploring virtual exchange programs, and adopting virtual communi -
cation platforms ( Wernick, 2023 ). As the three case studies in our paper 
are fledging education hubs from China, future studies could be con -
ducted to further examine how they evolve and thrive in response to an 
uncertain and dynamic global environment.
