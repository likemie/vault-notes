This paper examines the ways in which the results of the OECD Programme of International
Student Assessment (PISA) have influenced educational discourse in Germany. It argues that the
results caused shockwaves in the educational landscape and led to a re-evaluation of other interna-
tional comparisons which had presented an unfavourable picture of education in Germany. The
paper outlines three main areas affected by the reception of the PISA study in Germany: political
discourse, curriculum development processes and academic discourse on education. Changes in the
political discourse resulted in a wide-ranging reform agenda, with the introduction of national
educational standards being the most significant of a number of initiatives and programmes. In
terms of curriculum development processes, the paper argues that PISA has led to the growing
importance of principles such as outcome control, competence orientation and external assessment.
The post-PISA academic discourse in Germany can be characterised by the re-orientation of educa-
tional studies towards a greater emphasis on the empirical research of pedagogic practice (empirische
Unterrichtsforschung). All three areas of change are informed by more or less systematic comparisons
with educational structures in other countries and, therefore, it is argued that PISA has contributed
to a rediscovery of comparative education in the German discourse.
Introduction: the PISA-shock
The results of the first round of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development’s (OECD) PISA (Programme of International Student Assessment)
study in 2000 put Germany’s self-perception regarding education into perspective;
some would argue even regarding its societal and cultural values and achievements.
The PISA results have fundamentally undermined the implicit self-assessment of the

Because of these alarming findings, PISA resulted in a ‘shock’, officially admitted
by high-ranking politicians (cf. BMBF, 2005). Also, PISA has led to a re-evaluation
of other international comparisons which presented an unfavourable picture of
education in Germany. Whereas in the past the findings of comparative studies
attracted little public attention, they are followed much more closely in post-PISA
times. For instance, the weaknesses outlined in OECD’s 2004 Education at a Glance

Against this background, this paper argues that PISA can be regarded as a water-
shed in the discourse on education in Germany. Observers have compared the conse-
quences of PISA on education in Germany with the Sputnik shock (Ostermann,
2005) or even with the French Revolution (Herrmann, 2004) and have argued that
PISA has had a more far-reaching impact on German education than A Nation at Risk
had on education in the USA (Gruber, 2006). It is argued that the political and
academic discourse on education in Germany has changed mainly because a broad
societal consensus has emerged that reforms are inevitable. However, the emerging
reform agenda focuses on certain aspects of the educational structures (introduction
of national educational standards and quality assurance measures), while other
aspects are neglected. The most important of these aspects is the structure of second-
ary schooling. While politicians have moved surprisingly quickly in changing the
framework for teaching, assessment and curriculum development, there has been
little or no discussion on the controversial issue of the early selection of pupils. Also,
the paper shows that the importance of international comparisons for the educational
discourse in Germany has increased in the aftermath of PISA.
Consequences for the political discourse on education and the evolving 
reform agenda
The PISA results and their reception in the German context led to appeals for a
reform of secondary education from almost all relevant social groups, including polit-
ical parties, employers, trade unions (including teachers’ associations), parents’ asso-
ciations and academics. The Federal Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF)
reacted by taking a number of measures (see BMBF, 2005), none of which aimed at
structural issues of secondary education such as school types, early selection and
competition between schools.
Instead, the Ministry commissioned an experts’ report on the development of
national standards in education, commonly referred to as the Klieme Report (the
well-known educationist Eckehard Klieme chaired the committee of experts). This
focus on national educational standards ( nationale Bildungsstandards ) is new and
conceptually different from earlier attempts to agree on common standards for differ-
ent levels of education in Germany (see for instance, KMK, 1995). The Ministry
justified the focus on national educational standards by referring to countries which
were successful in PISA and in which standards and their regular evaluation are wide-
spread (BMBF, 2005).
The Klieme Report became the conceptual basis of the development of national
standards in education in Germany. According to the Report, ‘National educational
standards formulate obligatory requirements for teaching and learning in schools.

[…] They prescribe which competences pupils should attain at the end of certain
school years’ (Klieme et al., 2003, p. 9).5 The standards should not only provide clear
aims for education at schools, they should also be used as a basis for assessing pupils
(Klieme et al., 2003, pp. 9–10). They therefore represent a conceptual change in the
German context where educational aims and assessment are traditionally not very
closely connected.
Contrary to the expectations of most observers, however, national educational
standards were developed and implemented comparatively rapidly. The expectation
that appeals for national reform and commissioned expert reports in education do not
usually result in actual changes is derived from the experience with the federal struc-
ture in Germany, which gives each of the 16 federal Länder autonomy in matters of
culture and education (cf. Rürup, 2005). Also, the fact that the authors of the experts’
report concluded that ‘the development of nationally uniform educational standards
and performance criteria represents a very drastic turning-point within the federal
system of the Federal Republic of Germany’ (Klieme et al., January 2003, p. 116)
contributed to the scepticism of most observers.
However, as early as July 2003 blueprints of national standards for German, Math-
ematics and the first foreign language (English or French) for the end of Realschule
education (after ten years of schooling) were introduced. A revised version of the
standards was passed by the Standing Conference of Länder Ministers for Culture
and Education (Kultusministerkonferenz, KMK) in December 2003. All Länder agreed
to introduce the national standards at the beginning of the 2004/05 school year as a
basis for the development of new curricula and for initial and continuous teacher
training. From 2006 nationwide student assessment aims to examine whether all
Länder are meeting the standards.
Bung (2004) concluded that these unprecedented developments surprised many
who were convinced that a joint decision of all 16 Länder in the KMK would—as
usual—take several years, if an agreement could be reached at all. The introduction
of national standards amounts to an infringement of cultural autonomy, a principle
that has been jealously guarded by the Länder (Tillmann, 2004). The rejection of any
substantial competencies of the EU in education by the German Länder is only one
example of this (cf. Ertl, 2002).
As a result of the PISA shock, however, the 16 Länder agreed on national assess-
ments of students’ achievements. The assessment will be based on the requirements
specified in the new national educational standards. Currently, assessment criteria
and instruments are being developed which will make it possible to determine how
far the relevant national standards are being reached in each of the Länder. The Insti-
tute for Quality Development in Education ( Institut zur Qualitätsentwicklung im
Bildungswesen— IQB) was founded in 2004 and given the task of developing assess-
ment structures and conducting tests (KMK, 2005; IQB, 2006). This represents a
precedent in educational policy in Germany since the 1950s because it is for the first
time that the KMK, the body that co-ordinates the educational policies of the 16
Länder, has agreed on a national body that is responsible for quality assurance in
education beyond the level of the individual Länder.

The agreement on national educational standards and on an institutionalised struc-
ture to ensure that the standards are observed would have been inconceivable without
the impact of the PISA study. In almost every political document concerned with the
development of national standards the results of the study are used to make the case
for the radical reform of education in Germany. For instance, the Federal Ministry of
Education and Research has published a ‘post-PISA agenda’ (BMBF, 2005) in which
the reform spectrum includes the following diverse items: 
●Promotion of reading abilities: in the project LiD (Lesen in Deutschland, reading in
Germany), the Association of Libraries, the Association of Cities and Municipali-
ties, publishers, parents’ associations and teachers’ associations have joined forces
to establish a new ‘culture of reading’. The project aims at extra-curricular activi-
ties of young people at a local level. LiD was initiated by the Commission for
Educational Planning and Research Promotion of the German Federal State and
the Länder (Bund-Länder-Kommission für Bildungsplanung und Forschungsförderung,
BLK) (see BLK, 2003).
●Promotion of learning in the areas of mathematics and science: in the framework
programme SINUS (Steigerung der Effizienz des mathematisch-naturwisschenschaftli-
chen Unterrichts , increasing the efficiency of teaching mathematics and science)
the learning of students in mathematics and science is investigated. The findings
have formed the basis for implementing better learning environments at more
than one thousand schools. The SINUS pilot projects are funded by the BLK (see
IPN, 2004).
●Increase in the number of schools that offer all-day schooling: the programme
IZBB (Investitionsprogramm Zukunft Bildung und Betreuung, investment programme
for the future of education and care) was initiated by the Federal Ministry of
Education and is mainly funded by the federal government and aims to increase by
5000 the number of schools that offer all-day schooling. The Ministry argues that
all-day schooling will improve the integration of pupils with a migration back-
ground into German society and that it can also contribute to eradicating the social
inequalities of the school system. This argument is strengthened by international
comparisons which show that Germany is one of the few industrialised countries
that still follow the tradition of schooling mainly in the mornings only. In 2003,
only about 5% of schools in Germany offered schooling in the afternoon on a regu-
lar basis (BMBF, 2003, p. 3).

deciding on a framework programme that would provide funding for pilot

All these programmes and projects make explicit reference to the unsatisfactory PISA
results and can be regarded as a direct consequence of the reception of the study in
Germany. They all aim at improving educational opportunities and quality. The
projects and programmes described are all more or less directly funded by the Federal
and the Länder governments but give a variety of interest groups and local actors an
important role.
The reception of the PISA results has also led to strong reactions from the social
partners. While the opinions of German employers and trade unions on educational
and training matters are usually diametrically opposed, PISA triggered a joint state-
ment by the German Trade Union Association (Deutscher Gewerkschaftsbund) and the
Association of German Employer Unions (Bundesvereinigung Deutscher Arbeitgeberver-
bände). In an unprecedented agreement the two umbrella organisations identify two
reasons why educational reforms are an urgent matter: the overall low achievement
of pupils in German and the clear link between educational achievement and the
socio-economic background of students. In their joint statement, trade unions and
employers suggest ten reforms that aim at increased quality, equity and fostering of
talent in education. These reform proposals include better structures for language
learning of migrant children, redistribution of financial resources in favour of early
learning, more parental influence on schools, increased permeability between differ-
ent school types, higher and nationally-binding standards for teacher education,
increased autonomy of schools, and a stronger orientation of school education
towards the needs of the world of work (DGB & BDA, 2005).
It can be concluded that the reception of the PISA results has fundamentally
changed the educational discourse in Germany. Whereas the pre-PISA discourse was
characterised by a stalemate between the interests of the Federal and Länder govern-
ments (Rürup, 2005; Schwager, 2005) and diverging party political agendas, the
post-PISA context has for the moment brought about a new consensus regarding the
need for reform, which has in turn resulted in a—by German standards—frantic
reform agenda.
Curricular consequences
The introduction of national educational standards will substantially change the role
of curricula and the way they are developed and implemented. Traditionally, curric-
ula for German schools prescribed the aims and content of instruction in great detail.
This is due to the heritage of didactical theory in education ( bildungstheoretische
Didaktik) for which the selection of content was decisive. The advocates of this para-
digm (for instance Erich Weniger, Wolfgang Klafki, Herwig Blankertz) argued that a
sophisticated didactic analysis of content regarding its educational value is required
(cf. Jank & Meyer, 1994, chapter 5). This analysis resulted in the formulation of ideal-
istic and abstract educational aims and long lists of educational content, representing

the backbone of typical German school curricula in the 1960s and 1970s (Bohl,
2004). The influence of goal- and action-oriented approaches to curricular design in
the 1980s and 1990s did not change this situation substantially.
It can be argued that this kind of curriculum had little direct influence on what
went on in schools because teachers found it very difficult to implement them (cf.
Bung, 2004). These traditional curricula were too complex and written in too
abstract a language for them to be used by teachers directly. The influence of tradi-
tional curricula on teaching and learning was indirect in nature: firstly, they were the
basis of negotiations between the teachers of a subject in a given school about the
distribution of contents within school years and between school years. Secondly, text-
books were only accredited for a subject if they were in agreement with the current
curriculum for that subject. The impact of curriculum design on what went on in
terms of teaching and learning in German classrooms was filtered by the interpreta-
tion of (groups of) teachers and by reliance on textbooks.
The relative lack of influence of the curriculum was due to the fact that it did not
entail any form of output control. This can be attributed to the deep-rooted belief that
the assessment of learning should not form part of the curriculum and nor should the
method and style of teaching, or the materials and media used (Bung, 2004). In
Germany, all these elements are traditionally left to the professional judgment of
teachers, or to groups of teachers at a school. The state trusts that the prescription of
content in the curriculum, the definition of national standards for teacher training
and the accreditation of teacher qualifications suffice to ensure a sufficient quality of
education at schools (Klieme et al., 2003, pp. 91–94).
The introduction of national educational standards breaks with this tradition and
aims to build a new guiding principle for educational control which also entails a new
role for the curriculum.
Educational standards 
• build on the basic principles of the relevant school subject
• describe relevant subject-specific competences, including bodies of knowledge which
students should acquire by a specific point in time
• aim at systematic and interlinked learning and follow the principle of cumulative
competence formation
• describe expected achievements of the students in the framework of pre-determined
areas of competence
• are related to the core areas of the relevant subject and provide freedom for schools in
terms of conducting the teaching and learning processes
• aim at a medium level of competence
• are illustrated by examples of tasks and problems. (KMK, 2005, p. 6)
The standards have resulted in new conceptions concerning the future role of the
school curriculum; conceptions that vary substantially between the Länder. For
instance, Baden-Württemberg introduced ‘education plans’ (Bildungspläne), consist-
ing of the new national standards, core curricula (determining two-thirds of teaching
time) and school curricula that are developed by each school separately (determining
one-third of teaching time). North Rhine-Westphalia intends to replace existing

626 H. Ertl
curricula by less prescriptive ‘core curricula’ that are in line with the educational stan-
dards. In Rhineland-Palatinate the school law of 2003 even regulates that no more
new curricula should be developed and teachers should only use national standards
as a basis for planning their teaching (cf. Bung, 2004).
This approach seems to be overly radical since the national educational standards
are explicitly not designed to provide a sufficient basis for teaching. The Klieme
Report recommends that: 
the guiding function of educational standards and the orientation function of curricula
need to be combined [and argues that] educational standards in no way make curricula
superfluous since the standards do not—unlike curricula—provide guidance on the
content of teaching, on the structure of learning processes and on the timing of learning.
(Klieme et al., 2003, pp. 77f.)
However, Herrmann argues that the new national standards are very much structured
like old-fashioned curricula and therefore it seems hardly surprising that they are not
only mistaken for curricula, but also that they are unlikely to improve teaching and
learning: ‘The proposed “standards” are not standards but well-intentioned reformu-
lations of “curricula” (which do not deserve this term). The implementation of the
standards is in the lap of the gods’ (Herrmann, 2004, pp. 1f.).
On the basis of the reception of international models of regulation of teaching and
learning, German observers have identified a three-fold regulatory approach: auton-
omy of individual schools, general standards and regular external and internal assess-
ment (of students and schools) (cf. Bung, 2004). The concept of ‘educational plans’
as implemented in Baden Württemberg seems to follow this regulation model. The
model adopted in Rhineland-Palatinate, on the other hand, seems to overburden
schools as it is the responsibility of teachers and teams of teachers to fulfil the tasks
that were hitherto assumed by curriculum development commissions. In other words
teachers have to fill the vacuum caused by the absence of curricular guidance; a situ-
ation for which most teachers are not prepared in their training and for which schools
do not normally provide the necessary support structures.
Therefore, it can be expected that teachers will opt for a shortcut: rather than
developing teaching material themselves they will—once more—rely on recognised
textbooks as a basis of guidance in terms of contents, aims and structure of their
teaching. In this case it would depend on the quality of the textbooks used whether
the aims of the introduction of national standards can be achieved. Thus, it seems
likely that educational standards will not result in the far-reaching improvement of
teaching and learning at German schools, because they are implemented in a way
that allows or even pushes teachers to depend on textbooks as the main basis for
their teaching. In particular, it remains doubtful whether the orientation of teaching
and learning towards competences that students are supposed to develop will actu-
ally change teaching practice in Germany, which is often dominated by knowledge-
based and abstract learning. This doubt is derived from the lack of consensus
regarding the meaning of ‘competence’ in the academic discourse on education in
Germany.

Consequences for the academic discourse on education
Beyond the political and curricular consequences the PISA results had for education
in Germany, they also initiated a far-reaching debate about education as an academic
subject. This debate is not only concerned with the future direction research in
education should take, but also with the foundations of the discipline. For instance,
Bohl (2004) discusses the connection between empirical research of pedagogic prac-
tice (empirische Unterrichtsforschung) and general didactics (allgemeine Didaktik), which
is a long-established sub-discipline of education (Erziehungswissenschaft) in Germany.
In the tradition of general didactics, educational aims are deducted from the philo-
sophical discourse surrounding the nature of human beings and communities. In this
discourse the reality of schools and the demands students are facing after finishing
school play a secondary role. This is due to the substantial doubt general didactics has
regarding the measurability of teaching and learning processes (Lange, 2004). Educa-
tional research that follows this tradition focuses on the development of abstract,
normative educational aims, mainly using hermeneutic methods.
Empirical research of pedagogical practice, on the other hand, focuses on the
conditions in which teaching and learning processes actually take place and on the
real-life experience of teachers and learners. This kind of research aims to identify
interventions that improve teaching and learning, using empirical designs such as
quasi-experiments. This research necessarily differs from subject to subject, whereas
general didactics contends that valid educational aims go beyond the boundaries of
individual disciplines.
Put in simplified terms, general didactics broadly follows the paradigms and meth-
odologies of the humanities, whereas empirical research of pedagogical practice is
committed to the assumptions and methods of social sciences such as psychology and
sociology. Whereas general didactics is primarily concerned with how schools should
be, the main concern of empirical educational research is to find out how schools actu-
ally are (Lange, 2004).
Bohl (2004) concludes that PISA has shifted the balance of the academic discourse
on education in Germany clearly towards a more empirical and practice-focused
framework of research because the paradigmatic starting points and key concepts of
general didactics are, to a substantial degree, incompatible with the rationale of
empirical class-room research and with large-scale comparative studies such as PISA
or TIMSS. For instance, proponents of general didactics would argue that educa-
tional experiences constitute unique personal processes which cannot be compared in
a meaningful way. In this context, the discourse on PISA has contributed to the emer-
gence of empirical educational research from its ‘shadowy existence’ in Germany.
Bohl (2004, p. 419) also shows that literacy, one of the key concepts of the PISA
study, does not have a semantic equivalent in the traditional German discourse.
Therefore, it is difficult for researchers in general didactics to interpret the PISA find-
ings on literacy within the conceptual framework of their discipline (see also Kiper,
2003). This might explain why large-scale international comparisons had been less
influential on education as an academic discipline in Germany than in most other

countries before the PISA study was published. Bohl (2004) argues that it is not easy
to incorporate or interpret PISA in the conceptual framework of the tradition of
general didactics.
A further change in the academic discourse is the introduction of the concept of
competence as the overarching rationale of educational practice.
7 This amounts to
another re-orientation of the education system, and the discourse surrounding it, that
would not have happened—at least not at the same speed—without the PISA shock.
The new national educational standards adopted in Germany determine which
competences pupils should develop; the achievement of the education system is
measured in terms of its success in forming these individual competences. These
competences are oriented towards more general educational aims, but in the end
educational standards are focused on measurable competences (see Klieme et al.,
2003, p. 13). With the introduction of national standards, the concept of competence
has become a dominant issue in the educational discourse in Germany. The compar-
ison of different concepts of competence in a number of European countries has
demonstrated that there is no clearly defined and widely accepted notion of compe-
tence (Ertl & Sloane, 2005). As there is no tradition in Germany of formulating curric-
ula in terms of competences (i.e. regulating what the outcome of teaching and learning
for the learners should be), the reception of concepts of competence developed in
other national systems is a wide-spread phenomenon in Germany at the moment and
has led to misunderstandings and confusion in the educational discourse (Ertl, 2005).
Sloane and Dilger (2005) have described this situation as a ‘competence clash’—
not only do different notions of competence clash with each other, but the new educa-
tional ‘gospel’ of standards, competences and outcome-orientation is also at odds
with German traditions, such as content-based curricula and input-orientation (i.e.
regulating the structures, processes and conditions of teaching and learning). Sloane
and Dilger (2005) identify a number of detrimental consequences of this situation;
most importantly, the emphasis of the new national standards on the competence of
pupils pushes learning processes into the background. This is connected with the
narrow, behaviouristic notion of competence that can underlie educational stan-
dards—an objection that has been made numerous times in connection with the
concept of competence in NVQs (National Vocational Qualifications) in England
and Wales (see, for instance, Smithers, 1997).
These two issues, the revaluation of empirical educational research of pedagogical
practice and the debate surrounding the concept of competence in the new national
educational standards, demonstrate that PISA has changed the academic discourse
in Germany permanently. Bohl (2004, p. 414) describes this change as a ‘sharp
empirical turn’ which has initiated a restructuring process of education as an
academic discipline in Germany.
Conclusions: the (re)discovery of comparisons in education in Germany?
Although the development of national educational standards in Germany started in
the late 1990s, their rapid introduction must be regarded as a direct consequence of
=== PAGE 12 ===
The PISA study in Germany 629
the PISA results and the far-reaching ensuing public discussion. This represents an
example of the impact of a large-scale international comparative study in a national
context. However, the initial experience regarding the implementation of educational
standards makes it clear that the results of in-depth qualitative studies in other
national contexts also need to be taken into account. For instance, experience of the
introduction of standards in the USA, the UK and Sweden has shown that the devel-
opment of measures necessary for implementing standards and for developing new
innovative teaching concepts that carry them takes a long time (cf. Bung, 2004).
Van Ackeren (2003) has argued that the introduction of national educational stan-
dards can only be one element of an effective reform agenda. She refers to experiences
in other European countries which show that other aspects of reform are essential if
educational standards are to improve the quality of education. The examples van
Ackeren provides include England (where prescribed minimum standards are
combined with levels of competence), France (where compulsory learning objectives
determine what should be learned by each age cohort) and The Netherlands (where
newly developed national standards are combined with school-specific curricula
which allow for standardised and individual learning arrangements). However, in-
depth comparisons with countries that were successful in the PISA study are also
frequently cited in the public discourse. Comparisons with Finland and Sweden have
been prevalent (see for instance Herrmann, 2004).
The detailed comparison of the education systems in Canada, England, Finland,
France, The Netherlands and Sweden, commissioned by the Federal Ministry of
Education and Research in reaction to the PISA results, particularly looked at the way
in which educational standards are conceptualised, developed and applied interna-
tionally. The report resulting from the comparison concludes that ‘[…] educational
standards formulating uniform, obligatory expectations regarding the achievement of
pupils and assessment and quality assurance procedures based on these standards
play an important role in successful educational systems’ (BMBF, 2003, p. 267). This
conclusion is mirrored in the Klieme Report which provided the basis for the devel-
opment of national educational standards in Germany. Therefore, it can be argued
that PISA has initiated further comparative research which informed policy-making
processes in the German context.
These references and examples can be regarded as indications for the increasing
importance in Germany of comparisons with other countries after PISA. After
decades of introspective reflection on educational policy and practice, the discourse
on education in Germany seems to look beyond its own borders again. The PISA
shock has had a positive influence in this context, among others.
However, the impact of the PISA 2000 results in Germany can partly be explained
by the fact that Germany had either not been part of many of the other large-scale
comparisons in education since the 1960s (TIMSS was the only notable exception
here), or that politicians and the media ignored the results (Tillmann, 2004).
Comparisons of educational achievements had no previous tradition in Germany.
Educational and political reactions and their impact in the aftermath of the publica-
tion of the results of PISA were therefore unique (in no other country was the study

so widely discussed as in Germany, see Gruber, 2006) and surprising to most observ-
ers.8 Since PISA 2000, international comparisons in education have been high on
the agenda of educational ministries in Germany. Examples of this re-orientation are
the German participation in PIRLS (Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study),
9 the intra-German comparisons of educational achievement between the 16
German Länder (Baumert, et al., 2002;  Deutsches PISA-Konsortium, 2003b), and a
detailed study of school systems in selected PISA countries (see BMBF, 2003;
OECD, 2004). All of these studies have been more or less directly funded by the
German Federal Ministry of Education and Research and the Länder ministries and
have found their way into the discourse on education (see, for instance, Klieme,
2003; Klein, 2005b).
The Federal Ministry’s post-PISA agenda seems to be firmly focused on raising
national educational standards by pursuing measures that will improve Germany’s
low ranking in the PISA league table. It places less emphasis on the solution of the
other major problem identified by PISA, the strong connection between the socio-
economic background of students and their educational achievement. Teachers’
associations have criticised this and argue for a more long-term strategy for eradicat-
ing inequalities in the German education system. For instance, the Trade Union for
Education (GEW) argues for measures that would: 
• increase the level of competence of weaker students in particular,
• increase the share of students that achieve the Realschule certificate or the Abitur, with-
out lowering standards,
• narrow the gap between weak and strong students and between weak and strong
schools. (GEW, 2005)
Teachers’ associations have argued that these aims cannot be achieved if the reform
agenda is purely based on improvements of teaching and learning quality, which is
the ultimate aim of the introduction of national standards. In their view, educational
inequalities and the resulting high share of underachieving students in German
schools are a direct consequence of the selective school system (Stange, 2004).
Reforms that ignore the need to rethink the early selection of students in the tripartite
structure of secondary schooling will succeed in narrowing the gap between high
achieving and low achieving students. On the contrary, the introduction of national
standards will intensify selection and increase assessment pressure even in primary
education (Herrmann, 2004, p. 2). Parents’ earnings and education levels are deter-
mining factors for the allocation of students to the different types of secondary schools
in Germany. Teachers’ associations therefore argue for a ‘system change’ by develop-
ing integrative schools for students of all backgrounds and abilities (GEW, 2005).
However, despite the fact that many of the countries that performed well on the
PISA study have a comprehensive school system, the renewed interest in comparative
studies in education has not resulted in a critical assessment of selective secondary
schooling in Germany; neither by politicians nor by many educational researchers
(see Klieme, 2003). The frequent criticism of international observers, such as the
German PISA-coordinator Andreas Schleicher, regarding the selective German
