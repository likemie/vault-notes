ABSTRACT
This second-order meta-analysis examined 52 first-order creativity meta-analyses (164 effect sizes) 
which met inclusion and exclusion criteria. These meta-analyses provided data from 2609 studies 
(1,248,416 research participants) and 16,153 primary effect sizes. The original meta-analyses all 
examined creativity, but each had a specific focus. Even with that large range of foci, certain 
questions could be addressed with a second-order meta-analysis. More specifically, 
a comprehensive statistical analysis provided an effect size for all previous meta-analyses (a) 
where creativity had been used as a predictor and (b) where creativity was the outcome. This 
moderator was statistically significant, F(1, 16.3) = 9.43, p = .007. The average second-order effect 
size was .12 when creativity was the criterion and .29 when it was a predictor. Four categories were 
created for the type of creativity variable used in first-order meta-analyses: creative outcomes, 
creative process, divergent thinking, and overall creativity. In the first model, where creative 
process and divergent thinking were analyzed separately, creative process had an effect size of 
.27, while divergent thinking had an effect size of .14. Different correlates of creativity were also 
analyzed. Of these, interventions and educational programs yielded the largest effect size ( r = .20). 
Implications and limitations are discussed.
Creativity has long been recognized as enormously valu -
able for individuals. It is associated with health, self- 
expression, openness to experience, problem solving, 
and a slew of other adaptive tendencies and traits. 
Creativity is also an enormously valuable commodity 
for business and society (Florida & Gabe, 2024 ; Runco 
et al., 2020 ). Admittedly, the benefits of creativity are 
not all adaptive and benevolent. Creativity is also some -
times associated with malevolence and disturbance 
(Cropley, 1997 ; Runco and Richards, 1997 ).
Great headway has been made in the creativity 
research, much of it resulting from the new methods 
and technologies that have been brought to bear on the 
topic. A great deal has, for example, been learned from 
meta-analyses (Acar et al., 2020 ). Meta-analyses contri -
bute significantly to our understanding of creativity in 
part because they represent large samples. The second- 
order meta-analysis of da Costa et al. ( 2015 ), for exam -
ple, summarized data from over 15,000 research parti -
cipants and from just over 100 studies. The large 
samples make meta analyses highly reliable and support 
the generalizability of the findings (Acar et al., 2020 ). da 
Costa et al. ( 2015 ) analyzed effect sizes from seven meta- 
analyses. They included research on both creativity and 
innovation. Nine years later, we were able to locate over 
70 meta-analyses, each of which focused on creativity. 
Our search and inclusion/exclusion procedures are 
described in detail below.
da Costa et al. ( 2015 ) only asked about personal 
correlates of creativity (e.g., intelligence, motivation, 
and emotion). The second-order meta-analysis reported 
here took a broader view. Indeed, the meta-analyses we 
analyzed were categorized, first, in terms of creativity 
being treated as a predictor or an outcome. One of the 
moderators compared creativity when it was defined in 
the earlier research as a criterion vs creativity when it 
was defined as a predictor. This distinction fits with 
creativity research and theory, and in particular the 
psychometric conceptualization of criteria (e.g., as in 
criterion-related validity) and predictors (e.g., as in pre -
dictive validity). The distinction reflects the fact that 
sometimes creativity is defined as an outcome or result 
and used in analyses as the criterion, with other vari -
ables used as predictors of the creativity criterion or 
criteria. Other times the research has used creativity as 
a predictor and there are other outcomes (e.g., health or 
problem solving or academic success) other than crea -
tivity. This dichotomy parallels the distinction between 
independent variables in research and dependent vari -
ables. The labels predictor and outcome were used 
because they do not imply causality. The previous meta- 
analyses were also categorized in terms of the study

correlational. Next were categories for the types of crea -

for creative outcomes (e.g., achievements, honors, and 
awards), creative processes (e.g., flexibility and problem

meta-analyses defined moderators for the correlates of

background variables (e.g., birth order, family size, sex, 
race diversity), cognitive correlates (e.g., flexibility, 
time-on-task, problem finding), extracognitive corre -
lates (e.g., psychoticism, mood, stress), interventions 
and education (e.g., purdue creative problem solving, 
brainstorming), and finally, immediate influences (e.g., 
explicit instructions and stress). The cognitive category 
represents thinking or intellectual processes. The extra -
cognitive correlates represent things which research has 
suggested may influence the creative process but do not 
represent well-recognized cognitive (or intellectual) 
processes. Mood, for example, was coded as extracogni -
tive because it is affective and yet sometimes does influ -
ence creative thinking (Kaufmann & Vosburg, 2002 ).
These categorizations were chosen for several rea -
sons. First, they were chosen a priori and selected 
because they fit with creativity theory and research. 
The predictor vs criterion category, for example, fits 
with psychometric research on creativity (Runco, Park 
& Acar, 2020 ). The categorizations also represent a fairly 
optimal number of statistical tests, relative to the sample 
size. The predictor vs criterion categorization maxi -
mized power in that it could be applied to all previous 
meta analyses (rather than just a subsample) and it fits 
with general concerns about creativity as it may influ -
ence various aspects of life (and is in that sense 
a predictor) and creativity as it may be influenced by 
various factors (and can therefore be supported). It was 
necessary for the moderator analyses to have a sufficient 
number of effect sizes per each moderator level, which 
meant avoiding narrow categories and combined them 
when conceptually appropriate.
These categorizations of the previous meta-analyses 
– or, more precisely, the effect sizes previously 
reported – allowed second-order meta-analyses to 
determine what effect sizes have been reported across 
studies, where creativity was treated as a predictor or 
treated as an outcome, and, within each of those two 
broad categories, how the different types of studies 
(comparative and correlational), different creativity 
variables (e.g., divergent thinking), and different corre -
lates (e.g., cognitive, extracognitive, and background) 
moderated these effect sizes. The current second-order 
meta-analysis should provide a broad picture of where 
the field of creative studies stands, where there has been 
clear progress, and where there is more work to do.
Second-order meta-analyses (SOMA) have several 
unique advantages over literature reviews and other 
approaches used to synthesize research. First, SOMA 
are empirical rather than theoretical. Relatedly, unlike 
literature reviews, in SOMA reliability is calculated and 
reported. Then there is the fact that, instead of some 
theoretical conclusions offered by a literature review, 
SOMA synthesize previous results into an overall esti -
mate (or estimates), with known reliability. As Sala et al. 
( 2019 ) wrote, “Theory building in science requires repli -
cation and integration of findings regarding a particular 
research question. Second-order meta-analysis (i.e., 
a meta-analysis of meta-analyses) offers a powerful 
tool for achieving this aim.” Put differently, SOMA 
offers highly robust analyses. They utilize large samples 
(the present sample includes 1,248,416 individuals), 
often from quite varied primary meta-analyses, and 
thus provide results with justified generalizability. The 
generalizability is supported by the larger samples but 
also by the fact that no one methodology is used. 
Diverse methodologies (e.g., tests, measures, and con -
trols) had been used in the original meta-analyses, and 
the results of the SOMA are thus not indicative of any 
one methodology. Another key advantage of SOMA is 
that they estimate the proportion of the variance in 
mean meta-analytic effect sizes across multiple first- 
order meta-analyses that is attributable to second- 
order sampling error and use this information to 
improve the accuracy of the overall estimation for each 
first order meta-analytic estimates (Schmidt & Oh,  
2013 ). In short, they are highly accurate.
A SOMA of the creativity research can address 
broadly important questions that could not be 
addressed with any other analytic strategy. Over 70 
meta-analyses had in some way previously investigat -
ing the impact of creativity, and the SOMA reported 
here therefore offers an empirical answer to the ques -
tion, “does creativity matter?” Data used to answer 
this question were found in the previous meta- 
analyses that used creativity as an independent vari -
able, an influence, or a predictor. The answer to “does 
creativity matter” would be affirmative if it was in fact 
predictive of important outcomes (e.g., health, pro -
blem solving, and creative achievement), as indicated 
by an overall effect size. Another subset of primary 
meta-analyses included in the present data in some 
way investigated the things that influence creativity. 
Here, creativity was an outcome or the dependent 
variable. The SOMA reported here could therefore 
offer an empirical answer to the question, “what can

be done about creativity?” The degree to which is 
creativity influenced by experiences, education, treat -
ments and training, and group and individual char -
acteristics indicates whether or not it does react to 
varied influences. In a sense, this speaks to the ques -
tion, can anything can be done about creativity? That 
is a broad question, but in addition, SOMA can ana -
lyze moderators and thereby offer specific estimates of 
the degree to which various sources of heterogeneity 
(moderators) have been related to creativity in the 
primary meta-analyses. Admittedly, not all sources of 
heterogeneity can be empirical examined. The present 
analyses were limited by the available data. Theory 
might have suggested that one particular sources of 
heterogeneity should be analyzed (e.g., historical eras), 
but if there are too few extant primary meta-analyses 
(and effect sizes) about it, meta-analyses are not pos -
sible. Given the various advantages of SOMA, it is no 
wonder that they have been used to synthesize 
research on cognitive training (Sala et al., 2019 ), the 
impact of technology on learning (Tamim et al., 2011 ) 
the impact of relying on college students as research 
participants (Peterson, 2001 ), the impact of online 
and distance learning (Martin et al., 2022 ), and 
a large number of other research questions.
Abundant theoretical justification could be cited for 
each of these variables, correlates, and influences. Each 
of them was justified by theory in the original meta- 
analyses. Given that there were over 70 of these con -
tributing effect sizes to the present effort, the specific 
theories are too numerous to cite here. That being said, 
the idea that background variables may influence crea -
tivity follows from the theory that humans have creative 
potentials which may be fulfilled (e.g., Runco, 2024 ). 
The idea that there are cognitive correlates of creativity 
follows from numerous theories which point to diver -
gent thinking, insight, problem solving, and so on (e.g., 
Roberts et al., 2021 ). The idea that there are extracogni -
tive correlates follows from theories of personality (e.g., 
Eysenck, 2003 ), and mood and affect (Kaufmann & 
Vosburg, 2002 ; Hirt, 1999 ). The idea that interventions 
and education may influence creativity follows from 
theories of instruction, teaching, and training (e.g., 
Beghetto & van Geffen, 2024 ). Finally, the idea that 
there are important immediate influences on creativity 
(e.g., task instructions and stress) follows from theories 
of creativity an expression of current functioning 
(Rhodes, 1997 ). These are just examples and additional 
theoretical detail can be found in the original meta- 
analyses. The most important theory justifying the cur -
rent analysis is no doubt that which explains the mean -
ing and value of second-order meta-analyses (Schmidt 
& Oh, 2013 ).
Method
Search strategy
A multimodal search strategy was used to identify rele -
vant first-order meta-analyses on creativity. First, we 
conducted a multi-database search using EBSCOhost 
for peer-reviewed literature across the following five 
databases: ERIC, APA PsycINFO, APA PsycArticles, 
Education Research Complete, and Academic Search 
Complete. Additionally, we searched ProQuest 
Dissertations and Theses for unpublished meta- 
analyses. The keyword creativity and its associated com -
ponents and variations (e.g., divergent thinking, origin -
ality, and fluency) along with specific meta-analysis 
terms (e.g., meta-analysis and systematic review) were 
applied to article and dissertation titles and abstracts 
using Boolean operators and truncation. Second, we 
manually searched key creativity research journals, 
including Creativity Research Journal , The Journal of 
Creative Behavior , Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity 
and the Arts , and Thinking Skills and Creativity . Third, 
a document title search was performed in Google 
Scholar for additional gray literature. Finally, we 
reached out to creativity scholars to inquire about addi -
tional meta-analysis studies, including those not yet 
published. Our search identified 4228 studies which 
were screened at the title and abstract levels; 78 were 
full text assessed for eligibility, with 52 included in 
this second-order meta-analysis (see Figure 1 ).
Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Our database search was limited to first-order meta- 
analyses published in English between January 1, 1980, 
and August 1, 2023. To be included, studies had to 
report at least one meta-analytic effect size related to 
creativity or its components. Systematic reviews without 
meta-analytic methods were excluded (e.g., Bicer et al.,  
2021 ). Additionally, studies involving brain imaging 
that used Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE) meta- 
analysis of fMRI data were excluded, as they did not 
provide meta-analytic effect sizes on the relationships 
between creativity and other variables (e.g., Boccia et al.,  
2015 ).

A coding spreadsheet was developed to systematically 
record the following key features from each first-order 
meta-analysis: the authors, publication year, publication 
status (published vs. unpublished), the year span of the 
included empirical studies, the meta-analytic model 
employed (fixed effects and random effects), the meta-

The methodological quality of the included first- 
order meta-analyses was assessed using the AMSTAR 
measurement tool (Shea et al., 2017 ). The 12 study 
quality criteria used in the assessment are detailed in 
Table 1 . Each criterion was scored dichotomously, and 
an overall quality score for each meta-analysis was cal -
culated by averaging the scores across all criteria. Two 
independent coders evaluated the study quality variable. 
One coder assessed all 61 studies, while another inde -
pendently assessed 52 of them. The inter-rater agree -
ment was 97% for the 636 individual study quality 
variables. All discrepancies were resolved through con -
sensus during multiple review meetings involving both 
coders and an additional author. A dichotomous mod -
erator that indicated whether or not a first-order meta- 
analysis was considered high quality was then created, 
with the threshold for the average quality score set at 
0.75. In other words, a first-order meta-analysis should 
meet at least nine out of 12 criteria to be considered high 
quality.
Data analysis
Pearson correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r ) was used as 
the measure of relationship between creativity and its 
correlates. In cases where the first-order meta-analyses 
presented Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients 
(Spearman’s ρ ), we converted them to Pearson’s 
r using Equation 1 (Rupinski & Dunlap, 1996 ): 
If standardized mean differences (Cohen’s d or 
Hedges’ g ) were reported in the first-order meta- 
analyses, they were transformed to Pearson’s r using 
formulas in Borenstein et al. ( 2009 ).
Due to the non-normality of Pearson’s r , each r was 
transformed to Fisher’s z to better approximate the 
normal distribution. The z -transformed correlation 
coefficients were then used in the subsequent meta- 
analyses. These meta-analytic estimates were eventually 
converted back to Pearson’s r to aid interpretation.
All statistical analyses were performed in R, using the 
metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010 ) and clubSandwich 
(Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2017 ) packages.
The primary analysis employed the combination of 
a multilevel meta-analytical model with robust variance 
estimation (RVE; Hedges et al., 2010 ). Initially, 
a working model was used to approximate the depen -
dency structure of the effect size data, enabling the 
calculation of inverse-variance weights. More specifi -
cally, the CHE model was used (Tipton & Pustejovsky,  
2022 ). It assumes correlations between effect sizes 
within the same first-order meta-analysis due to possi -
ble measurements on shared participants, while 
acknowledging potential differences between these 
effect sizes. For the analyses, we assumed that effect 
sizes within the same first-order meta-analysis were 
correlated with r = 0.8. To mitigate misspecification 
risks related to the working model and the assumed 
correlation, standard errors were estimated and RVE 
used for hypothesis testing. This combined approach 
ensured the validity of hypothesis testing and increased 
the precision of the estimates.
The average effect size was estimated using an inter -
cept-only meta-analytic model. For moderator analysis, 
all potential moderators (i.e., creativity as a criterion or 
predictor, study design, the types of creativity, the types 
of correlates of creativity, and the indicator of high- 
quality meta-analysis) were included in the model. 
Moderator effects were evaluated using the F test with 
the Approximate Hotelling’s T -squared approach 
(Tipton & Pustejovsky, 2015 ). Since all moderators 
were discrete variables, we also presented the effect 
estimate at each level of each moderator (representing 
each subgroup), controlling for the remaining modera -
tors by centering to their mean levels. Confidence inter -
vals and prediction intervals were also presented along 
with the subgroup effect estimates.
Although no studies have specifically examined 
methods for publication bias in second-order meta- 
analyses, the methods used to address publication bias 
in first-order meta-analyses were presumed to be simi -
larly effective. A modified Egger’s regression test (Sterne 
& Egger, 2006 ) was applied within the multilevel meta- 
analytic model with RVE, to both detect and adjust for 
any small-study effects, which potentially indicated 
publication bias. If the test result was significant, then 
the bias-corrected estimates would be presented. The 
primary advantage of the regression-based adjustment 
is that it can be seamlessly integrated into the multilevel 
meta-analytic framework with RVE. By contrast, alter -
native approaches, such as the trim-and-fill method 
(Duval & Tweedie, 2000 ), Fail-Safe N test (Orwin,  
1983 ), and selection models (Vevea & Hedges, 1995 ) 
have not yet been thoroughly evaluated in the context 
of multilevel meta-analysis.

Examined moderators included creativity as 
a criterion or predictor, study design, the types of crea -
tivity, the types of correlates of creativity, and the indi -
cator of high-quality first-order meta-analysis. 
Correlates of creativity represented (a) cognitive corre -
lates (intelligence, imagination, cognitive load, incuba -

curiosity, schizotypy, psychoticism; e.g., Acar & 
Runco, 2012 ; Byron et al., 2010 ), (d) interventions and 
education (programs such as Osborne’s Creative 
Problem Solving or the Purdue Creative Thinking 
Program, explicit instructions given with DT tests; e.g., 
DeRosa et al., 2007 ; Itsarangkul et al., 2022 ), and (e) 
immediate influences (team composition or diversity, 
climate, role expectations; e.g., Hammond et al., 2011 ; 
Hunter et al., 2007 ). The modality or domain of crea -
tivity was not assessed because there were not enough 
first-order effect sizes across their levels. Publication 
status as a moderator also could not be assessed, as 
nearly all of the included meta-analyses were published.

Discussion
Creative studies have grown a great deal in the past 
several decades, so much so that not only have there 
been hundreds of original empirical studies, but also 
dozens of meta-analyses. The present investigation 
summarized what had been reported in the previous 
meta-analyses. Fifty-two first-order meta-analyses satis -
fied the inclusion criteria and were analyzed.
Across meta-analyses where creativity was used as 
a predictor, the average effect size was .29. Across 
those where creativity was instead the criterion (an out -
come), the average effect size was .12. This might sug -
gest that creativity as a predictor is easier to 
operationalize than creativity as an outcome. That may 
come as a surprise, given that creativity as an outcome 
could include actual creative activities and accomplish -
ments – things which can simply be counted. Of some 
relevance on this point is that the meta-analytic study 
design examined as a moderator and did not seem to 
matter. The quality of the first-order meta-analysis was 
also not important. Each of these nonsignificant mod -
erators suggests some generality of the present results. If 
study design had been statistically significant, for exam -
ple, it would suggest that the effect sizes for creativity as 
a criterion or as a predictor only applied some of the 
time (e.g., in studies that used certain designs). If the 
quality of the meta-analysis had been statistically sig -
nificant, it would imply that the effect sizes in the pre -
sent results only applied some of the time (e.g., only in 
high-quality meta-analyses). Also relevant is that there 
was a statistically significant amount of heterogeneity, 
which implies that the range in the previous meta- 
analyses was substantial, even though the summarized 
effect size for creativity as a predictor was large, at .29. 
Sometimes creativity was a reasonable predictor, but 
sometimes it was not. The significant heterogeneity is 
a reminder that in some of the previous meta-analyses, 
creativity as a predictor was more (or less) strongly 
associated with criteria than the value of .29 might 
suggest.
Standing back, these results might imply that there is 
a stronger argument for the claim that creativity is 
important (because of what it can predict) than the 
claim that creativity can accurately be predicted. It 
may, however, be best to postpone that conclusion. 
After all, the variables and measures used in the research 
where creativity was a predictor are not the same as 
those used in the research where creativity was 
a criterion, so it is not a fair comparison. Also, keep in 
mind that although the analyses reported above exam -
ined 52 meta-analyses, many influences and outcomes 
were excluded because they have not yet been examined 
(or not yet justified a meta-analysis). Conclusions about 
influences on creativity are similarly limited because not 
all possible influences on creativity have been studied. 
Conclusions about predictions from creativity must be 
qualified by the fact that there was a finite number of 
criteria used in the previous meta-analyses. This is in 
addition to the well-known criterion problem which has 
long plagued the creativity research (Paek & Runco,  
2018 ; Shapiro, 1970 ).
The type of creativity targeted in the previous meta- 
analyses was only moderately important. For studies 
focusing on divergent thinking, the average effect size 
was .14. When divergent thinking was included with 
other measures of the creative process, the effect size 
was .27. This is no surprise, given that divergent think -
ing is only one component of the creative process 
(Basadur et al., 2000 ; Cropley, 2006 , Runco & Chand,  
1995 ; Zeng et al., 2011 ). Thus, it makes sense that more 
information is provided when all process variables are 
examined rather than just one, even if it is divergent 
thinking. Incidentally, the largest effect size reported by 
da Costa et al. ( 2015 ) in their second-order meta- 
analysis, which included both creativity and innovation 
but focused more on personality than the 
present second-order analysis, was .31. This is very 
close to the largest effect size uncovered in our own 
results.
Analyses of the correlates of creativity as a moderator 
indicated that interventions and educational efforts had 
a modest association with creativity; the second-order 
meta-analytic Pearson correlation coefficient was .20. 
There was not much difference between the cognitive 
and extracognitive correlates of creativity, which were 
.12 and .14. This is important because it reinforces the 
idea that creativity cannot be well understood by only 
taking cognitive factors into account. Motivation, affect, 
attitudes, mood, and so on should also be recognized 
(Kaufmann & Vosburg, 2002 ). Background variables 
(birth order, family size, sex, race diversity) were non -
significant in the current analyses, with a confidence 
interval that included zero. This was somewhat surpris -
ing, given the logic for including culture, sex, and other 
demographics (cf Csikszentmihalyi, 1988 ; Rudowicz,  
2003 ). However, as is the case with all of the results

reported here, the statistics are averages that are not 
necessarily indicative of what might be found in any 
single sample. The prediction interval for background 
variables was −.38 to .49, indicating that the true effect 
size could range from a negative correlation to a positive 
correlation.
This brings us to limitations of the current research 
and future directions. One limitation was that the only 
data that could be used in the SOMA were those pro -
duced in the earlier meta-analyses. This meant that 
some of the results suggest important questions in 
need of research. Future research might follow up on 
some of the particular findings reported here. One 
obvious example is the result from the present SOMA 
that creativity as a predictor had a larger effect size 
than creativity as an outcome. Could that be 
a reflection of the operationalization of each? Is it 
a reflection of the creative process, or a measurement 
artifact? The present data do not provide an answer but 
perhaps future research can be designed along these 
lines. Similarly, the present results indicated that crea -
tive processes had a larger effect size than divergent 
thinking. Future research might follow up on that as 
well in an attempt to explain the discrepancy. In addi -
tion to research like this, additional second-order 
meta-analyses will be warranted as more and more 
primary meta-analyses on creativity are published. 
These will be interesting, especially given what was 
said above about the limitations of the present 
research. The current results are not indicative of all 
measures (predictors and criteria) that could be used 
when doing research on creativity. This research was 
also limited in that only certain a priori categories were 
used as moderators and correlates. Those analyzed 
(e.g., background variables, cognitive vs. meta- 
cognitive processes, and educational interventions) fit 
well with current theory and with the previous meta- 
analyses, but they were chosen a priori, and other 
categories could have been hypothesized. Future 
research might be able to use as of yet unreported 
meta-analyses on cultural differences or domain differ -
ences and other important issues. Undoubtedly, other 
categories will be used in future research when 
another second-order meta-analysis is justified by 
further advancements in the field.
