A B S T R A C T
As university involvement in technology transfer and entrepreneurship has increased, concerns over the patenting
and licensing of scientiﬁc discoveries have grown. This paper examines the eﬀect that the licensing of academic
patents has on journal citations to academic publications covering the same scientiﬁc research. We analyze data on
invention disclosures, patents, and licenses from the University of California, a leading U.S. academic patenter and
licensor, between 1997 and 2007. We also develop a novel “inventor-based” maximum-likelihood matching
technique to automate and generalize Murray's (2002) “patent-paper pairs” methodology. We use this metho-
dology to identify the scientiﬁc publications associated with University of California patents and licenses.
Based on a “diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences” analysis, we ﬁnd that within our sample of patented academic dis-
coveries, citations to licensed patent-linked publications are higher in the three years after the license, although
this diﬀerence is not statistically signiﬁcant. We then disaggregate our sample into (a) patented discoveries that
are likely to be used as “research tools” by other researchers (based on the presence of material transfer
agreements (MTAs) that cover them) and (b) patented discoveries not covered by MTAs. Citations to publications
linked to licensed patents in the latter subset (not covered by MTAs) are higher for publications linked to
licensed patents, and this diﬀerence is statistically signiﬁcant. In contrast, licensing of patented discoveries that
are also research tools is associated with a reduction in citations to papers linked to these research advances,
raising the possibility that licensing may restrict the ﬂow of inputs to “follow-on” scientiﬁc research.
1. Introduction
Growth in patenting of academic research advances in U.S. and
other universities within the OECD has triggered considerable debate
since at least 1980, the year of passage of the U.S. Bayh-Dole Act.
Supporters contend that patenting and licensing of university inven-
tions can speed the transfer of scientiﬁc discoveries to the private
sector, promoting the commercialization of such advances. On the other
hand, critics predict a collision between the norms of science and the
norms of commerce, arguing that the exclusionary eﬀects of patents will
slow the progress of science. Despite the importance of this question,
there has been relatively little empirical work on the extent to which
the patenting of academic research results aﬀects scientiﬁc research
progress. The modest volume of this work (e.g., Fehder et al., 2014;
Murray and Stern, 2007) reﬂects the challenge of obtaining data that
can be used to examine any such eﬀects.

2. Research and intellectual property in academia
Universities have long been important performers of research, par-
ticularly basic research. U.S. universities have expanded their perfor-
mance of basic research since the 1970s, performing almost 49% of U.S.
basic research in 1979 (National Science Foundation, 2012, Table 4–4)
and almost 59% in 2006 (National Science Board, 2016, Appendix
Tables 4–7, pp. 4-150–4-156). Expansion in their role in basic research
has coincided with increased patenting and licensing of U.S. university
discoveries, particularly in biotechnology.
The roles of universities as sources of both basic knowledge and
potentially valuable ingredients for commercial innovation raises the
possibility of conﬂict between these roles, with detrimental con-
sequences for the advance of scientiﬁc research. Do patents and licenses
restrict access to such knowledge? Or is the existence of a patent and/or
a license for that patent a signal of the quality or commercial potential
of scientiﬁc work that leads to greater exploration of the area?
Our examination of the eﬀects of patenting and licensing on scien-
tiﬁc communication focuses on “patent-linked publications” (Murray,
2002). These are discoveries that are published in scientiﬁc journals
and become the subject of successful patent applications.
2.1. The eﬀects of intellectual property protection and licensing on scientiﬁc
research and communication
The growth of academic patenting has been the subject of a large
literature and considerable debate over its eﬀects on the scientiﬁc re-
search enterprise (e.g., Mowery et al., 2004). Heller and Eisenberg
(1998) argue that expanded patenting of academic research results may
result in fragmented and overlapping property rights covering biome-
dical research, limiting the ability of scientists to access patented and
licensed research outputs for follow-on research. Other scholars raising
concerns over the expanded assertion of property rights in science in-
clude Nelson (2004) and David (2004). Empirical research seeking to
assess the eﬀects of patenting on scientiﬁc communication has ex-
amined the eﬀects of patenting on biomedical researchers’ willingness
to share information on their work (Blumenthal et al., 1997; Campbell
et al., 2002). More recent research has analyzed the eﬀects of patenting
biomedical discoveries that are also disclosed in scientiﬁc papers. Some
of this work ﬁnds that the issuance of a patent resulted in modest but
signiﬁcant declines in citations to the research papers related to the
patent (Murray and Stern, 2007; Sampat, 2005).1 Similarly, Williams
(2013) ﬁnds that patenting of genes by the private ﬁrm Celera reduced
citations to related scientiﬁc research. Other work, however, argues
that academic researchers rarely if ever search to determine whether a
prospective research project or experiment will infringe on patents
(Lei et al., 2009; Walsh et al., 2007), raising questions about the me-
chanism behind any observed citation decreases.
As the previous paragraph indicates, the majority of prior empirical
research on the eﬀects on scientiﬁc communication of academic pa-
tenting and licensing of discoveries has focused mainly on patenting of
academic research results. The eﬀects of university licensing of these
patents have received much less attention from scholars.
Why and how might licenses for a speciﬁc research advance aﬀect
the behavior of other researchers not involved as authors or patent
holders? Sampat and Ziedonis (2004) examined patent citations to
Columbia University and University of California patents that were li-
censed and found that citations were higher for licensed than for un-
licensed patents.2 These scholars interpreted this pattern of increased
patent citations as reﬂecting growth in patenting (and potentially, re-
search) activity in the technological area(s) surrounding the licensed
patents. More recently, Drivas et al. (2017) found that non-licensees
increased their citations to patents exclusively licensed (based on re-
strictions covering either geographic area or ﬁeld of use) by the Uni-
versity of California after the licenses were executed. Similar to Sampat
and Ziedonis (2004), Drivas et al. (2017) regard the increase in non-
licensee patent citations as a reaction to the potential commercial value
signaled by the negotiation of the license.3
A similar signaling eﬀect associated with the execution of a license
could increase citations to publications linked to a patented and li-
censed discovery. In such a case, the issue of a license would “de-
monstrate” that a particular area of research has scientiﬁc value,
leading other investigators to pursue work in related ﬁelds. It is also
possible (see Larsen, 2011) that contemporary academic researchers
may choose research areas partly based on their potential commercial
value, and therefore might respond positively to a “signal” that a given
area of research has attracted the attention of industrial licensees. Re-
gardless of whether any such signal operates through perceptions
among researchers of scientiﬁc or commercial potential, this argument
predicts an increase in citations to publications linked to patented
disclosures following the negotiation of the license.
Equally plausible arguments, however, suggest a chilling eﬀect of
licensing on scientiﬁc communication. Reactions by university tech-
nology licensing oﬃces and/or their licensees to any evidence of patent
infringement (even for research purposes, inasmuch as the legal status
of any “research exemption” from such infringement suits remains
unclear) could be swifter and stronger in the case of patents that are
licensed. And licenses may include provisions for reach-through roy-
alties and limitations on the management of intellectual property re-
sulting from follow-on research. Moreover, the negotiation of a license
may take considerable time, delaying other researchers’ access to the
materials or tools embodied in the disclosure.
We are thus agnostic on the likely direction of any eﬀect of licenses
on scientiﬁc communication associated with publications linked to li-
censed academic patents. Indeed, both eﬀects may be present for papers
in various ﬁelds of research, and our ﬁndings could shed light on the
magnitude of any oﬀsetting eﬀects.

2.2. The importance of access to research inputs
Patents and licenses increase the “excludability” of intellectual
property, exposing other researchers to potential legal liability in the
event that they utilize or exploit the intellectual property protected by
patents issued to others for their own research or commercial use. A
diﬀerent form of excludability, highlighted by Walsh et al. (2007),
occurs when one researcher denies physical access to materials or other
research results that are inputs to the experiments of another re-
searcher. The survey results of Walsh et al. (2007) indicate that such
denials can impose signiﬁcant costs and delays on the scientiﬁc work of
other researchers, costs and delays that according to these authors,
exceed those associated with patents.
One class of research inputs is widely (and imprecisely) identiﬁed as
“research tools.” For example, the NIH Working Group on Research
Tools (1998) deﬁnes them as “the full range of resources that scientists
use in the laboratory … the term may thus include cell lines, mono-
clonal antibodies, reagents, animal models, growth factors, combina-
torial chemistry libraries, drugs and drug targets, clones and cloning
tools (such as PCR), methods, laboratory equipment and machines,
databases and computer software.”4
As we noted above, Walsh et al. (2007) argue that denials of access
to such research tools are more likely when the erstwhile supplier of
them is engaged in “business activity,” such as licensing of the inven-
tion disclosure associated with the tools.5 Interviews with researchers
conducted for this study support this view, with one academic scientist
involved in a start-up ﬁrm saying “if another company asked to use our
[materials] for [the same purpose as our company uses them] we would
say ‘no’ ”.6
Even when permission is granted, however, researchers gaining
access to research tools that are associated with licensed disclosures
may encounter other diﬃculties, as highlighted by the celebrated case
of the Oncomouse (Murray, 2010):
“In 1984, scientists at Harvard University carefully engineered a
new mouse to have a predisposition to cancer, the Oncomouse
…The Harvard researchers …patented their creation and subse-
quently licensed this patent to DuPont …
[Dupont] set a high price per mouse …placed restrictions on
breeding programs …demanded publication oversight …[and] in-
sisted upon a share of any commercial breakthroughs made using
the Oncomouse.”
These and similar restrictions may limit the use by other researchers
of research tools such as the Oncomouse. Moreover, as the NIH Working
Group on Research Tools (1998) noted in its report, licensees may have
an incentive to restrict access to these materials:
“If the sponsor or licensee plans to develop the research tool as a
commercial product for sale to researchers, it may be unwilling to
permit the university to undercut its position in this particular
market by giving the tool away to potential paying customers.”
Surveys of scientists by Walsh et al. (2007) and Lei et al. (2009) ﬁnd
that requests by researchers for research tools from industrial re-
searchers, a group more likely to be engaged in commercial applica-
tions of research, were rejected approximately twice as often as re-
quests to other academics.
As this discussion of “research tools” indicates, developing a deﬁ-
nition that facilitates their identiﬁcation and empirical analysis is
challenging. The deﬁnitions of research tools employed by the NIH
Working Group and Walsh et al. (2007) are very broad and do not lend
themselves to empirical operationalization using administrative data.
Instead of attempting to develop and defend a deﬁnition of research
tools that relies on the characteristics of the relevant invention dis-
closure or patent, we identify research tools based on the existence of a
Material Transfer Agreement (MTA) associated with a patented inven-
tion disclosure.
Material Transfer Agreements (MTAs) are agreements that govern
the transfer and exchange of materials used in research. Although the
informal exchange by researchers of biological materials for use in
fundamental research has a long and occasionally controversial history
in the biomedical sciences, these materials exchanges historically were
governed by little more than a letter from the source accompanying the
materials, requesting acknowledgement and in some cases asking that
the materials not be passed on to third parties (McCain, 1991). The
more elaborate MTAs used in many contemporary materials exchanges
appear to be a byproduct of the post-1980 surge in academic patenting
(Streitz and Bennett, 2003).
It is important to highlight the diﬀerences between MTAs and
formal licenses. The “research tools” that typically are covered by MTAs
are inputs to other research activities, rather than a discovery that may
have commercial potential. Research tools frequently are linked to li-
censed research advances, but both the intent and the content of li-
censes and MTAs diﬀer from one another. Licensing of academic re-
search advances generally is limited to patented discoveries, but many
MTAs cover unpatented research results, including cell lines, data, and
physical artifacts. Many if not most licensing agreements contain ex-
tensive language covering the ﬁnancial terms under which the user of a
patented academic discovery is legally bound to compensate the li-
censor institution, while MTAs rarely include royalty provisions beyond
occasional “reach-through” provisions covering the use by recipient
researchers of a research input in a research advance that is itself li-
censed for royalties. Academic research projects may generate artifacts
that are transferred to other researchers under the terms of an MTA, but
produce nothing that is patented and/or licensed.
We use the presence of an MTA to proxy whether an invention
disclosure is a research tool.7 To conﬁrm that MTAs are a good in-
dicator that an invention disclosure is associated with a research tool,
we examine UC Berkeley data on incoming MTAs—that is, the agree-
ments associated with research materials requested from other re-
searchers by UC Berkeley researchers during 2004–2008. These data
describe the requested materials and their intended use by the recipient
UC Berkeley researcher. We analyzed a random sample of 50 of these
MTAs, and found that 44% were related to DNA/RNA/Plasmids, 32%
concerned cell lines or other biological/chemical agents, 16% were
animal models, 6% were data transfers, and 2% were concerned with
“other non-research inputs.” Overall, therefore, 98% of these MTAs

Using citations to related scientiﬁc papers as a proxy for scientiﬁc
communication, do licenses on patents tend to increase or decrease
such communication, and (2) how does negotiation of a license for a
disclosure linked to a research tool aﬀect citations to related research
papers?
3. Data and methodology
This section describes our data and methodology. We draw on two
principal sources of data for our empirical analysis. The ﬁrst, the “IP
data,” is an extract from the technology disclosure database maintained
by the technology transfer oﬃce within the University of California
Oﬃce of the President (UCOP). UCOP monitors and in some cases
manages invention disclosures, patent applications, and licensing
transactions for nine campuses of the University of California (including
ﬁve medical schools) during the period of our study.8
These data list 11,341 inventions reported by University of
California faculty from 1997 to 2007. These invention disclosures led to
2035 issued U.S. patents, 1890 licenses to these patents, and 3853
MTAs by the end of 2009. Note that only a small subset of technology
disclosures is patented, and universities’ patenting propensity varies
among ﬁelds of academic research—since the 1980s, patenting and li-
censing activity at UC has been dominated by biomedical research
(Mowery et al., 2004). The distribution of MTAs also is highly skewed,
with few disclosures generating the majority of MTAs and many dis-
closures associated with no MTAs. This echoes the ﬁnding in Mowery
and Ziedonis (2007) that MTAs are disproportionately concentrated in
biomedical ﬁelds of research, as are licenses.
The second source, “publications data,” comes from the Web of
Science, an internet-based service that tracks the bibliographic in-
formation and the citations to and from articles published in 10,000 of
the highest-impact journals across 256 disciplines.9 This database
supplied the title, author names, journal, publication date, and citation
information for each scientiﬁc paper. The information on “forward ci-
tations,” citations from subsequent published papers to that publica-
tion, was extracted through the end of 2009. The Web of Science also
provides a number of well-accepted measures of journal quality. The
most prominent of these is an “impact factor” that measures the average
number of times an article in that journal is cited in its ﬁrst two years,
and which we include in our analysis.
Observing the relationship between the licensing of a patent on ci-
tations to a related publication requires identifying the linkages among
publications, patents, and licenses. The connections among disclosures,
patents, and licenses are contained in the data provided by UCOP,
which tracks these directly. But the UC data themselves provide no
direct link between published papers and any patents covering the re-
search advances covered by them—what Murray and Stern (2007)
termed “patent paper pairs.” Prior work linking patents to related
journal articles has relied on expert judgment (Murray, 2002; Murray
and Stern, 2007) or matched publication characteristics to scientiﬁc
references in patents (Azoulay et al., 2012). We instead employ an
“inventor-based matching” methodology to link patents to scientiﬁc
papers. This technique assumes that inventors listed on a patent are
likely to be authors of related publications. We thus construct a max-
imum-likelihood estimator for the publication(s) that best match a
particular patent. We describe this technique in greater detail in the
Appendix.10
3.1. Construction of control groups
Designing a speciﬁcation to test the eﬀect of licensing on scientiﬁc
communication is a challenging exercise, because of the complex and
varied patterns of citations that publications may receive during our
sample period. Citations to some publications may grow throughout
the period covered by our data, whereas for other publications, ci-
tations may rise, peak, and then decline. Moreover, the timing and
rate of any ascent and decline in citations may vary. It is diﬃcult to
construct a suﬃciently ﬂexible parametric model to adequately ac-
commodate these diﬀerences, yet it is essential to accommodate
variations in citation patterns in order to construct a credible control
sample and avoid spurious statistical results.11 Because of the com-
plex and potentially non-linear interactions of various control vari-
ables that we employ to construct treatment and matching control
groups, we pursue a ﬂexible nonparametric approach. This technique
allows us to weaken the assumption of linearity and enables us to
account for the many plausible interaction eﬀects that may be present
in our analysis.
The non-parametric method we use is “nearest neighbor” matching.
It searches the set of non-treatment observations to identify the one
“closest” to each treated observation, based on the characteristics of
nontreatment and treated observations. Collectively, these “closest”
non-treatment observations form a control group. Because this search is

Bias Reduction] hold and, even more so, when they do not.”15
Our procedure employs two types of variables (observable char-
acteristics), those where we specify an exact match and those where a
nearby (nearest neighbor) match is suﬃcient. We require an exact
match on the following variables:
• Publication Age: Number of years between paper publication date
and year of the observation.
• Journal Subject: Academic discipline of the journal (e.g., medicine).
• Patent Granted (yes/no): Whether the related patent has been
granted at the time of license.
• MTA Issued (yes/no): Whether the paper has an associated MTA at
the time of the license.
For example, these restrictions imply that in our analysis a (li-
censed) treatment observation in the life sciences with an issued patent
and no MTA would be compared with a non-licensed control group
observation that also has those characteristics (i.e., (1) it is in the life
sciences, (2) it is associated with an issued patent, (3) it has no MTA,
and (4) the number of years since publication is identical for the
treatment and control observations).
For each treatment observation that matches on these exact char-
acteristics, we then choose its nearest neighbor based on its proximity
in the following ﬁve characteristics:
• Journal Impact Factor.
• Publication Year.
• Citations in t −2: Number of citations two years before the treat-
ment.
• Citations in t −1: Number of citations one year before the treat-
ment.
• Slope of citation curve between t −2 and t −1.
In each of these dimensions we limit the maximum “distance” be-
tween each treatment and control observation to one standard devia-
tion for that variable (the “caliper”), thus excluding any observation
that diﬀers by more than that amount for any characteristic.16 Under
this procedure, treatment observations with no equivalent control ob-
servation are dropped from the sample. Collectively, these restrictions
produce control observations such that for each characteristic, the
control matches either exactly or within one standard deviation to the
corresponding treatment observation.17
Before we estimate the eﬀects of licensing on citations to patented
publications, we must verify the covariate balance between the treat-
ment and control groups. An eﬀective matching procedure should yield
summary statistics for the treatment and control groups that are similar.
Table 1 reports the mean of each variable for the treatment and control
groups produced by genetic matching and the results of t-tests (diﬀer-
ence of means) and Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) tests (diﬀerence in dis-
tributions) between these two groups.
The means and distributions for the ﬁrst four variables reported in
Table 1, Publication Age, Journal Subject, Patent Issued, and MTA Issued,
are equal between the treatment and control groups, which is not sur-
prising in light of our requirement for an exact match in these dimen-
sions across the two groups. For the remaining variables, means for the
control and treatment groups are much more similar than prior to
matching, although diﬀerences in means remain statistically signiﬁcant
for four variables (Journal Impact Factor, Citations in t −1, Citations in
t −2, and Citations Slope). Diﬀerences in distributions are signiﬁcant for
only two variables, Citations in t −1 and Citations Slope, as shown by the
KS Test. The magnitude and signiﬁcance of these diﬀerences suggests a
relatively balanced sample, but one where further controls are needed
to ensure that these diﬀerences are not driving the result. The coeﬃ-
cient estimates reported in Section 4 suggest that the diﬀerences in
observable characteristics do not meaningfully inﬂuence our estimated
eﬀects.

Using a diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences estimator allows us to avoid bias
associated with changes that aﬀect the “before” and “after” periods
equally. For example, in the matched sample case, if a particular pub-
lication receives on average ﬁve additional citations per year due to
some unobserved covariate, these citations will be included in both
Citationst−1 and Citationst+1 terms, and the impact on the estimate will
be zero.
To summarize, our analysis employs two techniques. First, we
identify the nearest neighbor to each of our treatment observations
using genetic matching. This non-parametric matching technique
means that we make fewer assumptions about the parametric form of
the eﬀect than we would with a linear or generalized linear speciﬁca-
tion (e.g., negative binomial). We then estimate the size and direction
of the treatment eﬀect by employing a diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences ap-
proach that accounts for any remaining unobserved diﬀerences be-
tween the treated and control observations that are constant over time.
Based on this sample construction, our results should be interpreted as
an Average Treatment Eﬀect on the Treated (ATT). In other words, our
sample construction implies that our estimates of the eﬀects of licensing
on citations have the most external validity for patent-linked publica-
tions whose covariates resemble those that receive licenses.

4.1. The eﬀects of licensing on citations to patent-linked publications
We now turn to estimating the eﬀects of licensing on scientiﬁc
communication as measured by citations to patent-linked publications.
We ﬁrst report the ﬁndings based on our full sample (588 observations).
The eﬀect can be seen directly through the citation pattern for the
treatment group (dashed line with circles) and the control group
(dotted line with squares), as shown in Fig. 1.
As depicted in the ﬁgure, patent-linked publications that are li-
censed receive more citations immediately after a license is executed
than do publications that are linked to unlicensed patents. This diﬀer-
ence in the numbers of citations between the treated group and control
group slowly increases in years following the license.
Fig. 2 reports results from the diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences analysis (Eq.
(2)), conﬁrming the post-licensing citation pattern presented in Fig. 1
and testing for the statistical signiﬁcance of the diﬀerences in citations
between the two groups. The eﬀect of licensing on the number of ci-
tations is not statistically signiﬁcant in the year of the execution of the
license (γ = 0.91 with a standard error of 0.57). On average, papers
linked to a licensed patent receive 1.46 more annual citations than
papers linked to non-licensed patents by the ﬁrst year (standard error of
1.14), a diﬀerence that grows to 2.56 citations by year t + 2 (standard
error of 1.15) and 3.43 citations by year t + 3 (standard error of 2.17).
Only the coeﬃcient in year t + 2 is barely signiﬁcant at a 10% level.
This pattern is very similar to the citation trends depicted in Fig. 1, a
result that we interpret as reﬂecting the good covariate balance prior to
the diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences regression.19
Inspection of the residuals from our regressions (not reported)
shows that the mean and median are aligned and that the residuals are
distributed approximately normally, supporting the validity of our
model.

5. Discussion and conclusion
This paper has investigated the relationship between licensing and
communication among scientists of published research results that are
linked to patented academic invention disclosures within the University
of California system. Our results suggest that in general, licenses cov-
ering patented research disclosures have little impact on the number of
citations to related publications (changes in the numbers of citations
are not signiﬁcant for any years in the overall treated sample relative to
the control group and only weakly signiﬁcant (at the 9% conﬁdence
level) for a single year in the overall sample). When we disaggregate
our sample into those discoveries that are inputs to further research
(research tools, i.e., patented disclosures for which MTAs are nego-
tiated) and those discoveries not identiﬁed as research tools, however,
we ﬁnd contrasting results. Licensing is associated with a weak but
positive eﬀect on follow-on journal citations to articles related to pa-
tented disclosures that are not deﬁned as research tools. But licensing
has a signiﬁcantly negative correlation with citations to scientiﬁc pa-
pers linked to a class of patented invention disclosures that we believe
includes a high share of research tools. For these inventions we observe

Our results on the eﬀect of licensing on scientiﬁc communication
expand earlier ﬁndings in the literature that examine the eﬀect of li-
censing on the citation behavior of nonlicensees. Drivas et al. (2017)
and Sampat and Ziedonis (2004) found that licensing is associated with
increased inventive eﬀort in related ﬁelds, assuming that increased ci-
tations to the focal patents reﬂect such inventive eﬀort. We ﬁnd a si-
milarly positive but nonsigniﬁcant eﬀect (at the 5% level) of licensing
on citations to papers related to the licensed patent for non-research-
tool related discoveries.
Our ﬁndings regarding the links between licenses and citations to
patented disclosures covering research tools, however, are consistent
with other arguments and some evidence that academic licensing of
intellectual property may limit dissemimation and exploitation of re-
search advances that are important for follow-on research (Heller and
Eisenberg, 1998; Walsh et al., 2007). The results of Walsh et al. (2007)
underscore the potential impediments to research resulting from de-
nials of access to research materials.
Other anecdotal evidence also suggests that the commercial ex-
ploitation of university research advances may be associated with re-
strictions on access to research materials. One example of the con-
sequences of such a restriction was reported in Science (Cohen, 1997).
Scientists at Stanford University developed a machine to automate the
low-cost synthesis of oligonucleotides, which are critical building
blocks of DNA. Prior to the development of this technique, researchers
using “oligos” as research inputs had to purchase them from commer-
cial ﬁrms at high cost. The availability of low-cost research materials
had the potential to accelerate progress in research requiring such in-
puts, such as in the Human Genome Project. Stanford licensed its
technology exclusively to a private ﬁrm that sold oligos to researchers
but that ﬁrm refused to further make the technology available to re-
searchers for their own use. Although the project was completed ahead
of its original schedule, genomics researchers argued at the time that
this license was “counterproductive to the goal of completing the
Human Genome Project as quickly as possible” according to Cohen (p.
1489).
In a more recent episode, scientists studying Rett syndrome, a de-
bilitating disease primarily aﬀecting young girls, have complained
about the inability to obtain access to a genetically modiﬁed mouse that
exhibits traits useful in research on this disease (Hayden, 2011). They
blame provisions within the original license that restricted sharing of
this input with other researchers. According to Hayden, “scientists and
experts in intellectual property issues say that the case shows how
science can be impeded when onerous licensing rules govern the
sharing of research materials” (p. 318).
How might the licensing of a research tool cause a decline in sci-
entiﬁc communication or restrict follow-on research in scientiﬁc areas
that are reliant on access to such tools? As we note in Section 2.2, re-
searchers who receive requests for materials are more likely to refuse
requests when they are involved in related commercial activity, which
could include licensing, as Walsh et al. (2007) reported. Such activity
could center on the commercialization of a licensed invention linked to
a research tool. Campbell et al. (2002) found a similar result in their
survey of geneticists-those engaged in “commercial activities” were
72% more likely to deny others’ requests for research inputs than sci-
entists not engaged in such activities.
Restriction of scientiﬁc communication as a result of licensing may
also be an indirect outcome of scientiﬁc competition. For example,
Strandburg (2008) quotes the Harvard University scientist who devel-
oped the Oncomouse as characterizing the terms of the license with
DuPont as a “perfect alibi for refusing to send [the mouse] to
others—he was no longer a strategic opportunist but rather a scientist
beleaguered by legal constraints” (p. 64).
Our data unfortunately do not enable us to discern among these and
other possible explanations as underpinning a negative eﬀect of licen-
sing of research tools on subsequent scientiﬁc communication and
follow-on discovery that depends on access to such materials. This
question remains an object for future investigation.
Despite our best eﬀorts to construct comparable treatment and
control groups, could uncaptured diﬀerences in “quality” between li-
censed and unlicensed inventions account for our ﬁndings? We believe
that the contrasting results between the MTA-linked and non-MTA-
linked samples should mitigate such a concern. If our licensed pub-
lications sample exhibited higher quality than unlicensed publications,
we should ﬁnd higher citations everywhere, which would work against
our negative ﬁnding in the MTA-linked sample.
Nevertheless, our ﬁndings should be interpreted with caution. Our
sample of patented publications represents discoveries made by scien-
tists at a single institution, albeit one with nine campuses and ﬁve
academic medical centers.26 The University of California has been as-
signed more U.S. patents than any other U.S. university, and therefore
may not be representative of all academic institutions. Our MTA-linked
subset of inventions representing research tools is small (61 observa-
tions), also limiting our ability to generalize our ﬁndings. At the same
time, however, our comprehensive matching process generates treat-
ment and control groups that are much better balanced than larger-
sample analyses that assume that licensed and unlicensed research are
similar (which they often are not). Thus, while we have a small sample,
our internal validity is relatively strong, and we are less susceptible to
misleading results that arise from incorrect parametric assumptions.
Although our empirical results suggest that the licensing of research
tools may impede scientiﬁc communication, we are unable to reach
strong conclusions about the eﬀects of licensing on scientiﬁc commu-
nication for the (potentially) much larger set of research tools not
covered by MTAs. Moreover, our sample of MTA-linked disclosures is
limited in scope. Developing clearer and empirically tractable measures
that enable the identiﬁcation of research tools is an important task for
future research.
This study makes three contributions. First, it introduces a novel
method for linking patents and publications associated with an aca-
demic research advance, and thereby automates a previously arduous
process that has been diﬃcult to scale up. Second, it employs a
matching methodology that avoids strong and potentially inappropriate
parametric assumptions about the complex interactions that underpin
journal citation patterns. And ﬁnally, but most importantly, this paper
contributes to our understanding of the eﬀect of the licensing of in-
tellectual property rights covering academic research output on scien-
tiﬁc communication.
