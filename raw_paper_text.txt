ABSTRACT  
This study describes how middle school science students enacted 
epistemic vigilance while co-constructing scientific explanations 
with generative artificial intelligence (GenAI). Using a qualitative 
research design, the study investigated the interactions of eight 
middle school students with Canva and ChatGPT during two 
science lessons on plant growth and the classification of fungi. To 
support their epistemic vigilance, two explicit epistemic scaffolds 
were implemented: the criteria for good scientific explanations as 
epistemic criteria and epistemic question prompts as reflective 
guides. Data including transcribed and translated video 
recordings of student-GenAI interactions, student-generated 
digital artifacts, and semi-structured interviews were analyzed 
using a multi-layered analytical framework. Findings indicate that 
students enacted epistemic vigilance through three 
complementary evaluative practices: evaluating the scientific 
quality of GenAI-generated explanations, verifying the credibility 
of information sources, and reflecting on their own 
understanding and assumptions while interpreting AI outputs. 
The epistemic criteria primarily scaffolded students’ content 
evaluation while indirectly supporting source and receiver 
evaluation. Conversely, the epistemic question prompts explicitly 
scaffolded source and receiver evaluation while remaining 
grounded in evaluating their co-constructed scientific explanation 
quality. These findings suggest that within their cognitive levels, 
middle school students may engage in epistemic vigilance with 
explicit epistemic scaffolding. Limitations are further discussed.

Addressing this challenge requires epistemic vigilance, defined as learners’ capacity to 
critically evaluate communicated information before accepting it (Sperber et al., 2010). 
Although epistemic vigilance has been conceptualised in science education (Bielik & 
Krell, 2025; Tseng et al., 2021), little empirical research has examined how students 
enact it while co-constructing scientific explanations with GenAI, particularly when 
supported by explicit epistemic scaffolds. This study addresses this gap by investigating 
how middle school students enact epistemic vigilance during GenAI-supported co- 
construction of scientific explanations using epistemic criteria and epistemic question 
prompts.
Literature review
Because GenAI has the potential to enhance learning, creativity, and knowledge pro-
duction, interest in using it to teach science has grown significantly (Mishra et al., 
2023). According to O’Toole and Horvý’s (2024) study, conversational GenAI chatbots 
can aid metacognitive processes by making information retrieval and problem solving 
easier. Additionally, GenAI has potential for mechanical reasoning in physics discourse 
(Sirnoorkar et al., 2024), inquiry-based learning (Erduran & Levrini, 2024
; Penn & Ram-
narain, 2025), and evaluation of epistemic understanding (Chang et al., 2026). By offering 
immediate feedback and tailored guidance via student prompts, GenAI chatbots promote 
self-regulated learning (Ng et al., 2024).
Despite these potentials, researchers raised the issue of hallucination, which brings an 
emphasis on how humans, including students, must remain central agents in AI- 
mediated learning environments. Students’ agency involves their participation, owner
-
ship of outcomes, and mental effort while engaged with GenAI (Draxler et al., 2024; 
Guo et al., 2025). This framing revolves around the idea that although GenAI can accom-
plish tasks efficiently, they still need humans to demonstrate exploratory thinking pat-
terns (Boussioux et al., 2024). Accoridngly, GenAI cannot replace human judgment 
because the responsibility for validating knowledge remains with humans (Hemmer 
et al., 2021; Oh & Lee, 2025). Thus, in this time when AI has shaped science learning, 
students should be exposed to human-AI collaboration while being trained to be vigilant 
when to trust, question, or build upon AI-generated outputs (Hoßbach & Isaksen, 2025).

Theoretical framework
Epistemic vigilance
Epistemic vigilance, as earlier defined, is composed of three aspects: content evaluation, 
source evaluation, and receiver evaluation (Bielik & Krell, 2025; Pimentel, 2025; Sperber 
et al., 2010), each with some principles to be considered during evaluation (Figure 1). 
Content evaluation may be assessed through three complementary principles: plausi-
bility, one-sidedness, and context (Duncan et al., 2018; Tseng et al., 2021). In relation 
to learning, it requires students to determine whether explanations are evidence-based, 
credible, or logically coherent (Allchin, 2023; Osborne & Pimentel, 2023). It also involves 
identifying one-sided arguments, missing information, and alternative explanations that 
may challenge scientific claims (Barzilai & Chinn, 2018). Within science learning, this 
aspect is connected to constructing scientific explanations because students are expected

to evaluate evidence, scrutinise reasoning, and distinguish scientific explanations from

The second aspect is source evaluation, which focuses on assessing the trustworthiness 
and credibility of the information source, commonly described as ‘second-hand evalu-
ation’ or evaluating ‘who said it’ (Bromme & Goldman, 2014, p. 65; Sharon & Baram- 
Tsabari, 2020, p. 879). It can be assessed using the two complementary principles of 
expertise and reliability especially with the widespread presence of misinformation and 
unreliable information sources (Allchin, 2023). It is judging the credibility and trust-
worthiness of an information source by considering who produced the information, 
where and when it was published, and whether it reflects reliable scientific standards 
and evidence (Bromme & Goldman, 2014). It also involves evaluating communicators’ 
expertise through their credentials, publication records, experiences, and alignment 
with scientific consensus (Duncan et al., 2018; Sperber et al., 2010).
The third aspect, receiver evaluation, emphasises how individuals reflect on the ways 
information is interpreted. Bielik and Krell (2025, p. 877) described this as ‘third-hand 
evaluation’, wherein individuals critically examine how prior knowledge, beliefs, experi-
ences, emotions, and sociocultural influences shape judgments of scientific information. 
Receiver evaluation acknowledges that people are influenced by cognitive biases, political 
affiliations, and emotional responses when evaluating knowledge claims (Allchin, 2023). 
Complementing this, the principle of cognitive biases and socioemotional influences 
recognises that confirmation bias, intuitive reasoning, emotions, social identity, and 
other sociocultural influences may unconsciously shape the acceptance or rejection of 
scientific information (Allchin, 2023; Barzilai & Chinn, 2018; Feinstein & Baram- 
Tsabari, 2024; Tseng et al., 2021). In science education, this aspect encourages students 
to become aware of factors influencing reasoning and decision-making. In this study, 
while we acknowledge the broad components that may influence receiver evaluation 
from literature, we narrow down to students’ personal and anticipated peer understand
-
ing and how they information will be accepted. These three aspects and their correspond-
ing principles provide a comprehensive framework for examining how students critically 
evaluate GenAI-generated scientific explanations (
Figure 1).
Epistemic scaffolding for epistemic vigilance
Epistemic scaffolding refers to instructional support that explicitly guides learners’ epis-
temic thinking and practices by making the underlying epistemology of scientific inquiry 
visible (Lin & Puntambekar, 2019). Distinct from procedural, conceptual, and metacog-
nitive scaffolding, epistemic scaffolding focuses on how learners evaluate knowledge, 
justify claims, and engage in disciplinary practices that are aligned with those of scien-
tists. Although conceptually distinct, epistemic scaffolding is rarely implemented in iso-
lation. Instead, it is distributed across instructional materials, classroom discourse, 
technological tools, and learning activities, where complementary forms of support 
work synergistically to promote epistemic engagement (Puntambekar, 
2021). Within 
science learning, explicit epistemic scaffolding has been shown to strengthen students’ 
participation in authentic epistemic practices by making epistemic structures, criteria, 
and goals accessible to learners (Barzilai et al., 2020; Lin & Puntambekar, 2019).

In this study, epistemic scaffolding is operationalised through the complementary use 
of epistemic criteria and epistemic question prompts provided by the teacher. The cri-
teria for good scientific explanations, also treated as an epistemic criteria in the study 
(Figure 1) primarily scaffolded content evaluation by making explicit the disciplinary 
standards that scientists use to judge the quality of explanations. Instead of relying on 
intuitive judgments about whether an explanation ‘sounds scientific’, students are pro-
vided with explicit standards for evaluating the adequacy of GenAI-generated expla-
nations. As students apply these criteria, they may also begin to question whether the 
explanation is supported by credible knowledge sources and whether their own under
-
standing influences their interpretation. Thus, while the epistemic criteria are principally 
directed toward evaluating content, they may simultaneously stimulate source evaluation 
and receiver evaluation during the evaluation process.
Complementing the epistemic criteria, epistemic question prompts explicitly directed 
students to examine the credibility of information and to monitor their own epistemic 
reasoning while interacting with GenAI. Prompts that encourage students to verify expla
-
nations with other sources, consider the expertise behind the information, or reflect on 
whether they understood the explanation explicitly scaffold source evaluation and recei-
ver evaluation. However, these prompts remain grounded in the evaluation of scientific 
explanations because they are implicitly informed by one or more epistemic criteria. For 
example, evaluating whether another source confirms an explanation presupposes eval-
uating the scientific adequacy of the explanation itself, while reflecting on one’s under-
standing requires consideration of whether the explanation is conceptually coherent 
and scientifically meaningful. Therefore, the epistemic criteria and epistemic question 
prompts function as complementary explicit epistemic scaffolds that collectively sup
-
ported students’ enactment of epistemic vigilance by guiding coordinated evaluation 
of scientific content, information sources, and their own epistemic reasoning during 
interactions with GenAI.
Problem statement and research question
This study addresses the gap concerning students’ enactment of epistemic vigilance while 
co-constructing scientific explanations with GenAI. Specifically, the study examines how 
two explicit epistemic scaffolds (Barzilai et al., 2020) namely the criteria for good scien-
tific explanations, serving as epistemic criteria (de Andrade et al., 2019), and epistemic 
question prompts, serving as reflective guides supported students’ enactment of episte-
mic vigilance (Figure 1). The epistemic criteria directly guided content evaluation 
while indirectly prompting source and receiver evaluation during interactions with 
GenAI through specific evaluative criteria. Meanwhile, the teacher-provided epistemic 
question prompts guided students to reflect on source and receiver evaluation principles 
while being indirectly informed by at least one epistemic criterion throughout the co- 
construction process.
In this study, co-construction refers to the complementary roles of students and 
GenAI in an iterative process of generating, evaluating, revising, and refining scientific 
explanations. This process included generating images and text, iterative prompting, 
manual editing of GenAI outputs, and students’ interpretation of these representations 
to improve their explanations. Within their cognitive levels, students were particularly

On the other hand, the GenAI complemented the students’ epistemic work as an

Significance of the study
This study advances science education by describing how middle school students enact 
epistemic vigilance while co-constructing scientific explanations with GenAI through 
explicit epistemic scaffolds. It extends current discussions beyond GenAI integration 
to examine students’ epistemic vigilance in terms of content, source, and receiver evalu
-
ation (Bielik & Krell, 2025; Sperber et al., 2010; Tseng et al., 2021). The study also pro-
vides empirical evidence that students’ epistemic vigilance may be fostered through 
explicit scaffolding in the form of an epistemic criteria and epistemic question 
prompts. It further demonstrates how these scaffolds position students as primary epis
-
temic agents while GenAI functions as an information source, conversational partner, 
and epistemic provocateur. The findings may also offer practical guidance for educators 
and curriculum developers seeking to cultivate scientifically grounded reasoning, evalua-
tive judgment, responsible GenAI use, and critical engagement with AI-generated knowl-
edge in contemporary science classrooms through explicit epistemic scaffolding.
Methodology
Context and participants of the study
We used a qualitative research design to describe eight (8) middle school science stu-
dents’ enactment of epistemic vigilance of GenAI-generated knowledge of scientific 
explanations. The study was conducted in Middle School C in Gyeonggi City, South 
Korea as a special after-school session rather than a regular graded classroom activity. 
Prior to participation, it was explained that their participation was not graded, voluntary, 
and they can withdraw from participation anytime. The students were purposively 
selected based on three factors. First, they had previously taken the first author’s sixth- 
grade science class at Elementary School S in 2023, which established a strong 
teacher–student relationship that supported open dialogue and reflective evaluation. 
Second, they already had experience using GenAI tools, specifically Canva and 
ChatGPT in earlier science lessons with the first author. This familiarity allowed class
-
room interactions to focus on epistemic judgment alongside basic tool operation.

Data sources
Three forms of qualitative data were collected: video recordings of students’ interactions 
with GenAI, student-generated artifacts, and semi-structured interviews (Figure 2). All 
classroom conversations and interviews were transcribed verbatim in Korean. while an 
analysis was conducted on all the original Korean transcripts of the entire dataset, 
selected excerpts were translated into English for sample data presentation of our 
findings.
Data analysis procedure
We focused on describing students’ practice of epistemic vigilance of GenAI-constructed 
scientific explanations. The entire dataset, were analyzed following the multi-layered 
framework (Yin & Zeng, 2025) for qualitative inductive studies (Figure 2). The analytical 
framework consisted of four interconnected levels: (1) identification and segmentation of 
epistemic events (macro-level), (2) meso-level coding of evaluative moves, (3) micro- 
level multimodal discourse analysis, and (4) cross-data interpretation and triangulation. 
Following this framework, our sample coded data (Appendix 1) show how we merged the 
transcripts, artifacts, and students’ interviews to develop the themes based on the context 
of our study.
Segmentation and identification of epistemic events (macro-level)
We first applied segmentation to all datasets to capture units of meaningful discourse 
(Erickson & Shultz, 1992). We identified these segments across the two lessons and com-
piled them into a running list. We treated each segment to correspond to an episode 
where students discussed with each other, interacted with, or reflected on their co-con-
structed scientific explanations with GenAI. We paid particular attention to those seg-
ments that captured students’ epistemic practices (i.e. prompt constructions and 
refinement, asking for more detailed illustrations), and evaluation strategies of AI-gen
-
erated explanations, verify information, reflect on their own understanding. At this 
level, the video recordings were used to identify episodes in which students explicitly 
questioned, evaluated, revised, accepted, rejected, or supplemented GenAI outputs 
while referring to the criteria for good scientific explanations or the teacher-provided

Meso-level coding of evaluative moves
Using content analysis (Bazerman, 2006), we performed a meso-level analysis on the 
interaction segments in which students evaluated the GenAI results. We iteratively 
coded these portions with two objectives. First, we determined the students’ evaluative 
position, determining whether they used content evaluation, source evaluation, receiver 
evaluation, or all three. Second, we coded how the students used the explicit epistemic 
scaffolds (Figure 1) when they evaluated the GenAI-constructed scientific explanations. 
Each segment was categorised using one or more categories, depending on the nature of 
the students’ evaluative assessment. The epistemic criteria served as the primary coding 
framework for content evaluation, whereas the epistemic question prompts mostly 
guided the identification of source evaluation and receiver evaluation episodes. Coding 
was carried out in a constant comparative manner across datasets. Verbal utterances 
were cross-referenced with their final prompts. Interviews were also compared to their 
utterances, prompts, and artifacts. Student-generated artefacts were likewise compared 
across datasets to determine how evaluative decisions were reflected their final co-con-
structed scientific explanations.
Micro-level multimodal discourse analysis
We conducted a fine-grained, line-by-line discourse analysis (Feng, 2021
) on the segmen-
ted dataset. This was to unpack how the verbal utterances, written prompts, and artifacts 
provided meanings on the students’ practice of epistemic vigilance as they assessed the 
accuracy, coherence, and strength of their co-constructed scientific explanations. 
Using the meso-level codes as analytical anchors, we examined how students enacted 
content evaluation, source evaluation, and receiver evaluation through their spoken dia
-
logue, prompt revisions, interactions with GenAI, visual representations, and written 
explanations. The micro-level analysis examined how students enacted content, 
source, and receiver evaluation during their interactions with GenAI by analyzing the 
interplay of speech, prompt revisions, GenAI outputs, images and diagrams, and 
written explanations. Finally, we created a codebook (Appendix 1) detailing how the stu
-
dents practiced epistemic vigilance while being guided by the two explicit epistemic 
scaffolds. This served as a guide for coding the full dataset, providing us with insights 
into the principles of epistemic vigilance that the students demonstrated.
Cross-data interpretation and triangulation
We ensured triangulated analysis by iteratively coding all transcripts of students’ dialo-
gues, artifacts, and interview transcripts. The three datasets were integrated to explain 
how the explicit epistemic scaffolds supported students’ enactment of epistemic vigilance 
across the co-construction process. Video recordings captured students’ real-time eva
-
luative interactions, student artefacts documented revisions to their scientific expla-
nations, and interviews provided reflective accounts of why particular GenAI outputs 
were accepted, revised, or rejected. To minimise bias, the second author and an external 
expert independently coded the data before meeting with the primary author to resolve

Findings
In the following subsections, we present the themes generated from the analysis. For each 
theme, we present one representative transcript of the student dialogues containing some 
artifacts and the corresponding interview responses from each pair of students as illustra
-
tive examples of how they enacted epistemic vigilance while co-constructing scientific 
explanations with GenAI. These examples are intended to illustrate the findings within 
the context of this study rather than to represent all instances of students’ interactions.
Theme 1. Content evaluation of the scientific quality of GenAI explanations 
through epistemic criteria
Based on the results, the students enacted content evaluation by examining whether 
GenAI-generated explanations were scientifically plausible, complete, logically coherent, 
and represented appropriately. Their evaluation was mostly focused on the principles of 
plausibility, one-sidedness. Rather than accepting GenAI responses as authoritative, they 
questioned whether explanations were scientifically reasonable, whether important ideas 
had been omitted, and whether the responses were within the bounds of scientifically 
accepted ideas. These evaluative practices were primarily guided by the explicit epistemic 
criteria and supplemented by the epistemic question prompts.
Sample Dialogue 1 illustrates how Students A and B evaluated Canva’s explanation of 
plant growth. After receiving the initial response, they questioned whether it genuinely 
explained the phenomenon, asking, ‘
Does this explanation make scientific sense? It 
gives lots of ideas, but does it explain why plants grow?’ (Line 14). This reflected the cri-
terion of relevance, as students evaluated whether the explanation directly addressed the 
scientific question. They then shifted their attention to the causal story, with Student A 
explaining, ‘
Photosynthesis is important because that’s how plants make food. That 
explains why they can grow’ (Line 15). As they further discussed and recognised that 
Canva described plant structures separately, Student B suggested revising the prompt 
to explain ‘how they work together’ (Lines 17a–18), applying the criterion of conceptual 
framework. Their questioning also demonstrated the principle one-sidedness, as they 
recognised that important causal relationships were missing. So, instead of accepting 
the initial response, they evaluated it first. Their vigilance was further reinforced by 
ChatGPT’s cautionary statement, ‘ChatGPT can make mistakes. Check important info’,

Students also demonstrated plausibility evaluation by questioning whether Canva’s 
explanation aligned with established scientific knowledge. Student B asked, ‘Does this

established scientific knowledge rather than fluency. Student A later reflected, ‘It’s

Canva’s explanation with their science textbook (Lines 21–23), and whether the AI-gen-

its persuasive presentation.

Theme 2. Source evaluation by verifying the credibility of GenAI explanations

expertise principles (Bromme & Goldman, 2014; Sperber et al., 2010). During their 
peer discussions and iterative prompting, they sought corroborating evidence, ques-
tioned the absence of explicit citations, and compared GenAI outputs with authoritative 
scientific resources. Within their cognitive levels, their evaluative practices were triggered 
by the epistemic question prompts while remaining grounded in the epistemic criteria 
used to judge explanatory quality. Alongside content evaluation, students also appeared 
to treat ChatGPT’s reminder, ‘
ChatGPT can make mistakes. Check important info’ as an 
epistemic cue that encouraged them to suspend immediate trust, thus treating this as 
GenAI’s epistemic provocateur function as it constantly prompted them to verify the 
credibility of its own responses.
For instance, following their evaluation of Canva’s explanation of plant growth, Stu-
dents A and B shifted their attention from the explanation itself to the credibility of the 
information. Student B asked, ‘

Discussion
This study examined how middle school science students enacted epistemic vigilance 
while co-constructing scientific explanations with GenAI using two explicit epistemic 
scaffolds: criteria for good scientific explanations and epistemic question prompts. 
Across the three themes, students’ interactions extended beyond retrieving information 
to evaluating explanatory quality, scrutinising information sources, and reflecting on 
their own understanding before accepting AI-generated contributions. These findings 
demonstrate that epistemic vigilance can be deliberately cultivated in GenAI-supported

science learning when instructional scaffolds make disciplinary standards and epistemic 
practices explicit.
The first contribution concerns the role of explicit epistemic criteria in mediating stu-
dents’ evaluation of scientific explanations. Previous research has shown that learners fre-
quently accept AI-generated responses because they are fluent and always appear 
plausible (Kasneci et al., 
2023; Mollick & Mollick, 2023). In contrast, students in this 
study evaluated explanations against disciplinary expectations by questioning causal ade-
quacy, identifying conceptual omissions, reorganising to show interrelationships of plant 
structures, and revising textual and visual representations. These findings suggest that 
epistemic criteria functioned not merely as assessment tools but as mediational resources 
that externalised the norms of scientific explanation, enabling students to participate 
more authentically in disciplinary evaluation. Therefore, epistemic vigilance on their 
co-constructed explanation with the GenAI through content evaluation became an 
observable practice that may be explicitly framed as one of the classroom goals in devel
-
oping students’ epistemic agency.
The complementary use of epistemic question prompts extended this evaluation 
beyond scientific content to include the credibility of information sources and students’ 
own understanding. Students enacted the three dimensions of epistemic vigilance pro
-
posed by Sperber et al. (2010) and Bielik and Krell (2025) in an interconnected epistemic 
processes rather than discrete stages. Also, students frequently moved between evaluating 
explanatory adequacy, corroborating claims with authoritative sources, and reflecting on 
how prior knowledge, assumptions, and anticipated peer understanding shaped their 
evaluations. This dynamic interplay suggests that epistemic vigilance in GenAI-sup
-
ported learning is best understood as a coordinated process of epistemic regulation sup-
ported by explicit instructional scaffolds (Barzilai et al., 2020).
A central theoretical contribution of this study is the positioning of students as the 
primary epistemic agents within human–AI co-construction. Although discussions of 
GenAI in education often emphasise its capacity to enhance epistemic understanding 
(Chang et al., 2026), the present findings shift attention to learners’ epistemic work. Stu-
dents continuously questioned, challenged, verified, revised, and refined GenAI-gener-
ated explanations, with the responsibility for determining scientific acceptability 
remaining with them rather than the technology. This finding reinforces conceptualiz
-
ations of epistemic vigilance as an active human capacity for evaluating communicated 
information (Sperber et al., 2010) and extends this perspective by demonstrating how 
explicit classroom scaffolds enable students to exercise epistemic agency when interact-
ing with GenAI.
The findings also advance current understandings of co-construction between stu-
dents and GenAI. Based on the results, GenAI functioned as an information source, a 
conversational partner, and an epistemic provocateur that stimulated further inquiry. 
Together with the explicit epistemic scaffolds, students in this study became aware of 
incomplete, overly general, or insufficiently represented responses. This, therefore 
prompted them to identify explanatory gaps, formulate increasingly precise questions, 
and iteratively refine both prompts and explanations. Co-construction therefore 
emerged as a reciprocal epistemic process in which students’ evaluations shaped sub-
sequent GenAI responses, while those responses generated new opportunities for disci-
plinary reasoning. This perspective reframes the educational value of GenAI in the

learning process from producing responses to prompts to provoking productive episte-

The findings likewise suggest that prompting should be conceptualised as an epistemic 
practice rather than a technical skill. Students refined prompts because they first ident-
ified conceptual gaps, questioned explanatory adequacy, or recognised representational 
limitations. Prompt construction was therefore inseparable from scientific reasoning 
and epistemic evaluation. This implies that prompt engineering in science education 
should be embedded within disciplinary inquiry Siegle (2025), where increasingly soph-

Finally, this study highlights the importance of explicit epistemic scaffolding for 
science education in the age of GenAI. Because AI-generated responses are shaped by 
users’ prompts, students require support to formulate purposeful questions, evaluate evi

dence systematically, and refine explanations iteratively. These scaffolds functioned as 
mediational resources that supported participation in authentic scientific practices 
while preserving students’ responsibility for epistemic judgment. Therefore, as GenAI 
becomes increasingly integrated into classrooms, science education should therefore 
prioritise developing students’ epistemic vigilance alongside scientific reasoning and 
digital literacy. The findings demonstrate that epistemic vigilance is not an automatic 
consequence of GenAI use but a learnable disciplinary practice that can be intentionally 
fostered through carefully designed instructional supports.
Conclusion
This study described how middle school students enacted epistemic vigilance during 
their co-construction of scientific explanations with GenAI through three interconnected 
practices: evaluating the scientific adequacy of explanations, scrutinising the credibility of 
information sources, and reflecting on their own understanding before accepting AI-gen
-
erated knowledge. These practices were supported by explicit epistemic scaffolds that 
guided students to question, verify, and refine scientific explanations throughout iterative 
interactions with GenAI. Thus, the findings demonstrate that epistemic vigilance is not 
an automatic consequence of using AI but a disciplinary practice that can be intentionally 
fostered through instructional support.
This study also advances current literature by extending epistemic vigilance from a 
theoretical construct describing how people evaluate communicated information to an 
instructional framework for GenAI-supported science learning. It further contributes by 
showing how explicit epistemic scaffolds operationalise content, source, and receiver evalu
-
ation during authentic classroom interactions, while positioning students as the primary 
epistemic agents and GenAI as an information source, conversational partner, and episte-
mic provocateur. These findings broaden existing discussions of human-GenAI collabor-
ation by emphasising students’ epistemic work instead of the capabilities of AI systems.
The findings also suggest that science education should prioritise the development of 
epistemic vigilance alongside scientific reasoning and digital literacy. Explicit epistemic 
scaffolds can help students formulate purposeful prompts, critically evaluate AI-gener-
ated explanations, and iteratively improve scientific understanding, thereby supporting 
more meaningful engagement with GenAI.

Limitations
Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, it involved a relatively 
small sample of participants, which limits the transferability of the findings to broader 
educational settings. Additionally, participant selection involved students taught by the 
first author, creating a preexisting teacher-student relationship that may have influenced 
participants’ interactions and interview responses. Further, due to the students’ famili-
arity with the teacher, they have felt inclined to provide socially desirable responses or 
demonstrate behaviours perceived as academically appropriate. However, to minimise 
the bias related to power dynamics between the teacher and the students, the classes 
were non-graded. This is in addition to the several ethical procedures we implemented 
including voluntary participation, parental consent, and data anonymization. Future 
studies should therefore involve larger and more diverse participant groups, include 
researchers independent of classroom assessment processes, and examine epistemic vig
-
ilance practices across varied science learning contexts and educational levels. In light of 
the limitations, the credibility of this study can be attributed to robust qualitative data 
corpus, which ensured triangulation and a rich understanding of how epistemic vigilance 
was enacted during co-construction of scientific explanations with GenAI. Further, the 
fine-grained line-by-line interaction analysis captured students’ epistemic vigilance in 
real time.
