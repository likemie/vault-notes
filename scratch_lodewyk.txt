Students with differing profiles of epistemological beliefs—their beliefs about personal epistemol-
ogy, intelligence, and learning—vary in thinking, reasoning, motivation, and use of strategies while
working on academic tasks, each of which affect learning. This study examined students’ episte-
mological beliefs according to gender, school orientation, overall academic achievement, and
performance on two differently structured academic tasks. Epistemological beliefs in fixed and
quick ability to learn, simple knowledge, and certain knowledge differed significantly as a function
of gender, school orientation, and levels of academic achievement. These beliefs, particularly the
belief in simple knowledge, significantly predicted overall performance and reflective judgment
scores on the ill-structured task but not on the well-structured task. Implications concerning the
relations among epistemological beliefs, reflective judgment, gender, school orientation, task struc-
ture, and achievement are discussed.
Among a number of influences on student learning and performance are students’
personal epistemologies—their beliefs about the nature of knowledge. Psychologically
and educationally, personal epistemology beliefs are “how individuals come to know,
the theories and beliefs they hold about knowing, and the manner in which such epis-
temological premises influence the cognitive processes of thinking and reasoning”
(Hofer & Pintrich, 1997, p. 88). For example, some learners believe that knowledge
is simple (unrelated facts) and certain (absolute), and that learning is a genetically
inherited disposition that occurs quickly (Schommer, 1990, 1993), and reason
simplistically, with inadequate justification, from an individual frame of reference
(King & Kitchener, 1994). In the last decade, the construct of personal epistemology

Epistemological Beliefs
Conceptualisations of epistemological beliefs reflect different philosophical views
held about the origins of scientific knowledge and learning. Early theories of
personal epistemology characterised the construct as relatively unidimensional—
having a rather uniform developmental trajectory. These include Perry’s (1968)
scheme of intellectual and ethical viewpoints, ways of knowing (Belenky, Clinchy,
Goldberger, & Tarule, 1986), epistemological reflection (Baxter Magolda, 2002),
epistemic doubts (Boyes & Chandler, 1992), and persuasive argumentation (Kuhn,
1991). Several recent theories have stressed the multidimensionality and variable
developmental trajectories of personal epistemology as beliefs about schooled knowl-
edge (Buehl, Alexander, & Murphy, 2002), epistemic beliefs (Schraw et al., 1995),
and reflective judgments (King & Kitchener, 2002; Kitchener & King, 1981).
A general theme in current epistemological belief theory postulates that students
develop from a position where knowledge is “perceived as right or wrong to a posi-
tion of relativism and then to a position in which individuals are active construc-
tors of meaning, able to make judgments and commitments in a relativistic
context” (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997, p. 121). Theorists (e.g., Hofer, 2001) elabo-
rated this to characterise epistemological belief development as a continuum.
Learners initially hold a dualistic view of knowledge as certain and objective. This
evolves to a position where knowledge is considered relative, and multiple but
inconsistent positions can be held simultaneously: this is called uncertain subjectiv-
ism. Ultimately, learners can acquire the capability to coordinate multiple and
contrasting views relative to context while grounding evaluation of validity in rela-
tion to context.

The developmental sequence in many of the models of personal epistemology
takes place in different stages, phases, or perspectives, generally climaxing with a
cognitively mature individual who “thinks epistemologically, relativistically, and
dialectically—thinking that goes beyond formal logic beyond formal operations”
(Schommer, 1994, p. 308). For example, Perry’s (1968) intellectual and ethical
viewpoints scheme has nine stages; Baxter Magolda’s (2002) epistemological reflec-
tion model aligns four ways of knowing with Perry’s (1968) scheme; King and
Kitchener’s (1994) reflective judgment model uses interviews in which ill-structured
problems are posed and can have seven stages of response; and Kuhn’s (1991)
model examining persuasive argumentation on ill-structured problems posits three
categories of epistemological views (absolutist, multiplist, and evaluativist).
The most widely used quantitative approach to assessing beliefs about the
nature of knowledge and of learning in educational research has been Schom-
mer’s (1990) 63-item Epistemological Questionnaire (SEQ) and her Modified
Epistemological Questionnaire (SMEQ) for high school students (Schommer,
1993; Schommer & Dunnell, 1997). In it, items are categorised into 12 subsets
based on the view that epistemological beliefs are relatively independent (multidi-
mensional) rather than part of a coherent progressive structure. For example,
epistemological beliefs can vary within individuals along various dimensions or
factors. Schommer (1990) initially theorised five dimensions of epistemological
beliefs, but results from administrations of her questionnaire have identified only
four: speed of acquiring knowledge (learning quickly or not at all), simplicity of
knowledge (isolated facts to memorise or highly interrelated concepts to under-
stand), certainty of knowledge (absolute rather than contextual), and sources of
knowledge and ability (innate rather than acquired). Viewing knowledge as
simple, certain, or innate reflects a person’s beliefs about the nature of knowl-
edge, while views on the speed of knowledge acquisition and the source of ability
signal a person’s beliefs in the nature of learning. Her fifth theorised epistemolog-
ical belief component (belief in omniscient authority or that knowledge is handed
down by authority rather than attained through reason) has not been consistently
identified in subsequent research (e.g., Qian & Alvermann, 1995) using the SEQ
or SMEQ.
Research using the SEQ and the SMEQ has revealed links between several of
Schommer’s proposed dimensions and academic achievement. For example, in
several studies involving high school students, belief in quick learning has
predicted grade point average (Schommer, 1993; Schommer, Calvert, Garigli-
etti, & Bajaj, 1997), while beliefs that knowledge is simple and certain and that
learning occurs quickly have predicted conceptual change (Qian & Alvermann,
1995). Schommer (1990; see also Schommer et al., 1992) has found relations
between certain epistemological beliefs and accuracy of comprehension calibra-
tion, gender (1993), critical thinking and integration of information (Schommer
& Dunnell, 1997), coping with complex tasks and using strategies for studying
(Schommer et al., 1992), achievement (1990, 1993; Schommer et al., 1992),
students’ age and year in school (Schommer et al., 1997), and parental educa-

In the few studies investigating gender differences in epistemological beliefs,
female students had significantly fewer debilitating beliefs in quick learning
(Enman & Lupart, 2000; Schommer, 1993; Schommer et al., 1997; Wood &

found that college males had more sophisticated beliefs about the structure,
construction, and modification of knowledge, with stronger beliefs “that knowledge

Vekiri, & Harrison, 2004) have not found gender differences in epistemological
beliefs. More research is needed into gender differences on various dimensions of
epistemological beliefs.
Epistemological Beliefs and Task Performance
Research indicates that individual differences among students mediate how features
of tasks relate to learning and performance (Blumenfeld et al., 1991; Lodewyk &
Winne, 2005). For example, Balzer, Doherty, and O’Connor (1989), along with
Schommer et al. (1992), reported that students who believed a task or concept was
“just a bunch of isolated facts” were less inclined to select tactics for learning rela-
tionships within and among concepts. Researchers have asserted that some profiles
of epistemological beliefs can bias learners’ perceptions of important conditions in
academic domains (Buehl et al., 2002; Hofer, 1999) and tasks (Hofer, 2001; Winne
& Hadwin, 1998), and may interact with related behaviours like helplessness
(Pintrich, Marx, & Boyle, 1993) or constructs like belief in fixed (innate) ability
(Dweck & Leggett, 1988) when learners engage in difficult tasks. Schoenfeld (1985)
reported that the more students believe learning is quick, the less time and effort
they will invest in problem-solving.
Over time, the structure of learning tasks may shape epistemological beliefs that
are difficult to change. For instance, in his theory of epistemic motivation, Kruglan-
ski (1990) asserts that learning in the context of ambiguous tasks (Doyle, 1983) is
shaped by both cognitive and motivational factors such that students either seek
closure (cognitively freeze) or avoid closure (cognitively unfreeze). In reviews, Hofer
(2001) and Hofer and Pintrich (1997) reported that students who avoid closure
more actively engage in hypothesis testing, thereby creating opportunities to
progress along the developmental continuum of epistemological beliefs about knowl-
edge. In this context, epistemological beliefs may play a key role as standards or

In various lines of research, epistemological beliefs have been assessed in the
context of students pursing ill-structured or well-structured tasks (King & Kitch-
ener, 1994; Schraw et al., 1995) or domains (Buehl et al., 2002; Hofer, 1999).
Frederiksen (1984) and others (e.g., Spiro, Coulson, Feltovich & Anderson, 1988)
theorise that learning tasks generally display both well-structured and ill-structured
features. A relatively well-structured task (WST) is usually more clearly formulated
and presented, and requires straightforward operations, providing the necessary
information, algorithms, and precise criteria to indicate how the completed
learning task will be assessed to aid the learner in searching for a suitable answer
(Fredericksen, 1984; Schraw et al., 1995). A relatively ill-structured task (IST)
makes less obvious the operations to be used, is more ambiguous (does not have a
clearly identifiable answer), and does not provide the necessary information (clear
instructions), algorithms (cues and resources), or evaluative criteria for students to
determine if they are solving the problem correctly (Lodewyk & Winne, 2005;
Spiro et al., 1988). 
A task’s positioning on this continuum of well-structured to ill-structured can
have important effects. King and Kitchener (1994) reported on five studies ( n =
172) of the reflective judgment abilities of high school students completing ill-
structured problems. Students consistently demonstrated pre-reflective thinking
(Stages 1–3 of 7; mean = 3.2) that indicated beliefs in certain knowledge and
decision-making about tasks, while relying mainly on personal belief and feeling
rather than on sound argumentative reasoning and justification, which would
have indicated more quasi-reflective (Stages 4 and 5) and reflective thinking
(Stages 6 and 7). However, Blumenfeld et al. (1991) noted that tasks which are
not developmentally appropriate, have stringent time limits, have only one correct
answer or process, are performed in an environment that focuses on performance
at the expense of mastery, and are inauthentic are also probably correlated with
less productive profiles of epistemological beliefs. In essence, overly well-struc-
tured tasks can theoretically curtail self-regulation (Doyle, 1983), depress learning
and interfere with transfer (Spiro et al., 1988), limit self-determination (Pintrich
et al., 1993), and lower performance (Schommer, 1990, 1993), particularly in
students who are learning new content and have low domain knowledge (Kanfer
& Ackerman, 1989).
For these reasons, some researchers (e.g., Blumenfeld et al., 1991; Doyle, 1983;
Frederiksen, 1984; Spiro et al., 1988) are concerned about the high proportion of
overly well-structured tasks (like repetitive recall worksheets emphasising low-level
facts) in school. These researchers observe that people are much more likely to face
ill-structured tasks outside school. However, overcompensating by assigning too
many ill-structured tasks in school might also prove counterproductive. Students’
anxiety levels climb in the face of such ambiguous and cognitively challenging tasks
(Butler & Winne, 1995; Doyle, 1983). A balance of tasks seems necessary to facili-
tate more cognitive activity and conceptual change in students.

Research Questions and Hypotheses
Despite some research signalling that students with differing profiles of epistemolog-
ical beliefs vary in thinking, reasoning, use of strategies, and learning while working
on tasks (e.g., Kitchener & King, 1981; Schommer & Dunnell, 1992), relations
between epistemological beliefs and school orientation (religiosity), levels of
academic achievement, gender, reflective judgments, and more fine-grained instruc-
tional features like the structure of specific academic tasks have yet to be discovered.
The aim of this study is to illuminate any such relations via the following seven
specific inquiries using data collected in a regular school setting. 
1. An exploratory factor analysis of SMEQ items was undertaken, and the dimen-
sions found are reported.
2. Since there is no known research addressing differences in epistemological
beliefs according to school orientation (independent-religious and public
schools), such results are described.
3. As tested in previous research (e.g., Kardash & Scholes, 1996; Schommer,
1990, 1993; Schommer et al., 1992), the hypothesis that epistemological belief
scores should predict students’ overall academic achievement is assessed.
4. A study of gifted high school students (Schommer & Dunnell, 1997) has shown
links between fixed ability beliefs and academic performance; however, evidence
relating epistemological beliefs to students’ levels of overall academic achieve-
ment (high, moderate, or low) is still scant. In the fourth area of inquiry, a
strong hypothesis is waived in favour of a descriptive exploration of potential
differences in epistemological beliefs according to levels of overall academic
achievement.
5. As articulated in the theoretical review, a learner’s profile of epistemological
beliefs may differ as a function of the characteristics of different learning tasks.
This is examined. It is hypothesised that epistemological beliefs will have a
stronger predictive relationship with students’ performance on an ill-structured
task than on a well-structured task. This hypothesis is justified by research (e.g.,
Schommer, 1990; Schommer at al., 1992; Schraw et al., 1995) discovering rela-
tions between epistemological beliefs in students and their persistence, engage-
ment, and coping skills in ill-defined domains. Since ill-structured tasks are
probably perceived by students to be more cognitively demanding than tasks
that are highly structured, naïve (non-availing) epistemological beliefs, particu-
larly perceptions that ability is fixed and that knowledge is simple, are expected
to have a stronger mediating and predictive relationship with performance on a
loosely structured task than a more highly structured task.
6. Since an ill-structured task demands that students judge reflectively, achieve
academically (King & Kitchener, 1994, 2002), and argue persuasively (Kuhn,
1991), this study endeavours to illuminate further the relations between reflective
judgment and achievement on an ill-structured task by testing the assertion that
epistemological beliefs and levels of overall academic achievement will predict
students’ reflective judgment scores on an ill-structured task. For example,

Method
Procedure and Participants
Combined sample.   To derive a statistically robust factor structure for subsequent
analyses, a sample of 447 volunteer students (79%) from 10th grade science classes
in six secondary schools (five public and one independent) in a large city in western
British Columbia completed a slightly modified version of Schommer’s (1993)
Modified Epistemological Questionnaire for high school students (SMEQ). They
also completed a demographic questionnaire (DQ), in which they reported estimates
of their academic average in the 9th and 10th grades. The first author and the class-
room teacher administered each measure during regular class time. The sample
included 219 males (49%) and 228 females (51%) from lower to upper middle-class
socio-economic levels; the mean age was 15.3 years; 85% were Caucasian, 9%
Asian-Canadian, and 6% other.
Public school sub-sample.   Of the 447 students in the combined sample, 358 were
enrolled in public schools. These included 174 males (48.6%) and 184 females
(51.4%), from lower to upper middle-class socio-economic levels; the mean age was
15.2 years; 86% of these students were Caucasian, 7.5% Asian-Canadian, and 6.4%
other.
Independent school sub-sample.   During regular school hours, 94 students (included in
the combined sample) from all four 10th grade science classes at an independent (reli-
gious) secondary school completed the same questionnaires (SMEQ, DQ), and also
volunteered to complete two differently structured tasks (and another questionnaire
not used in this study) as reported in Lodewyk and Winne (2005). A pre-requisite to
enrolment in this school was adherence to the Protestant-Christian religious faith. All
of the students in each of the classes agreed to participate in the study, although two
students were excluded because of a lack of class attendance and three were dismissed

Measures
Overall academic achievement.   All students reported their estimates of overall
academic achievement (EOAA) on the DQ, which was designed by the first author.
It consisted of 14 brief fill-in-the-blank questions asking students to report their
name, age, gender, residence, any disability, study habits, and their EOAA in the 9th
and 10th grades (the DQ was administered in late March). Students were categor-
ised into high, moderate, and low groups according to their EOAA to ensure rela-
tively equal sample size and variance for the high and low groups. Students in the
high EOAA group ( n = 124) reported grades above 80% while those in the low
EOAA group (n = 124) reported grades below 70%. The moderate EOAA group ( n
= 199) comprised the remaining students.
To test the concurrent validity of the EOAA, independent school students’ actual
overall academic achievement (OAA) in Grade 9 and Grade 10 was provided by the
school. Then an overall weighted score was computed to reflect achievement over
time, taking 40% of their previous year’s overall academic average and 60% of the
mean of their two most recent academic terms. The means for students’ reported
EOAA and calculated OAA were 75.66 (8.90) and 74.43 (8.81); the Pearson corre-
lation coefficient between them was .91. This coefficient provides ample leverage for
using EOAA as a valid measure of academic achievement.
Epistemological beliefs.   Students’ epistemological beliefs were assessed using
Schommer’s (1993; Schommer et al., 1992, 1997; Schommer & Dunnell, 1997; see
also Qian & Alvermann, 1995) SMEQ for high school students. This questionnaire
is a self-report instrument also previously used to assess college students’ responses
to 63 reflective statements about knowledge and learning (e.g., Enman & Lupart,
2000; Jeung, Johnston & Anderson, 1993; Kardash & Howell, 2000; Schommer,
1990; Wood & Kardash, 2002). Schommer’s MEQ and SMEQ (or variations
thereof) are the most commonly used self-report measures (Hofer, 2005).

Students rate themselves on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly
disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5). Some of the items were: “To be successful in
school, it is best not to ask too many questions,” “You can believe almost everything
you read,” “Once a person is born, their ability to learn will not change,” and
“Successful students understand things quickly.” Schommer (1990, 1993; Schommer
et al., 1992, 1997; Schommer & Dunnell, 1997) clustered items into 12 subsets
reflecting degrees of student naïveté about seeking single answers, avoiding integration
and ambiguity, criticising authority, learning how to learn, and succeeding from hard
work and concentrated effort.
Previous studies using the SMEQ (e.g., Schommer, 1993; Schommer et al., 1997)
report moderate test–retest reliability (.74), internal consistency coefficients for
subsets in each factor of .63–.85, and reasonable predictive validity. For instance,
several studies (Schommer, 1993; Schommer et al., 1992, 1997) demonstrate strong
stability in the simple knowledge factor and consistent subset loading onto hypothe-
sised factors from several subsets. Despite its popularity, however, the factor struc-
ture of the SMEQ frequently deviates from the pattern of loadings Schommer
(1990, 1993) originally hypothesised. This may be because of contextual differences
and covariance among items (Hofer, 2001; Hofer & Pintrich, 1997). To account for
these concerns, an exploratory factor analysis of items was administered to delineate
the major dimensions of the factor structure within the combined sample.
Results of a pilot study with 24 10th-grade science students from a second inde-
pendent school were used to modify the SMEQ for the age and comprehension level
of students in this study. For example, some statements were changed to enable
10th-grade students to comprehend and answer the items more easily. Words
like “professor,” “ultimately,” “intent,” and “original” were replaced with “teacher,”
“eventually,” “purpose,” and “creative.” The wording of some other questions was
changed to reduce some anticipated cultural differences for the Canadian sample
used in this study. For example, “Nothing is certain, but death and taxes” was
replaced by, “We can’t be sure of anything, except that we will die someday.” One
question (“A clean desk is a sign of an empty mind”) was omitted because the major-
ity of students reported difficulty comprehending it even after it was revised. An addi-
tional 10 items that were theorised by Schommer (1989) to load on the omniscient
authority factor were also deleted from subsequent analyses, as in previous research
(Qian & Alvermann, 1995), because the factor has not been consistently identified in
previous research (Schommer, 1990, 1993; Schommer et al., 1992).

room settings (Blumenfeld et al., 1991; Doyle, 1983; Frederiksen, 1984; Spiro et al.,
1988), and cues inherent in each task’s structure were varied for students to self-
regulate their learning and performance. Each task was also purposely structured

resources were given to students in pre-packaged files, and grading criteria was

In contrast, the ill-structured task was entitled “Task-Force Issue” and was
designed to stimulate more critical thinking through argumentation about whether
government funding for cancer should focus more on preventative efforts or treat-
ment programs. In essay form, students had to justify how the government should
respond to cancer based on perceived priorities for battling it. This task was struc-
tured to require students to address a more ambiguous and complex cognitive prob-
lem, and engage in more reflective judgment (King & Kitchener, 2002) and
epistemological reflection (Baxter Magolda, 2002). It included fewer instructional
cues, required students to find their own resources in the classroom, and had less
detail and evaluative criteria in the outline for judging progress. For more informa-
tion about these tasks, please see Lodewyk and Winne (2005).
Learning task performance.   Performance on the well-structured and ill-structured
tasks was assessed using a composite mark based on the grade assigned by the first
author using a scoring rubric (weighted .90) combined with scores on a 10-item
multiple-choice quiz (weighted .10) that students completed after each task. The
well-structured task was graded according to how well students fulfilled require-
ments outlined in the rubric provided in their original task descriptions. To grade
the ill-structured task, the essay scoring criteria from the British Columbia Ministry
of Education’s History 12 Examination Specifications  (1998) was used. This rubric
was not shown to students. Each task was also graded by the classroom teacher. The
Pearson correlation between graders (first author and classroom teacher) was .88 for
the ill-structured project and .93 for the well-structured project.

reflective judgment (RJ) based on a grade given independently by the first author. RJ
was assessed with close consideration of King and Kitchener’s (1994) seven levels of
reflective judgment on ill-structured problems. Pre-reflective learners are in Stages
1, 2, or 3. In Stage 1, learners do not articulate the complexities within an issue (for

Results
Factor Analyses
To discern the factor structure of epistemological beliefs (EB) within the overall sample,
a principal axis exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation and a minimum eigen-
value of .98 was performed on 52 of 63 MEQ items (justified earlier), extracting 18
factors that accounted for 36.45% of the variance. The internal consistency coefficient
for the SMEQ was .67. The first three extracted factors—fixed and quick ability to learn
(FQAL), simple knowledge (SK), and certain knowledge (CK)—accounted for
16.81% of the variance (see Table 1). Fixed and quick ability to learn (7.49% of the vari-
ance, eigenvalue = 3.89), simple knowledge (6.51%, eigenvalue = 3.38), and certain
knowledge (2.82%, eigenvalue = 1.47) had internal consistency reliability coefficients
of .65, .51, and .73 respectively. Pearson correlation coefficients ( r) between factors
were: FQAL and SK .08, FQAL and CK .04, and SK and CK −.002. Content validity
for these three dimensions of epistemological beliefs is evident through their consistent
emergence in previous research with secondary school students (e.g., Qian & Alver-
mann, 1995; Schommer, 1993; Schommer et al., 1992, 1997; Schommer & Dunnell,
1997). These psychometric properties were deemed suitable to justify the three primary
factors being used in subsequent analyses within this study.

Discussion
The intentions for this study were to investigate the SMEQ factor structure, differ-
ences in epistemological beliefs between public and independent (religious) school
samples, and relations between epistemological beliefs and gender, overall academic
achievement, reflective judgment, and performances on an ill-structured and a well-
structured task. It was specifically posited that epistemological beliefs would have a
stronger predictive relationship with performance on an ill-structured task than on a
well-structured task, that epistemological beliefs would relate to students’ reflective
judgment scores, and that girls would achieve significantly higher grades and perfor-
mance scores on both tasks while believing less in quick learning and fixed ability
than boys.
To increase the validity of the SMEQ for use in this study, the recommenda-
tions of other researchers (e.g., Qian & Alvermann, 1995; Schommer, 1993)
were followed and the main factors resulting from an exploratory factor analysis
of items were used. The main factors to emerge were fixed and quick ability to
learn (FQAL), simple knowledge (SK), and certain knowledge (CK). The emer-
gence of the FQAL factor lends support to assertions (e.g., Hofer, 2000, 2005)
that quick learning and fixed ability may be metacognitive processes generalis-
able to, or independent from, other more epistemological dimensions. Hofer
(2000, 2005) and others (e.g., Hofer & Pintrich, 1997; Pintrich, 2002) explain
that beliefs about the speed of learning and about the incremental nature of
intelligence are important constructs related to learning, but are not part of a
philosophical understanding of a learner’s epistemology, which comprises beliefs
about knowledge and knowing. Schommer-Aikins (2004) has recently distin-
guished beliefs about knowing from beliefs about learning. While the debate
about whether beliefs about learning and intelligence constitute part of personal
epistemology centred on the nature of knowledge and knowing continues to be a
worthy one, this study reveals that beliefs about intelligence and the speed of
learning (FQAL) and knowledge (SK and CK) are linked to several constructs

In this study, students from the Protestant-Christian school had more sophisti-
cated epistemological beliefs overall, and beliefs about certain knowledge in particu-
lar, than students from the public schools. This may contradict other research (e.g.,
Dale, 1995; Kitchener & King, 1981) that has linked students’ beliefs in knowledge
as a revelation from God or “absolute authority-based knowledge” (p. 113) with
weaker reflective judgment. Since many students in the public school sample are
also probably religious, another explanation may be that the curricula of the inde-
pendent school or the learning environments in the homes of these students may
stimulate more critical thinking, thereby fostering more availing epistemological
beliefs. Perhaps more students in such schools have acquired a superior capability to
coordinate multiple and contrasting views relative to context (Hofer, 2001). More
research into the causal nature of such differences according to school orientation
would be welcomed.
Students’ overall epistemological beliefs and beliefs in FQAL and SK predicted
estimates of overall academic achievement, which corroborates other research on epis-
temological beliefs and achievement (Boyes & Chandler, 1992; Qian & Alvermann,
1995; Schommer, 1993; Schommer et al., 1992; Wood & Kardash, 2002). The find-
ing that beliefs in FQAL predicted academic achievement in this study is noteworthy,
since beliefs about innate ability—while having been linked to certain goal orienta-
tions, choice of tasks, and cognitive operations—have not been strongly linked to
students’ academic performance (Qian & Alvermann, 1995) other than in gifted
students (Schommer & Dunnell, 1997).
Epistemological beliefs, particularly beliefs related to FQAL and SK, were signifi-
cantly more naïve in lower than higher academically achieving students. Other
research (e.g., Hofer, 1999; Mason & Boscolo, 2002) has linked non-availing episte-
mological beliefs to various indices of academic achievement—each being part of the
tapestry of academic success and helping explain why more availing beliefs in FQAL
and SK are held by higher achieving students in this study. Epistemological beliefs
may mediate academic learning and performance indirectly, along with other
constructs. For example, students who view ability as fixed also have much lower
levels of perceived self-efficacy in problem solving, and potentially lower subsequent
academic performances (Bandura, 1993).
The determinant strength of epistemological beliefs on task performance was
significant for the ill-structured task but not for the well-structured task. This lends
some support to other research addressing similar instructional design queries (e.g.,
Schraw et al., 1995). When tasks are structured loosely to increase their complexity,
this may influence task performance by activating epistemic motivations that medi-
ate psychological operations for engaging cognitively in accessing information,
developing and testing new theories, persevering through discrepancies, and seeking
appropriate closure (Kruglanski, 1990). In instruction, teachers should monitor the
cognitive complexity and motivational cost of learning tasks, along with students’
beliefs that ability is fixed, that knowledge is simply an accumulation of isolated

facts, that integration is not necessary, and that learning is accomplished the first
time. The type of structure of assigned tasks also merits attention if, as Spiro et al.
(1988) conjecture, assigning too many well-structured tasks can promote a false
understanding that the domain is fundamentally simple and can stimulate superficial
engagement. However, balance is important—assigning especially ambiguous tasks
may induce anxiety and withdrawal (Doyle, 1983).
The finding that belief in simple knowledge had statistical links to both perfor-
mance and reflective judgment scores on the ill-structured task corroborates other
research relating capabilities for reflective judgment to performance on ill-structured
tasks (King & Kitchener, 1994, 2002). Belief in simple knowledge appears to have a
particularly influential role on performance on ill-structured tasks, perhaps because
of its mediating effect on reflective judgment ability. On the basis of these results, it
seems that students who see knowledge as simple—that is, they seek single answers,
avoid ambiguity, and depend too much on authority—tend to experience more diffi-
culty with the ambiguous features of tasks that call for reflective judgments, perse-
verance, and appropriate self-regulated learning. Viewing knowledge as simple can
limit subsequent conceptual change and performance in particular academic
contexts (Qian & Alvermann, 1995; Schommer & Dunnell, 1997). For instance,
Schommer et al. (1992) found that simple knowledge predicted test performance on
differently structured maths problems; it was negatively associated with comprehen-
sion and metacomprehension, and was mediated by study strategy.
Roth and Roychoudhury (1994) hypothesised that since knowledge structures,
epistemological assumptions, and tasks differ across domains, it may be prudent to
relate epistemological beliefs to specific contexts rather than to descriptions of
student’s general views. At certain ages, epistemological beliefs may be too global or
unidimensional to differ substantially across achievement levels and tasks, or they
may be more elusive to current measurement instruments. Winne and Hadwin
(1998) suggest that individual differences in epistemological beliefs that influence
perceptions of learning tasks may transcend tasks, thus rendering them unresponsive
to task conditions. However, Mason and Boscolo (2004) reported that high school
students with more availing epistemological beliefs altered their topic-specific beliefs
more than those with moderate or less availing beliefs. Useful research contributions
could include developing and implementing more task-specific and domain-specific
measures for assessing epistemological beliefs in fine-grained learning contexts, and
researching links between epistemological beliefs, academic learning, and perfor-
mance on very specific types of learning tasks.
Finally, this investigation reported gender differences in epistemological beliefs.
As predicted, boys believed more naïvely than girls that one’s ability is predeter-
mined, that learning occurs quickly, and that knowledge is certain. Epistemological
beliefs such as these could handicap boys’ engagement in learning and performance,
and may partially account for their low performance relative to girls on both
tasks and their lower overall academic achievement. However, Pintrich (2002) noted
that male–female biological status may not sufficiently explain group differences in
epistemological thinking because it may mask confounding relations with other

In conclusion, previous research in the educational and psychological literature has
highlighted the asynchronous state of the current conceptualisation of epistemologi-
cal beliefs while proliferating uncertainties and revealing numerous additional needs
for study. The results of this study illuminate important relations surrounding the
nature, assessment, and specificity of epistemological beliefs. Refinements in current
(e.g., Schommer-Aikins et al., 2002) and new (e.g., Buehl et al., 2002; Schraw,
Bendixen, & Dunkle, 2002) measures of epistemological beliefs should stimulate
increasingly favourable reliability of the construct and its dimensions that could
further clarify relationships between its developmental nature and specificity accord-
ing to age, domain, and instructional context—particularly in relation to learning
tasks, social influences, and the worldviews and beliefs of parents and teachers.
