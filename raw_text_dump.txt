An emerging donor in education and development: A case study of China in
Cameroon
Bjørn H. Nordtveit
University of Hong Kong, Faculty of Education, Pokfulam Road, Hong Kong, Hong Kong
1. Introduction
The aim of this article is to discuss China’s emergence as an aid
donor in Africa, using its educational cooperation with Cameroon
as a case study. China has been widely analyzed for its economic
growth and visibility as an investor in the developing world from
the time Deng Xiaoping started the reforms and opening-up
policies. However, there has been less examination of China’s new
role as an aid donor. 1 Cameroon is an interesting case to exemplify
China’s engagement with Africa, since it has had a well-established
diplomatic relationship with China for nearly 40 years, and the
educational cooperation between the two countries possesses
many characteristics that are helpful for the understanding of
China’s policies in Africa.
Research on China’s education aid to Africa is different from
researching other bilateral education aid, and its methodological
challenges are substantial. Contrary to many other bilateral
donors, there is no Chinese centralized institution responsible
for international aid. A Department of Foreign Aid in China’s
Ministry of Commerce is the main coordinator for aid, and
additionally, international units with elements of the external aid
budget are connected to the Ministry of Education and several
other ministries. Therefore, in China there is not a single uniﬁed
development agency that can be researched ( Davies et al., 2008;
King, 2006 ). Despite the encouragement of the China–Africa
Summit of 3–5 November 2006 in Beijing (FOCAC) to provide
increased public access to essential data about Sino-African
cooperation and aid, the documentation on Chinese aid to Africa
remains scarce ( Davies et al., 2008; Gill et al., 2007 ).
To better understand China’s development strategies, this
article will ﬁrst provide a historic overview on Sino-African
cooperation in education. Then, in a second section, it will explore
China’s bilateral educational cooperation with Cameroon. In
particular, Chinese language education, school construction,
capacity building and higher education research cooperation will
be investigated. In a third section, the article will survey China’s
general cooperation policies in Cameroon and also present how
government education staff, teachers, and education managers
perceive them.
Key facets of Chinese educational aid to Africa can be found in
the action plan from the Third and Fourth Forum of Sino-African
Cooperation (FOCAC). The aid covers four main areas, including the
provision of scholarships; setting up of rural schools; training of
educational ofﬁcials; and establishment of Confucius Institutes to
teach the Chinese language ( FOCAC, 2006; FOCAC, 2009a,b ).
Further, China has dispatched Chinese teachers to various African
countries, has established research cooperation with a number of
African Universities, and conversely also has established research
centers on Africa in a number of Chinese Universities ( He, 2006). A

ABSTRACT
This paper analyzes China’s new approaches of education aid to Africa through a case study of Cameroon.
China’s cooperation has been characterized by different discourses and different historic relationships
with recipient countries than those of traditional donors. Sino-African policies have gone through
different stages, each connected to wider political and economic realities. Currently, a broadening of
China’s engagement with Africa can be noticed through increasing aid packages and the inclusion of
African countries other than those that had traditionally been close to China. Cameroon has been one of
the countries that have had a relatively extended cooperation with China. Four types of Chinese
education aid to Africa are illustrated by this paper: (i) Confucius Institutes, which are providing
language and culture-related training in host countries; (ii) longer term scholarships and short-term
training for Africans in China; (iii) school construction; and (iv) stand-alone education projects. By
analyzing how these three different types of aid are taking place in Cameroon, various and apparently
contradictory strategies and discourses materialize.

Fourth Forum on Sino-African Cooperation took place in Sharm El
Sheikh, Egypt, in the end of 2009. Rather than changing the nature
of China’s policies towards Africa, the outcome of the new forum
was said by Chinese ofﬁcials (who I interviewed in Egypt prior to
the conference) to represent a ‘‘consolidation’’ of the current 3-
year phase. The outcome of the conference is summarized in the
Declaration and Action Plans of the Sino-African Summit in Sharm
El Sheikh, which purpose was to deepen ‘‘the new type of China–
Africa Strategic Partnership for sustainable development’’ ( FOCAC,
2009a, p. 1). The orientations of the Chinese education and
development policies in Cameroon (and in other countries in
Africa), in view of the outcomes of the fourth forum, are unlikely to
undergo major changes over the next few years.
1.1. Methodology
This article is based on a research project funded by The
Research Grants Council of Hong Kong (RGC) to study China’s
educational cooperation with Africa. In order to investigate
China’s resource bases for its African cooperation in education, I
visited the Institute for African Studies at Zhejiang Normal
University as well as aid- and cooperation-related institutions
and organizations in Beijing in October 2008. I also conducted 2
weeks of ﬁeldwork in Egypt in April 2009, during which I
collected information about Sino-Egyptian cooperation in
education, as well as on the Forth Forum on Sino-African
Cooperation that was to take place in Sharm El Sheikh in
November 2009. Fieldwork in Cameroon was conducted in
January and February 2009, and resulted in 31 interviews. During
ﬁeldwork in Cameroon I conducted on-site interviews and group
discussions, and consulted key informants, both Chinese and
Cameroonian, with special knowledge about Sino-Cameroonian
cooperation in education. The interviews were based on
stratiﬁed sampling in an attempt to cover all aspects of Sino-
Cameroonian cooperation in education, as follows: Confucius
Institute teachers (four individual interviews); Chinese language
students (11 students interviewed in two focus group settings
and two individual interviews), professors and administrative
personnel involved in Sino-African projects (three individual
interviews), personnel from the Cameroonian Scholarship
Commission (three individual interviews) and from the Coop-
eration Unit for Chinese and Japanese Projects (four individual
interviews), as well as personnel from the Chinese Embassy (two
individual interviews). Further, representatives from UNESCO
(one interview), the Non-Governmental Network for Education
For All (one individual interview), the Prime Minister’s Ofﬁce
(one interview), and the Coordination Section at the Ministry of
Higher Education (one interview). I conducted the interviews in
English and French, and later transcribed them into English.
Finally, I used a combination of methods to explore the case,
including coding and analysis of interviews (using NVivo
software) as well as the examination of various documentation
collected in Cameroon, China and Egypt.
2. A historic perspective of Sino-African cooperation in
education
Contemporary China’s development policies towards Africa
have gone through different stages, each connected to wider
political and economic realities. I have used a phasing of the
educational engagement inspired by Prof. He Wenping at
the Institute of West Asian and African Studies (IWAAS) at the
Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (CASS). According to He
(2006), China’s relations with Africa can be divided into three
successive periods. I have labeled these three phases according to
the main Chinese diplomatic preoccupations for Sino-African
educational cooperation. Hence, the ﬁrst period (1950–1989) can
be seen as Sino-African cooperation with countries that are
aligned with China’s foreign policy and its socialist government
philosophy ( Tu, 2009). A second phase (1989–2000) was initiated
with the events of Tiananmen Square in June 1989, and China’s
search for a broader political support in view of the country’s
relative isolation and criticism by the West. I have named the
third phase according to the increasing economic relationships
between China and Africa, and argue that presently, China’s
economic preoccupations bypass its traditional political align-
ments. It should be noted that I have used these labels for clarity.
Political, security and commercial interests characterize all three
periods, although at different intensity. As noted by Kwezi Kwaa
Prah, the Director of the Centre for Advanced Studies of African
Society (Cape Town):
I wouldn’t put it [the Sino-African relationship] in such
polarized terms that at one end of the scale it is ideological
and at the other it’s all economic. I think what one can say is that
it was preponderantly articulated and expressed in ideological
terms in the earlier period. It also had economic dimensions but
this was hardly pronounced. Now it is distinctly more
pronounced as China tries to search for markets and also for
raw materials ( Prah and Burnett, 2007 , p. 59).
An example of political interference in an otherwise economic-
oriented document can be found, for example, in the recent
Declaration of Sharm El Sheikh of the Forum on China–Africa
Cooperation, in which the signatories ‘‘African countries reiterate
their commitment to the one China policy and support for China’s
peaceful reuniﬁcation [with Taiwan]’’ ( FOCAC, 2009a , p. 4).
2.1. Period of political alignments (1950–1989)
During the period of political alignments (1950–1989), China’s
educational support to Africa was limited to the provision of
scholarships to African students and to the dispatch of teachers to
Africa to teach Chinese and basic educational courses such as
mathematics, chemistry and physics. The period can be sub-
divided into different phases, each corresponding to a different
alignment of China’s foreign policy. In the 1950s, China, with a
pro-USSR and anti-US international policy, supported African
communist parties and encouraged their struggle for indepen-
dence ( Li, 2006 ). During the Bandung Conference in April 1955
between Asian and African states to promote Afro-Asian political,
economic and cultural cooperation, the initial steps for a closer
relationship between China and African countries were taken. The
ﬁrst agreements of cooperation were signed with Algeria, Egypt,
Guinea, Morocco and Sudan. In particular, China’s dialogue with
Egypt (one of the organizers of the Bandung conference – and later
a main player in the Non-Aligned Movement) led to the beginning
of an educational cooperation between the two countries. As a
ﬁrst modest step in 1956, four Egyptian students came to China to
study and four Egyptian scholars went to China to lecture, while
one English teacher and seven Chinese students were dispatched
to Cairo ( He, 2006 ).
In the 1960s, China engaged in a double policy of anti-
revisionism and anti-imperialism, which led to a disruption of its
relationships with some of the pro-USSR communist parties in
Africa (e.g., South Africa). The period of the Cultural Revolution
(1966–1976) coincided with a further isolation of China, and led to
the breaking of diplomatic relationships with several African
countries. The deterioration of diplomatic relationships had a
direct and negative inﬂuence on educational cooperation: from
1967 to 1972, China did not receive African students ( He, 2006 ).
However, President Nixon’s 1972 visit to China, which was

2.2. Africa as a source of political support (1989–2000)
A second phase of educational cooperation, I argue, started in
the 1990s. More speciﬁcally, this second phase of cooperation
was initially linked to the violent events surrounding the
Tiananmen Square in June 1989. 3 It should be underlined that
the Tiananmen movement was inspired by the perestroika
reforms introduced in June 1987 by the Soviet leader Mikhail
Gorbachev. In 1989, China’s relative isolation in the face of
intense criticism from the West – coinciding with silence from
various African countries – arguably led to the Chinese
government’s realization that Africa was a useful and manage-
able source of international support ( Taylor, 2006 ). Many African
leaders perhaps felt threatened by the West’s post-Cold War
discourse on human rights and democracy. In this, they found a
degree of common ground with Beijing. The political events of
1989 thus provide the backdrop of the increasing cooperation
between China and Africa in the 1990s, leading to the doubling of
African students in China and the number of Chinese teachers in
Africa. This boost was not only due to growing assistance to
Africa from China: the number of self-funded African students
studying in China increased from two in 1989 to 1580 in 1999
(He, 2006 ). During this period, Chinese and African Universities
also increasingly sought to develop common research programs
(He, 2006 ). Gradually, the need for Africa as a source of political
support was complemented with the need of Africa as a source of
natural resources ( Taylor, 2006 ).
2.3. Increasing economic relationship (2000–present)
Presently, China is focused on the development of its
international economic ties rather than on a diplomacy serving
ideology (Tu, 2009). As Foreign Minister Yang Jiechi said on March
2009: ‘‘The focus of our diplomatic work should be ... to directly
serve the economy ... Diplomacy should not be a zero-sum game,
but one that strives to create win–win situations’’ ( Xinhua, March
7, 2009). The ﬁrst Forum on China–Africa Cooperation (FOCAC) in
October 2000 increased the pace of this economic cooperation. The
forum, according to China’s Africa policy statement ( FOCAC, 2006),
has the aim to become a mechanism for dialogue and cooperation
between China and Africa:
Launched in 2000, the Forum on China–Africa Cooperation has
become an effective mechanism for the collective dialogue
and multilateral cooperation between China and Africa and
put in place an important framework and platform for a new
type of China–Africa partnershi p featuring long-term stability,
equality and mutual beneﬁt. ... China will work with African
countries within the framework of the Forum to explore new
ways to enhance mutual political trust, promote the compre-
hensive development of pragmatic cooperation, further
improve the mechanism of the forum, and try to ﬁnd the
best way for furthering cooperation between the Forum and
the NEPAD [New Partnership for Africa’s Development]
(FOCAC, 2006 ,p .1 ) .
Similar fora have (since 2000) been conducted every third
year – in Africa and China alternatively, and according to
interviewees (both in Beijing and in Cameroon), functions in lieu
of a more permanent cooperation structure. A second forum was
held in Addis Ababa in December 2003, and a third forum was
convened in Beijing in November 2006, receiving unprecedented
media attention because of the presence in Beijing of a
considerable number of African leaders. This third forum was
marked by an acceleration of the cooperation between China
and Africa, as outlined in an Action Plan that provided a list of
Sino-African aid and cooperation strategies, including a con-
siderable development of the ﬁeld of human resource building
and support to education in Africa ( FOCAC, 2006 ). For human
resources development, it was claimed that following the 2004–
2006 Sino-African Inter-governmental Human Resources Devel-
opment Plan (adopted at the Second Forum), China had trained
over 10,000 African professionals in various ﬁelds under its
African Human Resources Development Fund (AHRDF). The
Third Forum’s Action Plan foresaw the continued training of an
additional 15,000 professionals and management personnel for
African countries ( FOCAC, 2006 ). In terms of education, the
Chinese Government would (i) help African countries set up 100
rural schools over the next 3 years; (ii) increase the number of
the Chinese government’s scholarships to African students from
the current 2000 per year to 4000 per year by 2009; (iii) provide
in-service training for educational ofﬁcials and teachers of
universities, primary, secondary and vocational schools in
Africa; and (iv) establish Confucius Institutes in African
countries (
FOCAC, 2006 ). The fourth forum took place in Sharm
El Sheikh, Egypt, on 8–9 November 2009, claimed that the prior
phase had successfully achieved its targets, and can in many
ways be seen as the continuation and consolidation of the third
forum. The action plan established by the conference included
the building of 50 China–Africa friendship schools, the set up of
a new type of cooperation between universities, continue
scholarships and other human capacity building programs,
and continue the implementation of Confucius Institutes
(FOCAC, 2009b ).

3. Cameroon’s educational cooperation with China
China’s policies towards Cameroon have gone through
different stages, each connected to wider Sino-African and
international political and economic realities, as outlined in the
ﬁrst section above. Cameroon, with a population of 18.5 million, of
which 40% are under the poverty line, has modest oil resources
and favorable agricultural conditions. The country severed its ties
with the Taiwanese authorities and established a diplomatic
relationship with China on 26 March 1971 ( MOFA, 2003 ). A trade
agreement was set up with Cameroon in 1972 – and a number of
high-level visits took place in the 1970s. Medical aid (in the form
of a number of doctors sent to Cameroon) started in 1975 and,
according to the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs ( MOFA, 2003 ,
p. 1), a policy of ‘‘mutual-beneﬁt cooperation between China and
Cameroon started from 1982.’’ The education cooperation
between the two countries became increasingly important since
the 1990s, with the establishment of a Chinese Language Training
Center in 1996 (transformed into a Confucius Center in 2007) at
the University of Yaounde´ II. The Institute has observed a growing
interest for Chinese studies among Cameroonian students, with
an increasing number of enrollments every year. Likewise, a
steadily growing number of applications for scholarships given
from China started to take off in the 1990s. Before that,
scholarships were few, and correspondingly few Cameroonians
were interested in pursuing their studies in China. In 2009, China
emerges as the most important donor of scholarships (40 new
scholarships in 2009), largely because students are allowed to
attend training from bachelor level and upwards, whereas most
DAC donors focus on Post-Doctoral seminars and training. One
reason for this difference is related to China’s visa and immigra-
tion policies, which are very different from Western countries’
policies. As noted by a Chinese Embassy staff member interviewed
during ﬁeldwork, ‘‘our visa policy is not very strict as compared to
France or the USA. People coming to China are not considered as
illegal immigrants but as friendly ambassadors.’’ Likewise, the
training both in the Confucius Institute in Yaounde ´ and in China
(through scholarships given to Cameroonians) has, according to
interviewees, two distinct functions: the training is related to
resource building and to cultural sensitizing about China
(‘‘building friendship’’). Four types of Chinese education aid to
Africa will be illustrated below, following the plan outlined by
FOCAC (2006): Confucius Institutes, which are providing language
and culture-related training in host countries; scholarships for
Cameroonians in China; school construction, and stand-alone
education projects.

3.1. Chinese language training
The Confucius Institute (CI) in Yaounde ´ was inaugurated in
2007. Originally it was a Chinese Language Training Center that
started up in 1996 as a cooperation project between a higher
education institution in China, more precisely the Zhejiang Normal
University, and in Cameroon, the International Relations Institute
of Cameroon (IRIC), attached to University of Yaounde ´ II. The
Chinese non-governmental organization (NGO) Hanban 6 ofﬁcially
took over the administration of the center on 9 November 2007.
Originally the center focused on training students at IRIC for which
Chinese was an optional language, together with Arabic and
Spanish. The center also started to enroll other interested learners,
in addition to its commitment to Yaounde´ II students, when it was
converted into a Confucius Institute (CI).
Since 2007, the CI has been very busy because the ﬁve full-time
staff members (two Chinese teachers, two African teachers, and
one administrative assistant), in addition to the language teaching,
also (in the terms of a teacher) need to organize ‘‘colorful events to
spread information about China’s culture.’’ As an example, I
attended a Chinese karaoke competition for African students that
celebrated the Chinese Spring Festival. Other events include a
knowledge game labeled a ‘‘Bridge competition,’’ which involves
all of the Confucius Institutes worldwide. It consists of three parts,
questioning participants on their knowledge to China’s culture,
language, and on a speciﬁc subject chosen every year. For example,
in 2008, the speciﬁc theme was the Olympics. Also, a Chinese club
meets every Wednesday afternoon to organize cultural activities,
such as paper cutting, watching Chinese movies, learning Chinese
songs, and discussing in Chinese. On Saturday mornings a Tai Chi
class is organized.
The students from the public can attend three levels of Chinese
language learning, whereas the IRIC students study the ﬁrst
semester, and then continue during a second year, also for the ﬁrst
semester. Further, since the IRIC is a center with sub-regional
aspiration, the CI spreads interest for the Chinese language beyond
Cameroon. According to a teacher, ‘‘the students in this [diplo-
matic] course come from various countries in the subregion,
including Gabon, Central Africa, Chad and other countries.’’ In
general, according to interviewees (and supported by enrollment
statistics), the interest for Chinese language is growing, and reﬂects
the shift from an ideology-based interest in China’s language and
civilization towards an economic interest. This change ﬁts with the
larger and more generalized periods of Sino-African educational
cooperation outlined by He in the ﬁrst section above, i.e., the shift
from political to economic cooperation noticed around 2000:
We had very few learners in the beginning [1996]. Only four
students were enrolled during the ﬁrst year, of which two were
girls, and four students during the second year. After that, the
number of students improved. ... There is a very strong demand
for learning Chinese now, more than 50 students per year.
... The students say that there are a lot of Chinese businesses
here, and that they would like to facilitate the Chinese
businesses by working as translators. Also, they believe that
China is a super power in competition with the USA. Other
students would like to do business with China (Interview with a
CI teacher).
Interviews with students conﬁrmed their interest in ‘‘doing
business with China,’’ or working as translators. After studying at

3.2. Scholarships and human resource building
The scholarship cooperation with China took off in 1995.
Before that, Cameroon rarely received scholarships, but as noted
by a staff of the Scholarship Commission, ‘‘at that point China
emerged, the communist area took an end, and people started to
get interested.’’ The fact that China performed so well economic-
ally stimulated a lot of interest. As noted by the Chinese Embassy
in Yaounde´ ,
In the 1950s and 1960s the students went to China for political
reasons, and 100% of them returned to their country of origin.
But all this has changed with the economic development of
China. Now the students are trying to stay in China just the
same way as the Chinese students studying in Europe try to stay
in Europe.
During the last 6 years Cameroon received between 20 and 35
scholarships from China annually. In 2009, the offer was 40 places,
placing China as the ﬁrst scholarship donor, followed by Morocco
and Algeria. Usually the students enter at bachelor level – and can
reapply after obtaining the ﬁrst degree to continue to Master’s
level. Most of the students reapply to continue higher studies
when they have completed the ﬁrst scholarship period. The
students select their subject and the university they wish to
attend, based on a list of available universities. Science, IT and
economics are the most popular subjects, but Chinese Embassy
personnel (which is coordinating the scholarship program with
the Ministry of Higher Education) also request that a number of
students interested in studying Chinese language and culture
should be offered places:
The students going to China favor economics, industry and IT
studies. Very few are involved in cultural studies. I sort of
imposed cultural studies for at least four places this year, to
promote the Chinese language.
Staff at the Scholarship Commission underlined that there is a
language and cultural barrier in China that African students going
to Europe do not encounter. Some students in China complained
about isolation and in certain cases ostracism, or even outright
racism. It should be recalled that the Tiananmen Square
demonstrations followed anti-African demonstrations among
Chinese students in Hehai University in Nanjing ( Crane, 1994 ).
However, with the increasing number of Africans in China, it seems
as if the relationships have improved:
It is easy for Cameroonian students to integrate in Europe.
However, students going to China are complaining about
isolation. ... The number of African students is increasing in
China, so they generally feel less and less isolated.
Personnel at the Ministry of Higher Education’s Scholarship
Commission indicated that the earlier integration problems
are largely solved: currently there is even a Cameroonian
student association in China. During ﬁeldwork focus
group settings, however, the students often asked me about
ostracism in China. They had heard former or current African
students in China complaining about such problems. Despite
these issues of isolation and ostracism, the interest for studying
in China has multiplied: a few years ago, only 40–50 candidates
applied for the scholarships, of which half received an offer.
Sometimes the scholarship commission had to investigate
applicants for other scholarships to obtain enough candidates
to go to China, and thus suggested to students who had applied
for scholarships for Morocco or some other country, that they
could go to China instead. Currently, for 2008 and 2009, the
situation has changed substantially, and the commission
received 350 requests for the scholarships to China. As noted
by an interviewee, ‘‘Now, we’re in the inverse situation; there
are too many applicants.’’
A number of Cameroonian students go to China to study, using
their own money or relying on their family’s ﬁnancial support.
Often they revert to the Embassy when their funds run out: this
problem is so common that the Cameroonian embassy in Beijing
has a speciﬁc budget to deal with it. It is straightforward for self-
funded students to go to China because of the ease to obtain a
visa. Although neither the scholarship commission nor the
Embassy has exact numbers, both estimate that currently there
are an equal number of Cameroonian self-funded and scholarship
students in China. The Chinese Embassy in Yaounde ´ said, ‘‘there
are maybe 30 or 40 self-funded students going to China every
year.’’ Many of the students, after ﬁnishing their studies, ﬁnd
employment in China. Also, for those coming back, Chinese
companies based in Cameroon may employ them. The Ministry of
Higher Education does not have an overview of the further
employment of Cameroonian students with a Chinese education,
but noted that:
They do not come back to the Ministry to tell that they are back,
or that they are unemployed. We believe that either they
remain in China or that they come back and get employment
straightaway in Cameroon.
Currently, Cameroon receives scholarships, not only from the
Chinese government, but also from independent institutions in
China. For example, the Ministry of Higher Education recently
received an offer from the Beijing municipality of ﬁve places in the
University of Economics in Beijing. It was an offer of payment of

Since a number of students already are going to China to study

with the Chinese language training in Cameroon: the cooperation
increased substantially in importance in the 1990s, prior to which
it was limited to political exchanges. In the 1990s, with the boom in
China, the public increasingly became aware of China as a new
international economic power. Hence, interest for China, Chinese
language studies, and studies in China increased in parallel to
China’s new economic visibility.
In addition to the long-term scholarships, the Chinese are also
providing short-term training to Cameroon and every year ﬁnance
between 100 and 110 study tours in China for technicians and civil
servants. Chinese authorities offer two types of training,
technician training which is longer and last between 6 months
and a year, and training for civil servants, which usually lasts
about 2–3 weeks. In 2008, approximately 60 Cameroonian civil
servants and 30 technicians were trained. They went to different
places in China, depending on the theme of the training. In general,
the trainees and training type are selected by the Cameroonians,
based on a list of offers from the Chinese Ministry of Higher
Education. As noted by an Embassy staff member, ‘‘the global goal
for this 3-year period [i.e., FOCAC, 2006 goals] is to train 30,000
people from Africa and Latin America, but we don’t have a target
number for each country.’’ In general, the Chinese Embassy is
involved in the organization and coordination of the short-term
training.
3.3. School construction
Traditionally, China has been – and is – involved in large
infrastructure-building projects in Africa. Hence, Chinese compa-
nies have been involved in road building in Cameroon, and have
also constructed several sports complexes ( Palais des Sports )a s
well as administrative buildings. Until the 2006 FOCAC promise of
building 100 primary schools in Africa, however, the Chinese were
not much involved in basic education:
The cooperation with China began in 2006, so it’s very recent.
China’s aid is organized by events [ la Chine agit par a `-coup]. In
2006, we received a project for the construction of a single
primary school as a result of the visit of the Cameroonian
president to China ... In 2007, the Chinese president visited
Cameroon, and we received two primary schools (Interview
with a civil servant in the Ministry of Basic Education).
In 2009 the Chinese completed the building of three primary
schools (out of a total of 100 promised to Africa at the Third
FOCAC). The Cameroonian Ministry of Basic Education found the
cooperation with the Chinese so promising that a speciﬁc unit
dealing with Japanese and Chinese Projects has been set up within
the Ministry. The head of the Chinese section has spent 5 years
studying in China, and is ﬂuent in Chinese. She explained, ‘‘The
Chinese cooperation is at its beginning phase. ... We aim at setting
up the same type of coordination with China as we have with
Japan.’’
For primary school construction (as with other projects), the
Chinese cooperation can be said to be in the ‘‘response mode’’
(King, 2009 ). Accordingly, Cameroonians selected the location
where the schools were to be built, and then the Chinese, according
to an interviewee at the Cooperation Unit, ‘‘did everything,
including using their own architectural style.’’ The Chinese
Embassy explained that the construction of schools is ﬁnanced
by a gift from China that Cameroon ‘‘does not need to reimburse.’’
The Chinese authorities organize a bidding process in China to hire
a Chinese enterprise to build the school. This is, according to the
embassy, ‘‘part of a win–win situation’’ since it produces
international employment for Chinese enterprises. The prospective
enterprises need to have an authorization from the Chinese
government to participate in the bidding process, certifying that
they are capable of implementing an international project.
Apparently, according to an Embassy interviewee, ‘‘thousands of
enterprises meet this qualiﬁcation.’’ These enterprises can be
private, owned by civil society associations, or they can be state
enterprises. There is no limitation as far as the type of association is
concerned. In Cameroon, three enterprises were selected for the
construction of the three schools – and at least one of them was
private.
Japan is the largest bilateral donor for school construction in
Cameroon. Generally, the Chinese use more of their own experts
and workers in the construction process, whereas the Japanese
generally makes use of a local subcontractor who will do the actual
construction under supervision of Japanese experts. The techni-
cians are all local people. In contrast, the Chinese use technicians
from China together with local workers. Materials are brought
from China or purchased in Cameroon. The Cameroonian control
engineers that were following up on the construction, found the
Chinese construction norms perplexing:
We have a French education and we use French norms. What is
irritating with the Chinese is that they use Chinese norms. We
ﬁnd these norms less rigorous than our norms.
However, it was also noted that the Japanese had been studying
the Cameroonian environment and pedagogical needs for many
years, and had succeeded, over time, in adapting the school
buildings to Cameroonian needs and norms, whereas ‘‘China is still
a beginner’’ in terms of development cooperation:
China has therefore not yet normalized the design of school
buildings, and is working with provisional norms in order to
complete existing projects (Interview with a Cameroonian
Control Engineer).

3.4. An example of higher education cooperation: the microbiology
project
In 1993, a convention was signed between University of
Yaounde´ I and the University of Zhejiang (ZJU) for student
exchange and research cooperation in the ﬁeld of sciences and
microbiology. Under this cooperation, which is still ongoing
(albeit not very signiﬁcant at present), a number of students left
to China, and also a number of teachers received training in
China. In 1993, 10 academic staff came from China with the
objective to help local students at master’s level. The main phase
of the program focused on microbiology and lasted from 1993 to
1997. In the beginning, the cooperation encountered some
difﬁculties related to the cultural differences between the
Chinese professors and the Cameroonians. A professor stated
that ‘‘the Chinese didn’t speak with us; they spoke among
themselves. They were always calm.’’ However, to ﬁnd a
solutions of these problems, it was agreed that instead of

4. Policies and perception of Sino-Cameroonian cooperation
In looking at the various approaches and types of coopera-
tion, a pattern emerges: ﬁrst – the Chinese aid to Cameroon is
visibly on the rise. Second, the cooperation is still in a beginning
phase, and is still being organized to a large extent by the
involvement of the Chinese Embassy, not by professional
development and aid workers. Third, the cooperation is
trying to provide demand-driven, practical, useful aid to
Cameroon – which can be of mutual-beneﬁt for the two
countries; hence the emphasis on ‘‘win–win.’’ Finally, the
cooperation is largely based on programmatic events, such as
the FOCAC. In the two sections below, I ﬁrst outline the policies
of Chinese involvement in Cameroon – and then the perception
of the cooperation and aid.
4.1. Chinese aid and cooperation policies
As noted above, the aid and cooperation policies and strategies
are largely based on the FOCAC process. Therefore, it can be said
that the Sino-African aid and cooperation strategy is based on 3-
year programs. Most of the aid and cooperation projects are
decided as an exchange between two countries during FOCAC or
during some other high-level meeting. As noted by the Chinese
Embassy in Yaounde´ :
We are trying to make a strategic plan, a long-term plan. Every
third year, the interministerial forum [FOCAC] evaluates the
actions, and makes plans for new actions. For example we
decided to construct schools. In the years to come, we will
evaluate whether we should continue to give support in the
same sector. These decisions are taken during the meetings of
the forum.
The principles of the cooperation are said to be related to
African needs, and secondly, to help in domains that the Chinese
are capable to help them in; hence the focus on practical aid
projects, rather than the use of conditionalities and a meta-
narrative on, for example, structural adjustment or economic
policies, human rights, the environment,
7 or other themes that are
often being promoted by the Western donors.
This type of aid is characterized by the limited possibilities of
the Embassy to follow-up on the aid and cooperation. As noted in
the introduction, China distinguishes itself in not having a central
institution coordinating aid, nor professional aid workers that are
in charge of the follow-up of the cooperation projects. It follows
that China generally doesn’t fund any regional projects, only
particular projects in each country. It is interesting to note that the
Chinese Embassy in Cameroon has been participating in meetings
with the other donors at the World Bank to discuss the economic
situation for aid and cooperation. Also, Chinese Embassy repre-
sentatives participated in an Education For All sector meeting at
UNESCO-Yaounde´ . Also, the Embassy expressed a wish to stream-
line Chinese aid with other donors’ aid, and to co-implement
projects with UN agencies:
The China–Africa aid politics are a bit special. We don’t have
joint projects within international agencies, but only bilateral
arrangements. In the future, we hope to make joint programs
and projects with the World Bank, UNICEF and UNESCO. For the
moment, our only cooperation with these agencies is at the
level of exchange of information.
The Embassy in Yaounde ´ has only two key staff members
following up on the aid and cooperation projects (in education and
other domains), and who are also performing regular consular
functions. Such low number of staff limits the capacity of the
Embassy to conduct a realistic monitoring of the aid program and
of implementing a structured and coherent aid policy. The lack of
human resources for follow-up also has its repercussions in terms
of project documentation. Often, China has been criticized by the
West about its limited transparency and of lack of documentation
regarding the aid and cooperation projects. However, it should be
emphasized that China has only recently emerged as a donor, and
the Chinese institutions have not yet had time to adjust to the
dramatic increase in aid and cooperation that took place after the
ﬁrst (2000) FOCAC:
Our statistic services are not very good, and we have yet not
established a very good data system. ... Chinese aid and
cooperation with Africa have been hindered by a lack of
transparency, and a lot of the documentation on prior projects
has been lost. The new strategy of aid to foreign countries is
important for us, and we are currently reviewing old projects
and practices, to learn for the future (Embassy Personnel,
Yaounde´ ).
The personnel in the Embassies mostly follow-up on an aid
and cooperation agenda that is already set at a high political
level. For example, they will every year receive a list of training
seminars from the Chinese Ministry of Higher Education, which
they pass on to the Ministry of Higher Education in Cameroon.
Then they will follow-up on the selection and process the names
and visa applications for the chosen participants. Some of this
training, especially short-term training, clearly has a diplomatic
goal rather than providing capacity building. A Chinese ofﬁcial
criticized the selection of short-term trainees as biased, and said
that,

number of cooperation projects are set up between private

companies operate under the umbrella of a university, it can be
difﬁcult for the recipient country to determine whether the
aid and cooperation is part of an ofﬁcial agreement, or if it is
an institutional deal sought by a private partner. The

the same name as the university. These companies can offer
cooperation projects to foreign countries.’’ The Embassy does

negotiation between the company and the country’s host
institution. Hence, there could in some cases be confusion
between the public and private of China’s development aid. The
aforementioned microbiology project is a good example; it
started out as an institutional cooperation project between two
universities, which was facilitated by the Chinese Embassy
(which was involved in the negotiations between the institu-

bring in a private Chinese company for the installation of a
production facility to transform medical plants into medicine.
The Cameroonians said they were unsure of the expertise
provided by the Chinese (i.e., whether the Chinese would
dispatch professors or factory specialists to help the imple-
mentation of the project), and how the modalities of the
cooperation would be carried out, e.g., who would own any
patents that may come out of the research. Again, this situation
takes us back to the novelty of the strong Chinese involvement
in Africa, and the rapid expansion of the Sino-African aid and
cooperation, which maybe has precluded a more organized and
systematic approach.
4.2. Cameroonian perception of Chinese aid
A number of different attitudes and reactions can be
distinguished among the Cameroonian aid recipients. First, a
limited number of Cameroonians found the aid to be exploitative
and aligned to other donors’ excessive approaches. Often, these
ofﬁcials point to negotiating techniques employed by the Chinese
that they ﬁnd ‘‘unfair,’’ and complain about the Embassy’s
involvement in institution-to-institution negotiations. Also, inter-
viewees at the Prime Minister’s ofﬁce pointed out that the Chinese
look for raw materials and that the aid is tied to economic
conditionalities: ‘‘the Chinese are very shrewd and come with
things that we like. They give us a stadium; we give them an oil
well.’’

adjustments, we prefer dealing with the Chinese because they do
not ask for transparency or that we respect ethnic minorities in our

5. Conclusion
The Sino-Cameroonian educational cooperation illustrates
facets of China’s development engagement with Africa. First, one
notes a clear transition from a political engagement phase to a
more economic driven phase that can be dated to the period of
opening-up policies preceding the ﬁrst FOCAC (in 2000). The
transition is visible both from the Cameroonian and the Chinese
side: increasingly Cameroonians are interested in China because
of the economic development of the country. Conversely, China is
interested in a ‘‘mutually beneﬁcial’’ economic cooperation in
Africa. Second, the support has recently changed pace – and
China is therefore increasingly visible among the donors to
education in Cameroon. Third, China is interested in enhanced
cooperation with other education donors and aid agencies in
Cameroon.
The analysis of various Chinese education projects in Cameroon
demonstrates that China is a new donor – with particular problems
and weaknesses related to its quick expansion of the aid and
development sector: absence of a professional aid and coordina-
tion structure, ad-hoc replies to demands, unstructured aid that
does not always correspond to the needs of the recipient.
10
Kenneth King, in a recent study of China’s educational aid to Kenya
(2010), also found similar characteristics of Chinese aid, which is
said to be ‘‘on a learning curve.’’
During the ﬁeldwork, I noted the openness and interest of staff
at the Chinese Embassy to position themselves in the international
donor coordination structures, and to improve their own
implementation, monitoring, and reporting systems. China’s
involvement in Cameroon therefore seems to be slowly changing
in nature, as the Chinese Embassy has been increasingly present
and visible during education coordination meetings organized by
UNESCO, the World Bank, or the Cameroonian Government. Also,
the Chinese Embassy has conﬁrmed its current information-
sharing interest with DAC donors, and its willingness to coordinate
with bilateral and multilateral initiatives; and even to co-ﬁnance
projects. The Cameroonian stance seem to echo the discourse of the
new FOCAC summit and the Declaration of Sharm El Sheikh
(2009a), which seem to move towards a discourse aligned with
other donors. In particular, the declaration ‘‘call on the developed
countries to further assist African countries to achieve the UN
Millennium Development Goals’’ (p. 2) as well as makes a
commitment to ‘‘make sure that Africa’s endeavor to achieve
the Millennium Development Goals will not be reversed’’ (p. 4).
Further, the declaration ‘‘oppose[s] protectionism in all its forms,
and stand[s] to uphold an open and free trade and investment
environment’’ as well as commits the partners to ‘‘continue to
advance the Doha Round of trade negotiations’’ (p. 3).
Similar characteristic does not seem to be shared in all African
countries: Kenneth King, in the aforementioned study of China’s
educational aid to Kenya (2010) found that China did not plan to
harmonize its procedures with other donors in Kenya and that it
upheld a distinct discourse among the donors. Up till now, China
has distinguished herself by a discourse based on notions of ‘‘win–
win,’’ friendship and non-interference. The main political
commitment of African countries seems to be linked to the
‘‘one China policy and support for China’s peaceful reuniﬁcation’’
(FOCAC, 2009a , p. 5). However, this situation may change if the
FOCAC process becomes a tool to monitor China’s delivery on
pledges against speciﬁc agendas, including the Millennium
Development Goals, in which case ‘‘China will run the risk of
being perceived as an aid donor rather than in its preferred role of
being seen as a large developing country involved, to the best of its
ability, in South–South cooperation with other developing
countries’’ ( King, 2009 , pp. 3–4). It will be interesting to see
whether China in the future will keep its distinct vocabulary or if it
instead gradually aligns its discourse and practices with the
traditional donor groups.
