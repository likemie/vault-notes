From cooperation to de-risking: The making 
of “China Competence” in Germany’s policy 
transformation (2015–2024)
Von Kooperation zu De-Risking:  
Die Konstruktion der „China-Kompetenz“ im 
Zuge des politischen Wandels in Deutschland 
(2015–2024)
https://doi.org/10.1515/ifdck-2026-0005
Abstract: Over the past decade, the German federal government has established the 
“China Competence” initiative both as a funding policy instrument and as a central 
pillar of its China strategy. Despite extensive funding schemes and sustained aca -
demic debate, it remains unclear how “China Competence” is conceptually defined 
and practically operationalized. Drawing on a retrospective policy analysis cover -
ing the period from 2015 to 2024, this article examines the discursive and prac -
tice-based construction and transformation of “China Competence” within funding 
governance. The analysis operates across three interrelated analytical levels: polit-
ical decision-makers, funding recipients, and funded actsivities. The findings reveal 
a two-phase yet closely intertwined development trajectory: an initially coopera -
tion-oriented phase centered on cultural understanding, language acquisition, and 
trust building, followed by a strategic reorientation toward de-risking, security gov-
ernance, legal compliance, and policy-relevant expertise. On this empirical basis, 
the study advances a conceptual model that reconstructs “China Competence” as a 
dynamic concept constituted through discourse and practice. It further highlights 
the growing ambivalence faced by German higher education institutions as they 
navigate the tension between logics of cooperation and de-risking in contemporary 
Sino-German academic relations.
Keywords: China Competence, China policy, funding policy, Sino-German relations, 
de-risking

1  Introduction
In October 2015, the German Federal Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF) 
introduced the China Strategy 2015–2020, characterizing cooperation with China as 
an “unmissable opportunity” (BMBF 2015a). This strategy underscored the signifi -
cance of establishing trustworthy and reliable strategic partnerships with China in 
scientific collaboration, aiming to secure Germany’s leadership in future technolog-
ical innovation and economic prosperity (BMBF 2015a).
To operationalize this vision, the BMBF coined the concept of “China Compe -
tence” and launched a series of funding programs over the past decade to foster 
this capability within German higher education (Bundesregierung 2024). Since 
then, the initiative has assumed two interconnected roles: first, as both an instru -
ment for – and a prerequisite to – achieving the objectives of the China Strategy 
2015–2020; and second, as a policy goal guiding the design and implementation of 
related funding programs.

The release of the federal government’s new China Strategy in July 2023, 
however , marked a significant shift in the framing and implementation of the 
“China Competence” initiative. While reaffirming core elements such as Chinese 
language proficiency, intercultural competence, and comprehensive knowledge of 
contemporary China, the updated strategy expanded the concept to include aware-
ness of China’s global engagement objectives and practical experience in navigating 
its political system in the context of bilateral cooperation (Auswärtiges Amt 2023).
Despite this refinement in policy discourse, systematic understanding remains 
limited regarding how “China Competence” has been constructed through funding 
governance and the practices of implementing actors. Furthermore, as the policy 
frameworks guiding the “China Competence” initiative have evolved over time, it is 
essential to examine how governance mechanisms have adapted to these shifts and 
whether “China Competence” primarily functions to promote mutual understand -
ing and trust or increasingly operates as a strategic and political instrument (Fricke 
2022) within Sino-German relations.
This paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews existing scholarly 
debates on “China Competence”. The subsequent section outlines the theoretical 
framework and methodological approach, introducing the three-layer analytical 
model and the corpus-based retrospective policy analysis. The following section 
reports the empirical findings, tracing the evolution of the “China Competence” 
initiative across two distinct policy phases. Drawing on these findings, a concep -
tual model of “China Competence” is then developed. The final sections discuss 
the broader implications of the policy transformation, outline avenues for future 
research, and conclude with the study’s main theoretical and practical contribu -
tions.
2  Literature review on “China Competence”
In its initial funding calls, the BMBF called for measures to foster “China Compe -
tence” without providing a precise definition of the term (BMBF 2016). Building 
on these suggestions, German universities and research institutions that received 
funding to develop “China Competence” training programs spearheaded the first 
academic discussions on its conceptual scope, theoretical foundations, and peda -
gogical approaches (Thelen et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2021; Hou et al. 2022).
In area studies, for instance, “China Competence” is typically conceptualized 
as a broad, country-specific competence, covering comprehensive knowledge 
of China’s politics, economy, science, law, culture and society. By contrast, sinol -
ogy emphasizes proficiency in the Chinese language as both a prerequisite and

foundation for deeper cultural understanding and the development of “China 
Competence” (Zimmer 2021). Scholars in management and law adopt a different 
perspective, highlighting the importance of professional expertise in facilitating 
bilateral cooperation, as such expertise is essential for delivering practical solu -
tions (Expertenkommission Forschung und Innovation 2020; Stepan/Frenzel 2021).
Other scholars approached “China Competence” as a set of practical skills. Zimmer 
(2021) for instance, observes that professionals with extensive experience living 
or working in China  – despite limited language proficiency  – often demonstrate 
stronger judgment in problem-solving and collaboration with Chinese counter -
parts. He argues that this judgment is a crucial component of “China Competence,” 
one that can only be cultivated through processes of “socialization” within Chinese 
culture (Zimmer 2021).
Unlike perspectives that conceptualize “China Competence” as a mere aggrega -
tion of knowledge or skills, Sinologist Guder foregrounds curiosity as its central 
pillar – understood as a deep and sustained interest in the lived conditions, aspi -
rations, values, and social environments of people in China (Kierst 2022). Drawing 
on competence models in educational science, Robak (2021) further advances a 
content-oriented framework for “China Competence”. Together , these disciplinary 
perspectives have produced a more nuanced and multifaceted understanding of 
the concept, thereby enriching the design and implementation of academic training 
programs.
3  Theoretical framework and methodology
Unlike academic definitional debates, this study conceptualizes “China Compe -
tence” not only as a stable or discipline-bound set of individual skills, but also as a 
politically initiated policy concept whose meaning is institutionally embedded and 
discursively constituted and transformed through funding governance and prac -
tice. Accordingly, it examines how “China Competence” has been constructed and 
reconfigured through government funding policy and funded activities between 
2015 and 2024. Drawing on Lepori’s (2011) model of public funding systems, which 
distinguishes four organizational layers and illustrates how policy rationalities 
cascade through funding agencies, performing organizations, and project groups, 
this study employs an adapted three-layer analytical framework.
The first layer focuses on policy and policymakers, notably government bodies 
such as the Federal Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF), which simulta -
neously act as funding providers for the “China Competence” initiative. Informed 
by interpretive approaches to policy, policy is treated here as a process of mean -

ing-making rather than a purely instrumental response to pre-defined problems 
(Yanow 2000). Funding policy texts  — including strategies, calls, guidelines and 
think tank reports — are thus understood as discursive interventions that define 
what “counts” as a policy problem and policy rationale, what is considered legiti -
mate knowledge, and which actors are authorized to speak and act (Fischer 2003).
The second layer examines policy implementers and funding recipients, most 
notably higher education institutions (HEIs) and their affiliated project groups. To 
systematically reconstruct actor constellations, role attributions, and governance 
relationships within the “China Competence” initiative, the analysis draws on stake-
holder analysis as a heuristic tool for mapping key actors and their institutional 
positioning (Brugha/Varvasovszky 2000). This perspective allows for an examina -
tion of how policy meanings articulated at the strategic level are operationalized, 
coordinated, and mediated through funding governance structures.
Building on this, the third layer adopts a practice-oriented perspective, con -
ceptualizing “China Competence” as enacted and stabilized through funded pro -
jects and activities. Drawing on practice theory, competence is understood as a 
socially produced capacity that materializes in training formats, advisory services, 
networks, compliance routines, and toolkits (Reckwitz 2002; Schatzki 2001). This 
approach enables the study to derive an empirically grounded understanding of 
“China Competence” based on concrete practices rather than solely on policy for -
mulations.
Methodologically, the study employs a retrospective policy analysis of the 
“China Competence” initiative from 2015 to 2024. Retrospective approaches are 
well established in policy research to trace trajectories, shifts, and causal contexts 
of policy development (El-Jardali et al. 2014; Williams et al. 2009). Since policy pre -
dominantly manifests in discourse or textual forms (Levinson et al. 2020), a corpus 
of public documents was compiled to reconstruct the discursive and institutional 
evolution of “China Competence” over time (Fischer 2003; Yanow 2000). Data were 
collected through systematic Google searches using the German keyword “China 
Kompetenz”, covering the period from October 2015 to May 2024. After screen -
ing for relevance and quality, the final corpus comprised policy and government 
reports (18), funding announcements (5), policy briefs and think tank reports (11), 
guidelines and recommendations (4), news articles (26), as well as project websites 
(24) maintained by funded institutions. Together , these diverse genres reflect the 
broader policy-text ecology through which “China Competence” has been con -
structed and institutionalized.
Interpretive Policy Analysis provides the epistemological foundation for under-
standing policy as a process of meaning-making (Yanow 2000), while qualitative 
content analysis offers a systematic procedure for operationalizing this interpre -
tive approach. Following the three-layer framework, qualitative content analysis

4  Findings
The findings suggest that the development and implementation of “China Com -
petence” policy over the past decade can be analytically divided into two distinct 
phases. The first phase began with the BMBF’s publication of the China Strategy 
2015–2020 and concluded with its premature termination in late 2019. The second 
phase commenced in 2020 and extends to the present. Guided by the three-layer 
analytical framework, the following sections delineate the defining features of each 
phase across three dimensions: 1) policymakers and guiding policy documents; 2) 
policy motives and objectives; and 3) funding recipients together with their imple -
mented activities. Building on this phase-based analysis, a conceptual model of 
“China Competence” is presented at the end of this chapter .
4.1  First Phase: 2015–2019
1)  Policymakers and guiding documents
The development of “China Competence” was primarily guided by the China Strat-
egy 2015–2020 , led by the BMBF . However , the Standing Conference of the Minis -
ters of Education and Cultural Affairs of the States, along with the Federal Foreign 
Office, also played active roles in shaping the policy (Kultusministerkonferenz et 
al. 2018). Since educational policy at the primary and secondary levels falls under 
the jurisdiction of the state ministries rather than the BMBF , close coordination 
between the federal and state authorities was essential. Moreover , “China Compe-
tence” extends beyond research and education policy overseen by the BMBF , inter-
secting with cultural exchange policy under the mandate of the Federal Foreign 
Office. Consequently, all three government bodies were actively involved in the 
policymaking process, each within its respective domain of responsibility.

2)  Policy motives and goals
Following the establishment of diplomatic relations in 1972, Germany and China 
signed a bilateral agreement in 1978 to promote cooperation in higher education 
and scientific research. Since 2011, this collaboration has continued within govern-
mental consultations, which emphasize expanding university research partner -
ships, facilitating student and scholar exchanges, and advancing cooperation in 
vocational education (Internationales Büro 2017a).
With China’s growing influence in politics, the economy, science, and culture, it 
has become Germany’s largest trading partner and a key actor on the international 
political stage. Since 2000, cooperation between Germany and China in higher edu-
cation has experienced rapid growth. By 2015, German and Chinese universities 
had established more than 1,100 partnerships (BMBF 2015b), while the number of 
Chinese students in Germany rose from 5,000 in 2000 to over 40,000 in 2023, making 
them the largest group of international students at German universities until the 
winter semester of 2022 (DAAD/DZHW 2015, 2023).
By contrast, the number of German students enrolling in Chinese or Sinology 
programs has steadily declined, with only about 500 new entrants in 2016. Similarly, 
the number of German students studying in China has stagnated (Frenzel/Gode -
hardt 2020). Although many German universities maintain close partnerships with 
Chinese institutions, the expansion of research collaborations — and the establish -
ment of new ones — has not kept pace with China’s rapid advances in science and 
higher education.
Notable asymmetries also characterize student mobility between the two coun-
tries. Most Chinese students in Germany are self-funded and pursue full degree 
programs, requiring proficiency in German and a minimum stay of three years to 
complete their studies. Conversely, the majority of German students in China partic-
ipate in short-term exchange programs of six to twelve months, with relatively few 
pursuing full degrees outside of double-degree programs. Moreover , most German 
students do not use Chinese as the language of instruction during their studies.
In Germany, the institutional base for Chinese language acquisition remains 
limited: only about 100 secondary schools offer Chinese as an elective foreign 
language (Kierst 2022), with roughly 5,000 students enrolled annually. By con -
trast, approximately 7 million students study English and 1.4 million study French 
as electives (Frenzel/Godehardt 2020). This disparity is also evident in academic 
exchanges. In 2015, more than 700 German scholars and researchers visited China; 
by 2021, that number had fallen to just 120 — a decline of nearly 83 % (DAAD 2024).
As China’s global influence continues to expand and the imbalance in Sino-Ger-
man exchange in science and higher education deepens (Abels/Bode 2022; Rudyak 
2021), the German government has become increasingly aware of critical gaps in its

advancements in China’s scientific research (BMBF 2015a).
3)  Funding recipients and funded projects
The inaugural “China Competence” funding program was launched in 2017. While 
some funded projects extend into the second phase, all programs initiated before 
2020 are classified as part of the first phase for the sake of analytical clarity. 
Funding recipients during this phase included a panel of experts specializing in 
China’s innovation research, as well as universities and research institutions. Their 
funded activities are detailed in the following section.
A)  Expert group
To strengthen understanding of China’s innovation landscape, the BMBF launched 
the German-Chinese Innovation Platform in 2017, forming a nine-member expert 
group from academia and industry. The group analyzed China’s innovation policies, 
advised on Sino-German cooperation, and contributed to government dialogues 
(Internationales Büro 2011). Between 2017 and 2019, it issued four policy briefs on 
topics such as the evolution of China’s innovation system, science and industry pol-
icies, technology transfer , market prospects, data protection, and high-tech invest-
ments (Deutsches Zentrum für Luft- und Raumfahrt 2017, 2018a, 2018b, 2019)
B)  Universities and research institutions
Between 2017 and 2022, the BMBF’s program “Developing China Competence at 
German Universities” (BMBF 2016) funded 11 universities (see Table 1) to institu -
tionalize “China Competence” through China centers, advisory programs, language 
and culture courses, intercultural training, student exchanges, and joint curricula 
with Chinese universities (Internationales Büro 2017b). Alumni, as pivotal facilita -
tors of Sino-German academic exchange, have garnered significant attention from 
funding bodies. The German-Chinese Alumni Program (2017–2023) supported seven 
universities and one research institution in building discipline-based networks 
across fields such as the humanities, logistics, business, urbanization, neurosci -
ence, and medicine, fostering ties with professional associations and enabling new 
collaborations (Kooperation International 2017). At the school level, Heidelberg 
University’s Department of Sinology launched the China School Academy (2019–

4.2  Second Phase: 2020–2024
1)  Policymakers and guiding documents
By late 2019, the China Strategy 2015–2020, initially introduced by the German Federal 
Ministry of Education and Research (BMBF), was prematurely discontinued and 
removed from the ministry’s website (Bundesregierung 2024). It was replaced by 
the Federal Government’s China Strategy, drafted by the Federal Foreign Office and 
officially released in July 2023 (Auswärtiges Amt 2023). Unlike its predecessor , which 
largely reflected the BMBF’s priorities, the new strategy provides a comprehensive 
framework for the federal government’s overall approach to China. This transition 
marks a shift in leadership over “China Competence” development from the BMBF to 
the Federal Foreign Office, indicating a broader and more integrated policy direction.
2)  Policy motives and goals
In the second phase, the German federal government redefined its approach to 
Sino-German relations, framing China simultaneously as a partner , competitor , and 
systemic rival (Auswärtiges Amt 2023). Within this revised framework, the objec -
tive of “China Competence” development shifted from promoting cooperation to 
prioritizing strategic competition and risk mitigation – a process widely described 
as “de-risking”. This approach highlights the safeguarding of Germany’s core inter-
ests and values (Auswärtiges Amt 2023).
Concerns about Chinese state influence, particularly through Confucius Insti -
tutes, have prompted revisions to the concept of “China Competence” under the 
new China Strategy (Müller 2023). The updated policy calls explicitly for cultivat -
ing “independent China Competence”, emphasizing the protection of academic 
freedom in German higher education and the importance of critical engagement 
with China (BMBF 2021b).
In accordance with this strategic reorientation, the BMBF continues to under -
score the value of scientific and educational cooperation with China but now 
restricts support to collaborations that provide clear added value for Germany and 
Europe – particularly in fields such as climate change and environmental protec -
tion. Conversely, partnerships in high-tech sectors involving dual-use technologies

This policy shift reflects broader changes in political discourse within Germany 
and the European Union (see Figure 1). Its origins can be traced back to the European 
Commission’s “EU-China: A Strategic Outlook (March 2019)”, the first official EU doc-
ument to characterize China not only as a partner in economic interests and a com-
petitor in technological leadership, but also as a systemic rival advancing an alterna-
tive governance model (European Commission 2019). This recalibration of EU’s China 
policy prompted Germany to reassess its own approach to China, ultimately leading 
to the premature termination of the BMBF’s “China Strategy 2015–2020” and marking 
the conclusion of the first phase of “China Competence” development.
It is noteworthy that the release of the new China strategy was delayed until 
2023. While the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to this postponement, 
the transition of the federal government in late 2021 and the evolving political dis -
course were decisive in shaping the strategy. In July 2020, for instance, the German 
Institute for International and Security Affairs (SWP) issued a policy report calling 
for a broader approach to “China Competence.” The report argued that its develop-
ment should extend beyond higher education policy, be coordinated across federal 
ministries, and be framed simultaneously as an educational and foreign policy ini-
tiative (Frenzel/Godehardt 2020).
Similar concerns were raised by the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and the 
Christian Social Union (CSU), the largest opposition parties in the Bundestag at the 
time. In a strategic paper published in April 2023, they underscored the urgency of 
developing “independent China Competence” to strengthen Germany’s capacity for 
risk assessment in its cooperation with China (Mayer-Kuckuk 2023). Their proposals 
included the establishment of a “China Competence Center” under the Chancellor’s 
Office to advise federal and state authorities, as well as mandatory “China Compe -
tence” training for all new employees in top federal institutions within their first 
two years of service – a model inspired by practices in the United Kingdom (May -
er-Kuckuk 2023).

Following the policy shift in the second phase, there has been a significant trans -

phase, the range of actors and institutions engaged in policy implementation has 
expanded beyond universities and research institutions to include public founda -
tions, law firms, database service providers, think tanks, and the German Academic

B)  Public foundations
In the second phase, public foundations emerged as new actors in advancing 
“China Competence”. Through the BMBF-funded China-KoBe project (2020–2023), 
outstanding students and doctoral candidates from Germany’s 13 talent promotion 
foundations received scholarships and a three-year training program combining 
preparatory courses in Germany, intensive Chinese language training, and oversea 
semesters in China (Hans Böckler Stiftung 2020). Initially run jointly by the Hans 
Böckler Foundation and the Foundation of the German Economy, the project has 
been managed solely by the Hans Böckler Foundation since April 2023.
C)  Legal firms and database services
Aligned with the new China Strategy, security concerns have become central to 
managing academic cooperation and competition with China. Legal expertise is 
now considered a key component of “independent China Competence”. To support 
universities, the BMBF has engaged law firms and database providers to offer legal 
advice and partner background checks (Bundesregierung 2024). Several universi -
ties have also integrated legal training into their “China Competence” programs, 
stressing compliance with regulations, academic ethics, and research norms, and 
urging clarification of legal issues before initiating cooperation (Universität Jena 
2024). In response to rising economic competition, some universities have advo -
cated the establishment of robust legal and institutional frameworks to mitigate 
risks related to intellectual property protection, economic espionage, dual-use tech-
nology. These concerns also extend to institutional constraints that may affect per-
sonal safety, free speech, and academic freedom for foreign researchers in China. 
(Jülich Aachen Research Alliance 2024).
D)  Think tanks
Amid escalating geopolitical tensions in Sino-German relations, the demand for 
policy-relevant China expertise has intensified. In this context, the early program -
matic emphasis of “China Competence” — primarily centered on language acqui -
sition, cultural knowledge, and general insights into contemporary China  — has 
increasingly been regarded as insufficient for supporting decision-making and 
safeguarding national interests in a rapidly changing strategic environment. Conse-
quently, policymakers have progressively shifted attention toward think tanks and 
policy advisory institutions capable of producing what is framed as more “inde -
pendent China Competence”.
A prominent example is one of Germany’s leading China-focused research and 
advisory institutes, which has expanded its role by offering policy consultation and 
expert analyses on China’s political system, economic development, societal trans-

ence has been publicly recognized by state actors and major political stakeholders 
(Auswärtiges Amt 2023; Papenbrock/Schmid 2023). Notably, from 2024 onwards, the 
Federal Foreign Office has committed sustained institutional funding of €500,000

In parallel, the institute has increasingly been positioned as a key partner for 
China-related training and consultation, extending beyond government bodies to 
include stakeholders in the business sector . Taken together , these developments 
suggest an emerging policy logic in which “China Competence” is increasingly 
defined as strategic knowledge production oriented toward risk assessment, 
national resilience, and geopolitical positioning.
E) German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD)
As Germany’s largest funding body for international academic exchange, the 
German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD) holds a strategic position in academic 
diplomacy and the internationalization of higher education. In January 2024, sup -
ported by the Federal Ministry of Education and Research and the Federal Foreign 
Office, the DAAD issued guidelines for academic cooperation with China (DAAD 
2024). These guidelines emphasize three core principles: (1) alignment with German 
interests and balanced partnerships, (2) risk minimization and transparency, and 
(3) the development of “independent China Competence” through critical engage -
ment (DAAD 2024).
Within this framework, risk management and strategic security have emerged 
as primary concerns. The guidelines explicitly mandate that universities must 
comply with export control regulations on dual-use goods and knowledge (BAFA 
2023), and require due diligence to assess potential partners for ties to security-re -
lated research or national defense projects (DAAD 2024). Ethical concerns linked 
to fundamental values and human rights must also undergo review by university 
ethics committees or relevant self-regulatory bodies (DAAD 2024).
Together , these measures establish a comprehensive compliance framework 
spanning federal, state, and institutional levels, thereby strengthening de-risk -
ing practices in Sino-German academic relations. Looking forward, both German 
and Chinese institutions are likely to encounter growing challenges, even in areas 
where cooperation remains formally permissible.

4.3  A phase-based conceptual model of “China Competence”
As the primary initiator and funding provider of the “China Competence” initiative, 
the BMBF has implemented a total of ten funding programs between 2017 and 2024, 
with the cumulative budget reaching 24 million euros (Bundesregierung 2024). In 
2023 alone, the disbursed funding amounted to 5 million euros (Bundesregierung 
2024). Beginning in 2025, the BMBF – renamed BMFTR – plans to launch two addi -
tional funding programs: one dedicated to modern China research and another 
to Chinese language acquisition, bringing the total number of programs to twelve 
(BMBF 2024a, 2024b).
The comparative analysis of the two phases of the “China Competence” ini -
tiative reveals a significant transformation following the strategic shift in 2023 
(see Figure 3). The initiative’s primary objective has transitioned from deepening 
strategic cooperation with China to prioritizing competition and risk mitigation. 
Consequently, “China Competence” has evolved from a broadly defined concept of 
intercultural competence into a dissociative policy focused on de-risking practices, 
rendering the term increasingly ambiguous.
This transformation is observable at both the individual and institutional 
levels. At the individual level, the initial phase emphasized enhancing proficiency 
in the Chinese language, intercultural competence and a comprehensive under -
standing of contemporary China. However , in the second phase, the scope has 
expanded to include expertise in legal frameworks, compliance regulations, and

practical experience in China. At the institutional level, the focus of “China Com -
petence” has shifted from talent development and information tracking to a more 
comprehensive capacity for legal and policy consultation.
The temporal analysis of the “China Competence” initiative (see Figure 1) indi-
cates a linkage between European and German policy shifts and their cascading 
effects on academic and scientific relations. The findings suggest that the initiative 
reflects not only a domestic policy adjustment but also a response to broader geopo-
litical realignments within the European Union. The evolution of Germany’s China 
strategy has directly shaped its “China Competence” initiative, positioning it at the 
nexus of foreign policy and scientific exchange policies. This dynamic interplay 
has continually driven the evolution of funding measures associated with “China 
Competence” over time.
5  Discussion and Future research
The findings reveal several underlying challenges that merit further scholarly 
attention. First, the coexistence of two divergent policy logics — namely the pro -
motion of cultural and epistemic understanding on the one hand, and the pursuit 
of strategic de-risking on the other — constitutes a fundamental tension that risks 
undermining the internal consistency of the “China Competence” initiative. Moreo-
ver , the initiative’s pronounced sensitivity to exogenous political shifts underscores 
the contingent and path-dependent nature of its institutional trajectory. Against 
this backdrop, future research should interrogate whether the reconfiguration of 
transatlantic relations following the Trump administration, together with the for -
mation of a CDU/CSU-led federal government in Germany, may trigger further stra-
tegic realignments of China policy at both EU and national levels.
Second, the initiative’s dual positioning at the intersection of foreign policy and 
scientific exchange policy risks generating tensions between political objectives, 
the educational missions of universities, and the principle of academic freedom. 
This raises important questions about how universities, research institutions, 
and individual scholars navigate the top-down de-risking approaches introduced 
by policymakers and funding bodies, while adapting to expanding legal and reg -
ulatory constraints and simultaneously sustaining opportunities for academic 
cooperation with China (Cai 2023). Given the persistent uncertainty surrounding 
the translation of Berlin’s China policy into operational practice at municipal and 
university levels (Godehardt 2024), future research should explore whether—and 
how—these institutions can act not merely as passive implementers but also as 
“creative agents” (Levinson et al. 2020), developing bottom-up strategies that con -

tribute to more resilient and context-sensitive models of scientific and educational 
exchange at all institutional levels.
Third, the evolving and dual nature of the “China Competence” initiative, com-
bined with adjustments in funding instruments and implementing bodies, compli -
cates assessments of its overall effectiveness. While the evidence suggests that the 
initiative has expanded the China-related expertise within Germany’s science and 
education sectors, its translation into more effective China policymaking remains 
contingent on the meaningful integration of diverse and domain-specific knowl -
edge into inclusive, evidence-based decision-making processes. This integration 
also depends on how policymakers balance risk management with opportunities 
for cooperation. Clarifying whether the initiative’s primary objective lies in foster-
ing collaboration or advancing strategic de-risking is essential, as fully achieving 
both goals simultaneously appears unrealistic.
2Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The analysis relies 
primarily on publicly available documents, which may not fully capture internal 
decision-making processes or informal negotiations among policymakers and 
implementing actors. Moreover , while the retrospective corpus approach enables 
systematic tracing of discursive and institutional change, it cannot directly assess 
the lived experiences and perceptions of project participants. Building on these lim-
itations, future research should move beyond policy-level analysis to examine the 
concrete impacts of the “China Competence” initiative on scientific relations and 
the broader dynamics of knowledge diplomacy between Germany and China (Liu 
et al. 2025). In-depth interviews and case studies could provide valuable insights 
into how higher institutions and individual actors respond to evolving policy 
frameworks, navigate compliance requirements, and manage emerging risks. Such 
empirical evidence would inform more adaptive and forward-looking strategies for 
sustaining Sino-German scientific relations in an increasingly complex geopolitical 
environment.
6  Conclusion
This study examines the evolution of Germany’s “China Competence” initiative 
between 2015 and 2024 through a retrospective analysis of policymakers, funding 
recipients, and funded activities. The findings identify two interconnected phases: 
an initial cooperation-oriented phase emphasizing cultural understanding and aca-
demic exchange, followed by a strategic shift toward de-risking, security govern -
ance, regulatory compliance, and policy-relevant expertise, reflecting broader geo-
political realignments in Germany’s and the European Union’s China policies. This

transformation has reshaped both institutional actors and the substantive meaning 
of “China Competence”, extending it from intercultural capacity toward strategic 
knowledge production and risk management.
Theoretically, the study advances an empirically grounded conceptualization 
of “China Competence” as a dynamic policy concept and institutional knowledge 
form. Moving beyond normative and discipline-specific definitions, the analysis 
demonstrates how it is discursively constructed, enacted through funded practices, 
and transformed over time. The three-level analytical model developed in this 
study offers a transferable framework for analyzing the meanings of political con -
cepts across policy domains. Practically, the findings illuminate growing tensions 
between the objectives of academic cooperation and strategic de-risking, posing 
significant challenges for higher education institutions in an increasingly politi -
cized policy environment.
