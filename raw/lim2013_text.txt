Meritocracy, elitism, and egalitarianism: a preliminary and
provisional assessment of Singapore’s primary education review
Leonel Lim*
Department of Curriculum and Instruction, University of Wisconsin-Madison, Wisconsin, USA
(Received 1 January 2011; ﬁnal version received 12 September 2011 )
Meritocracy functions in Singapore as the key principle of governance and educational
distribution. However, the concept of meritocracy itself contains a number of inherent
contradictions, most evidently witnessed in the tension between its egalitarian and
elitist strands. This tension is documented in the recommendations of Singapore’s
recent primary education review, already accepted by the Ministry of Education and to
be implemented in the coming years. This article considers these recommendations
against the backdrop of a tentative movement towards egalitarianism in educational
distribution. Speciﬁcally, it provides a preliminary and provisional assessment of how
far they would go towards providing for greater educational equity and, therefore, in
recovering the ideology of meritocracy in the nation-state.
Keywords: educational equity; educational policy; meritocracy; primary education;
Singapore
Since its inception as a nation-state in 1965, the notion of meritocracy has remained a key
principle of governance and educational distribution in Singapore (K.Y. Lee, 2000; Mauzy
& Milne, 2002; J. Tan, 2008a). Through a highly competitive education system culminating
in “bonded” government scholarships, top positions in the civil service administration and
political leadership are staffed by individuals with demonstrated track records of merit
(Barr & Skrbis, 2008). However, the concept and practice of meritocracy contains a number
of inherent contradictions, most evidently witnessed in the tension between its egalitarian
and elitist strands (C. Tan, 2008; J. Tan, 2008a; K.P. Tan, 2008). As early as 1978, for
example, the Report on the Ministry of Education, by targeting such phenomena as the
“failure to achieve the expected standards, premature school leaving, repetition of grades,
and unemployable school leavers” (as cited in Barr & Skrbis, 2008, p. 84), sought to ensure
that the education system would serve to beneﬁt the lowest common denominator of
Singapore’s society. Yet despite this assumed role of education as a crucial instrument of
social mobility, in the decades that followed levels of educational attainment have
persistently reﬂected class and ethnic biases. Indeed, as income gaps in Singapore continue
to widen conspicuously, it seems inevitable that “the old consensus on meritocracy” would
need to be reinforced by new measures “in order to contain an emerging politics of
disillusionment and resistance” (K.P. Tan, 2008, p. 7).

This article examines in depth one such measure that relates to Singapore’s educational
policy – the recent report of the Primary Education Review and Implementation (PERI)
committee, published in 2009 and to be implemented in the coming years. Speciﬁcally, it
considers its recommendations against the backdrop of a tentative movement towards
egalitarianism in educational distribution and provides a preliminary and provisional
assessment of how far they would go towards providing for greater educational equity in
the near future. The discussion is prefaced by an exposition of how the key tenets of
egalitarianism and elitism constitute an inherent instability in the concept of meritocracy.
Managing, if not giving the semblance of resolving, these tensions requires substantial
intervention by governments that espouse a principle of meritocracy, and the section details
such efforts in the education system by Singapore’s leaders.
Egalitarianism and elitism in the concept of meritocracy
Meritocracy, as the rule of merit, may be understood broadly as the rewarding of
individual merit with social rank, job positions, higher incomes, general recognition and
prestige, and, in the education system, greater educational resources (Jencks, 1988; K.P.
Tan, 2008). As Swift (2003, p. 24) notes, “people with the same level of merit – IQ plus
effort – should have the same chance of success.” Meritocracy, in its egalitarian moment,
by focusing on “careers open to talents” (Rawls, 1971, p. 65), signals merit as the rule or
principle that governs how limited resources and rewards in a society are to be distributed.
This ensemble of ideas central to the concept of meritocracy, however, is potentially
contradictory. As Satz (2007) points out, equality of opportunity here requires as well a
principle of non-discrimination: social positions, careers and rewards should be open to all
individuals and individuals should be selected based only on their talents and qualiﬁcations
for the position and not their race, class, or gender. Yet, as has been demonstrated many
times in the US and elsewhere, categories of race, gender, and class do afford unequal social
beneﬁts, both within and outside of schools (Mills, 2008; Lareau, 2003; Pateman, 1988).
This contradiction seems most prominent in Singapore, where the twin national beliefs
of meritocracy and multiracialism (or non-discrimination) are simultaneously invoked to
both emphasize the “fairness” of the socio-political system and to “explain” (away) the
subordinate role of the large population of non-Chinese minority races who lag behind in
areas of life such as politics, income, language dominance and education (Barr & Low,
2005; Moore, 2000; Rahim, 1998). In such a context, meritocracy can in fact give the veneer
of equality while simultaneously masking the real advantages and disadvantages that have
been differentially distributed across a society (Jencks, 1988). Or worse, it can lead to the
privatization of blame – as biological/cultural determinism (Barr, 1999) – amongst groups
that have traditionally been underserved by society. As Rahim (1998, p. 58) puts it,
[t]he rhetoric that Singapore is a meritocratic society where equal opportunities are available
to all has also served to add legitimacy to the cultural deﬁcit thesis which infers that Malays
have not been able to make it in a meritocratic society because they have not worked hard
enough and thus have only themselves to blame.
Indeed, to the extent that such a “charade of meritocracy” (Barr, 2006a) evacuates a
concerted focus at a politics of difference – one that foregrounds what Fraser (1997) calls
needs recognition and needs redistribution – the egalitarian dimension of meritocracy is
surely threatened.
This moves us to another understanding of meritocracy, one that is less interested in
providing everyone with equal rights to resources, and more concerned about revealing the
right person to manage resources (Cavanagh, 2002; Jencks, 1988; K.P. Tan, 2008). From

this utilitarian perspective, meritocracy functions as an efﬁcient system that sorts
individuals for positions of leadership in order to maximize the average level of well-being
in a society. This latter rendering of meritocracy often involves motivating individuals to
do the best they can. Implicit is the idea that through a ﬁerce competition for educational
resources and, later, material rewards, human talents may be developed to their potential
(K.P. Tan, 2008).
Given, however, the ways in which the economic and cultural capital of one generation
may be converted into the educational capital of the next (Bourdieu, 1984; Lareau, 2003),
such a focus on efﬁciency and competition can as well displace the egalitarian aspects of
meritocracy and lead to the legitimization of an unequal distribution of resources both
within and beyond the education system. Indeed, the then Singapore Prime Minister Goh
Chok Tong once warned that if social disparities and unequal rewards did not exist, those
with initiative and skills “will lose the incentive to contribute their utmost to the economy.
Then everyone will be poorer off. Do not begrudge them their high salaries ... for getting
the big prizes in the free market” (as cited in Kang, 2005, p. 3).
Thus, as pointed out by K.P. Tan (2008, p. 9), meritocracy in practice often translates
into “an ideology of inequality”, a belief that an unequal distribution of resources will
ultimately be beneﬁcial to the general if not entire population. In Singapore this emphasis
on developing a small group of talented individuals who will eventually work to maximize
the overall well-being of society clearly shows itself in the policies of the education
system. Beginning at the primary level, for example, the Gifted Education Programme
identiﬁes and tracks the top 1% of 9-year-olds into a special and separate academic route
in order to “develop [in them] intellectual rigour, humane values and creativity [and] to
prepare them for responsible leadership and service to country and society” (Ministry of
Education, 2011a). To be sure, the socio-political rationale for such an elitist impulse
explicitly alludes to the (putative) fact that “Singapore is a small nation with only human
resources to rely on for its progress and prosperity. It is to the advantage of the nation that
the gifted are helped and nurtured” (Ministry of Education, 2011a).
At the secondary level, the Integrated Programme allows students who are “clearly
university-bound” to enrol into programmes that bypass the standard General Certiﬁcate of
Education Ordinary Level examinations at the end of secondary school and to proceed
directly to the Advanced Level examinations at the end of high school (Ministry of
Education, 2011b). Such a provision further demonstrates the elitist strand in the education
system; schools offering the Integrated Programme, by optimizing the time freed up from
preparing for the Ordinary Level examinations, will be able to “stretch pupils and provide
greater breadth in the academic and non-academic curriculum” (Ministry of Education,
2011b). And for this cre `me de la cre`me, yet another option consists in bypassing the
Advanced Level examinations altogether, obtaining instead the more prestigious
International Baccalaureate diploma or one from the local National University of
Singapore High School of Mathematics and Science.
Other measures that make clear the system’s elitist orientation include the Independent
Schools scheme under which selected top performing (academically) schools enjoy greater
ﬁnancial and curricular autonomy, which translate into the school-based development of
niche programmes that heightens the prestige of the schools and their students; the families
of schools structure that allows well-established secondary schools and junior colleges to
provide preferential admission for students from their feeder schools, effectively making it
more difﬁcult for students from mainstream schools to gain a place of study; and the Special
Assistance Plan schools, where academic high-performers may enrol into schools designed
to immerse students in Chinese culture and values, triggering concerns of ethnic

This meritocratic drive to nurture a pool of elites, however, takes place largely in the
absence of a level playing ﬁeld and, as alluded to above, coupled with an overt principle of
non-discrimination, obscures the fact that students in Singapore stem from different socio-
economic backgrounds and go to school differently prepared (Barr, 2006b; Kang, 2005;
C. Tan, 2008). As a survey conducted in 2007 by the leading national newspaper reveals,
wealthier students from English-speaking homes predominate in the elite schools (Kwek,
2007). Seventy-one per cent of students from these schools speak English (the major
medium of instruction in all Singapore schools) at home while only 34% of students
from non-elite schools do so. Even among students who speak English at home, those from
elite schools come from families with a median monthly household income of S$7100
(US$5900), compared to those from non-elite schools whose median family income trails
at S$3,560. From 2001 to 2005, one third of the government’s Public Service Commission
scholarship holders in Singapore came from families with household incomes of more than
S$10,000 a month, while only 7% came from families with monthly household incomes of
less than S$2000 (Lim & Kwek, 2006). Most recently, former Prime Minister Lee Kuan
Yew has admitted that 60% of the students at Rafﬂes Institution, one of the most prestigious
secondary schools, “have parents who are both university graduates, making them of high
socio-economic background” with the other 40% “probably not far behind” (quoted in
Chang, 2011a). He also acknowledged that other “brand name” schools share similar
student proﬁles. Nearly 72% of the students at Anglo-Chinese School (Independent) have
graduate parents; at Crescent Girls the ﬁgure is about 50%; and Victoria School 45%. These
ﬁgures contrast sharply with those of less prestigious mainstream schools that range from
7 to 13% (Chang, 2011b).
As J. Tan (2008a) adds, further entangled with the problem of class-based disparities is
that of ethnic inequalities. Data from the 2010 population census indicate that the Chinese
are signiﬁcantly overrepresented in local universities, forming 86% of the total enrolment
in 2010, while constituting 77% of the overall population. Malays (5.5%) and Indians
(6.8%) are correspondingly underrepresented in tertiary institutions (Department of
Statistics, 2011). From 1966 to 2005, of the 200 winners of Singapore’s most prestigious
scholarship, the President’s Scholarship, only 14 (6.4%) were non-Chinese (Barr & Skrbis,
2008). To be sure, this educational gap is already present at lower levels of schooling, only
perpetuating itself at the tertiary level. Arguing that education in Singapore is “racialized”,
Barr’s (2006b, p. 15) historical analysis of primary school English textbooks documents
how these curricular materials have been “systemically designed [to] evoke high levels of
racial consciousness [and] have displayed a pro-Chinese bias that has deprived non-Chinese
children of inspiring role models”. Indeed, and in this connection, Barr & Low (2005) have
gone further to demonstrate how, even at the pre-school level, state-supported kindergartens
engage in a form of socialization that, by implicitly eulogizing the cultural attitudes, norms
and values of the Chinese, estranges the Malay population. While we note that Malay and
Indian students have made signiﬁcant improvements in educational attainment over the last
decades, their public examination results continue to lag behind those of their Chinese
counterparts (Ministry of Education, 2010a; see also Kang, 2005). Indeed, Lee has recently
asserted that the Malays would never close the gap in educational attainment with the
Indians and Chinese “because as they improve, the others also improve” (K.Y. Lee, 2011).

Regardless of their impact and efﬁcacy, these efforts nonetheless represent incipient
attempts at breaking away from the education system’s long-standing history of fostering
competition, with an emphasis on talent allocation and unequal rewards. Instead, they seem
targeted at recovering the egalitarian strand of meritocracy, one that explicitly commits
itself to the establishment of an “education system [that] provides equal opportunities for
each student to learn and achieve his or her potential” (Wong, 2000). Yet, as the
stratiﬁcation of educational achievement along ethnic and class lines becomes increasingly
apparent (Ministry of Education, 2010c), and as individuals’ social mobility continues to be
indexed by their educational attainment, the piecemeal manner of their conception and
implementation would ultimately prove insufﬁcient in engendering a common commitment
towards meritocracy. Consequently, a more concerted effort at containing the tensions
between egalitarianism and elitism becomes increasingly exigent. Such an effort, this
article suggests, is most evident in the recent report of the PERI committee.

moving towards – such as curricular and pedagogical innovation (Ng, 2008), greater

(K.P. Tan, 2008) – directions that may or may not be expressly committed to greater equity,
taken together, the egalitarian emphasis on levelling up features signiﬁcantly. Also worth
pointing out is the extent to which these recommendations resonate with efforts in the US

The move towards all single-session primary schools, for instance, allows the
lengthening of the school day, providing more time and space for students to master
skills/content through after-school remediation. Teachers, being freed of their duties for
the next session, would be able to engage in higher quality interaction with needy students
after classes, and have access to more venues and spaces to hold such sessions. As
documented by various studies on closing achievement gaps, increasing curricular time
beneﬁts less advantaged students more than they do for able ones (Neuman, 2009;
Rothstein, 2004; Thernstrom & Thernstrom, 2003). Given that many of the former come
from working-class families that often possess the least access to educational resources
outside of the school, keeping them in school for longer hours and having them engaged in
meaningful academic (and non-academic) tasks provides them with a conducive learning
environment which they might otherwise not have access to when the school day has
ended. In this connection, the provision made to house Student Care Centres in schools
raises the level of in-school support available to students from disadvantaged families,
providing them with a familiar and ready source of advice in times of need.
Providing primary schools with the discretion to determine their own start and end
times, taking into account the educational needs of the students as well as the demographic
proﬁle of their families and their transportation arrangements would allow schools to
better cater to the needs of less advantaged families in two ways. First, in less afﬂuent
neighbourhoods where parents commonly walk their young children to school (instead of
driving, or paying for the services of a school bus), schools have the ﬂexibility to start a
half hour later, thereby reducing the very real chances that students miss the early morning
lessons. Second, schools in less advantaged neighbourhoods where students on average
require more curricular time to master the skills and content would be able to lengthen the
school day accordingly to accommodate these needs.

The decision to recruit only graduates, or those who qualify for an undergraduate
education also signals a clear attempt at raising and standardizing overall teacher quality.10
As of 2008, while 55% of primary school teachers are graduates (Ministry of Education,
2008b), it is likely that the more prestigious primary schools would nevertheless be able to
attract and retain a greater number of these through, amongst other things, more ﬂexible
and challenging curricula, greater teacher autonomy, social and occupational prestige. The
move towards all-graduate recruitment thus serves to ensure a more equitable distribution
of teacher quality.
Providing language facilitators to ease students’ transition from their home mother
tongue languages to English, the medium of instruction in schools, also serves to better
prepare less advantaged children for learning. As highlighted above, in Singapore, the
majority of working-class families do not speak English at home. As a consequence, the
children of these families are ill-equipped to beneﬁt from the teaching and learning
activities in their ﬁrst years of school – especially if, as Barr & Low (2005) note, a larger
proportion of children from the minority races have not beneﬁtted from kindergarten
education – a handicap that contributes signiﬁcantly to their placement in lower academic
tracks in later years. By conducting before- and after-school enrichment lessons in areas
such as story-telling and drama, language facilitators can work to complement these
students’ acquisition and use of the English language, enabling them to beneﬁt more
equally from the classroom instruction and interaction as compared to their counterparts
from English-speaking families.
While increasing the number of Allied Educators (school counsellors, special needs
educators, and co-teachers) would, at some level, be of use to all students, those from less
advantaged communities would, again, seem to beneﬁt more from such support. For one,
we know that students from low-income communities are more likely to come to school
burdened with social and emotional problems. These problems could be related to the lack
of ﬁnancial stability at home, various forms of institutional and social discrimination,
(their own or their parents’ or siblings’) involvement in crime, and suchlike. If left
unattended, these issues are likely to turn the student’s attention away from matters of
academic importance. Additionally, in the case of special educational needs, whereas
more afﬂuent families will have the means to provide costly out-of-school learning
support for their children’s particular needs, less advantaged families often can only rely
on the support provided by public schools. Increasing the number of co-teachers, who in
addition to helping out with the teaching and learning activities in the classroom also assist
in providing pastoral care and support to students, thus constitutes a reduction in the
student-teacher ratio. We note that the MOE has plans to reduce the ratio in primary
schools from 21:1 to 16:1 by 2015 (Ministry of Education, 2009b). While empirical
studies remain inconclusive as to whether reducing the student-teacher ratio improves
overall teaching and learning processes in the classroom, there is evidence that
academically weaker students tend to beneﬁt more than their academically stronger
counterparts from the increased interaction and attention afforded by teachers in smaller
classes (Rothstein, 2004).
By recommending that all Primary 1 and 2 students be exposed to the broad areas of
sports and outdoor education, and performing and visual arts, the PERI committee in effect
advocates a widening of what counts as merit in the education system that goes beyond
traditional academic success. This move to “provide opportunities for exposure to a broad
range of activities so that children can discover their aptitudes and interests” (Ministry of
Education, 2009a) provides more avenues for children – especially those who might
otherwise lack the means to do so – to enjoy and experience a variety of activities, and to

dominant form of assessment. In a highly competitive education system that centres on the
use of high-stakes examinations as a sorting mechanism for the distribution of unequal
resources, many wealthy and middle-class families have taken to procuring key
examination skills for their children by sending them to reputable and expensive learning
centres, engaging private tutors, and suchlike, often as early as when the latter are in pre-
school. As a result, these students would sit more comfortably with, and hence perform
better in, the existing examination-oriented culture of schools, beginning at the early
primary levels. Reducing the emphasis on such major summative examinations in Primary
1 and 2 as the principal form of assessment then, and exploring the use of bite-sized forms of
assessment such as, amongst other things, topical tests, regular class work, does more than
just improve students’ conﬁdence and desire to learn, or provide more timely and regular
feedback to parents. More signiﬁcantly, it works to level the playing ﬁeld for students from
less advantaged backgrounds who, prior to starting formal primary education, have little
means to access these crucial examination skills.
Finally, the PERI committee’s recommendation that assessment rubrics should include
richer and more holistic evaluations of students and that schools and teachers should not
only provide feedback on students’ academic, but also non-academic performance,
resounds with the increasing awareness in research on how non-cognitive skills contribute
to improvements in educational attainment as well as social and emotional well-being in
their adult lives (Carneiro & Heckman, 2003; Raver, 2004; Rothstein, 2004). Such
research reveals that while the possession of character traits (perseverance, motivation,
self-conﬁdence, discipline, etc.) and social skills (cooperation, conﬂict resolution, self-
management, etc.) signiﬁcantly enable children to both enjoy and better perform in school,
these non-cognitive skills are also found most wanting among children of low-income and
disadvantaged communities. By requiring that evaluations of students include these
attributes, the recommendation directs teachers’ and schools’ efforts towards making gains
in these areas. If such initiatives translate into well-conceptualized curricular programmes
at the school and/or classroom level, we can expect that students from less advantaged
families, those who stand most in need of these skills, will have the most to gain.
Future challenges
Understood thus, the PERI report indicates a clear desire for greater equity in educational
policy, and functions critically to recover the egalitarian strand in the government’s
meritocratic ideology. However, just as new measures may be needed to contain a new
“politics of disillusionment and resistance”, these will in turn open up new sites of struggle
as different groups in society continue to press for greater egalitarian or elitist impulses and
rework the system in ways that beneﬁt them. To the extent then, that the ability of the PERI
recommendations to actually effect a more equitable distribution of educational resources
remains contingent on conditions extraneous to the policies themselves, our preliminary
assessment should remain cautious of any outright congratulatory pronouncements. In the
remaining space I highlight how in the near future, if left unchecked some of these

recommendations might instead serve to further instantiate the ideology of inequality,
ultimately solidifying the elitist structure of the education system.
First, while the decision to expose all Primary 1 and 2 students to the broad areas of
sports education and the performing arts in order that they may be able to discover and
hone their skills in a variety of non-academic pursuits constitutes a widening of what
counts as merit, it is not clear that all students are equally placed to do well in these new
arenas. To be sure, even if the MOE were able to provide similar amounts of ﬁnancial
support to all schools to fund these non-academic programmes, it remains likely that
middle-class parents, by possessing the time and the ﬁnancial wherewithal to enrol their
children in such activities as exclusive sports camps, tennis lessons, and dance classes that
engage expensive and exceedingly qualiﬁed instructors, would still be able to secure
signiﬁcantly higher achievements for their children in these domains (Lareau, 2003).
These parents would also be more likely to go on to use their children’s achievements in
these extra-curricular areas to secure placements for them in elite schools through the
Direct School Admissions exercise (discussed above), and/or to increase their chances of
winning prestigious scholarships. Ironically then, in this case, the widening of what counts
as merit can lead to a greater monopolization of success by select groups.
Likewise, situating the move towards all graduate teacher recruitment amidst an
increasingly neo-liberal educational climate characterized by school-choice impulses and
the celebration of merit pay for teachers as a means of “sifting the wheat from the chaff”
cautions us against the romantic assumption that all schools will thus be staffed by teachers
of similar expertise. As schools strive to differentiate themselves from the competition, the
calibre and credentials of their teachers ﬁgure as a crucial selling point. Schools that enjoy
more regulatory autonomy – and in Singapore’s case, these are schools that demonstrate
excellent academic results – will continue to possess greater means (such as offering
higher salaries, increased professional autonomy, more ﬂexible working schedules, etc.) of
attracting and retaining teachers with a proven and impressive track record.
The PERI committee has also recommended that schools employ more “innovative
and engaging pedagogies” in order to reach out to the interests of the child. Instead of
traditional didactic forms of teacher-centred instructional processes, schools are
encouraged to develop forms of teaching and learning that actively draw on the interests
and abilities of students. Following Bernstein’s (1977) inﬂuential work in understanding
the forms of transmission of educational knowledge and their differing effects on the
larger social order, we might surmise that such a change in pedagogical emphasis replaces
notions of adult’s time with notions of child’s time, and notions of adult’s space with
notions of child’s space. Thus, facilitation takes over imposition in curricular strategies
such as, amongst other things, Learning by Doing, Learning by Inquiry, Understanding by
Design, Teaching for Understanding, all already extensively used by schools praised for
their innovativeness (see, for example, Ng, 2008).
While there certainly is good sense in having schools assume greater responsibility
in effectively delivering the curriculum, yet, as Bernstein (1977) and other critical
sociologists of education (e.g., Apple, 2006; Bourdieu, 1984; Bourdieu & Passeron, 1977)
have demonstrated powerfully, the principles and assumptions of such child-centred
approaches to curriculum design are fundamentally rooted in middle-class patterns of child
socialization and interaction. When enacted in the formal curriculum, these pedagogies
typically presuppose, amongst other things, students’ familiarity and facility with particular
middle-class language codes, a long educational life, as well as the presence of middle-
class mothers as both crucial agents of cultural transmission and catalysts in the educative
process (Bernstein, 1977, 1990).

Perhaps of a more detrimental consequence, the theories and principles that underpin and
render sensible these pedagogies may not be known or understood by most working-class parents
who themselves have not enjoyed high levels of education and, as it is, can ill-afford time off from
making ends meet. To be sure, these pedagogies contain a different theory of transmission that
views the parents’ own informal teaching, where it occurs, or their pedagogical values, as
irrelevant if not downright harmful. There are new reading schemes, new mathematics that
replace arithmetic, and a focus on ways of knowing that displaces previous emphases on states of
knowledge. As Bernstein (1977, p. 139) puts it, “[i]f the [parents are] to be helpful, [they] must be
re-socialized or be kept out of the way.” If the former, they may well ﬁnd themselves at the mercy
of complex psychological theories of child development. Depending on the speciﬁcities of how
such “engaging teaching methods and pedagogies” would be implemented by the MOE and
communicated to families then, these could possibly (intended or otherwise) add to the
educational advantages already afforded to advantaged groups in society, leaving instead
working-class families further removed from the instrumental and moral orders of the school.
Together with the egalitarian promises found in the recommendations of the PERI
report, these concerns need to occupy any longer term assessment of the consequences of
adopting them. Our preliminary and provisional assessment suggests that, contained in the
recommendations are very real possibilities for both greater educational equity and
stratiﬁcation. While of course in all societies social groups with privilege and power will
always ﬁnd ways to work (and rework) the system in order to translate these advantages to
their children, what is noteworthy is that in the case of Singapore, the principle of
meritocracy that underpins the education system has for a long time actively perpetuated
the ideology of inequality, under the guise of equality.
As globalization continues to widen the income gap in Singapore, making social
divisions more pronounced and the lifestyles of wealthy and elite Singaporeans more
conspicuous, and as the less advantaged in society, with greater access to alternative views
via the Internet and other social media, are beginning to disbelieve the promises of upward
mobility through education and are less inclined to internalize their own and their
children’s misfortunes (K.P. Tan, 2008), a continued focus on a manifest egalitarianism in
Singapore’s educational policy will prove indispensible in upholding the collective faith in
meritocracy. Yet, if such efforts – as encapsulated in the recommendations of the PERI
report – are to advance further than those of earlier reforms in concretely establishing a
more level playing ﬁeld, the education system would also need to in the coming years
devote an unwavering attention to how these recommendations, well-intended as they may
be to improving the prospects of the less advantaged, when translated into policies and
implemented on the ground might concurrently instigate the deep-seated elitist impulses of
a social structure well used to an ethos of competition and individualized notions of merit.
