The Changing Frontiers

A Forty-year Retrospective on European Education 
European Education (originally known as Western European Educa-
tion) may no longer be directly associated with the field of compara-
tive and international education, yet its establishment in 1969 was an 
attempt to make a direct contribution to the academic debates about the 
future of comparative education. The journal emerged at a time when 
comparative educators in the United States were engaged in a complex 
process of reexamining historical foundations of the field, exploring its 
methodological and theoretical directions, and expanding its geographic 
boundaries. While the debate on comparative education theory and 
method was often polarized into two extremes—advocating for either 
cross-national, quantitative research or single-case, historical studies—

at the College of Education, Lehigh University, Pennsylvania. Dr. Silova’s research 
focuses on the study of globalization, democratization, and policy “borrowing” in

study attempts to trace the journal’s history. The fortieth anniversary of 
the establishment of European Education provides a unique opportunity 
to reflect on the journal’s role in shaping the contours of comparative 
education during the past four decades. Through content analysis and 
interviews with former editors and advisory board members, this study 
discusses the context within which the journal emerged, its original goals 
and changing vision during the past forty years, as well as its role in the 
field of comparative and international education. 
A retrospective: “A vital addition” to comparative education
Commenting on the establishment of the journal in 1969, Professor 
George Bereday
2 welcomed Western European Education as “a vital ad-
dition to the library of students of comparative education” (1969, p. 6). In 
addition to expanding the field of comparative education, the journal also 
aimed to advance a set of new ideas in the ongoing process of redefining 
the contours of comparative education. First, it attempted to expand the 
geographical areas of comparative education research by bringing back

Western European perspectives to the field, which had increasingly 
been dominated by the study of education in the Soviet Union and 
nonaligned countries during the cold war. Second, the journal aimed to 
advocate for multiple case-study research methodology as a response 
to the traditional overemphasis on single-case study analysis and the 
growing interest in quantitative cross-national studies of the 1960s. As 
such, the journal was skillfully positioned to make contributions to the 
ongoing debates about the future of the comparative education field in 
the 1960s and 1970s.
Expanding the geographic focus of comparative research 
Western European Education was established at a time when comparative 
education began to lose its European-centric focus by shifting research 
agenda toward the Soviet Union and the nonaligned countries in the 
context of the cold war. Fueled by an immense federal funding through 
the National Defense Education Act (NDEA), area studies (especially 
Soviet studies) were considered crucial in stimulating the advancement 
of education in science, mathematics, and foreign languages in order to 
“keep the United States ahead of the Soviet Union through education” 
and ultimately “help the country win the Cold War” (Noah, 2006, p. 10; 
Tyack & Cuban, 1995).

It furthered the academic study of foreign societies and their educational

Steiner-Khamsi (2006) described “the development turn” in comparative 
and international education, which was characterized by “the greatest 
territorial gain” both in terms of geographic areas of comparative re-
search (as reflected in the increasing focus on the study of education in 
nonaligned countries) and higher education programs (as reflected in the 
growth of new degree programs across the United States).
While the boom of area studies played a major role in the develop-
ment of comparative education as a field, American preoccupation with 
Soviet studies (and area studies more generally) had at the same time 
revealed some tensions within the emerging field of comparative and 
international education. In particular, the cold war, and “the development 
turn” associated with it, had had major consequences for the selection 
of target countries for comparative education research, “averting atten-
tion from high-income countries and directing attention to low-income 
countries” instead (Steiner-Khamsi, 2006, p. 40). In particular, academic 
interests of the “founding fathers” of comparative education in the United 
States (including William Johnson, George Bereday, George Counts, 
William Brickman, and others) reflected an “overemphasis” on Soviet 
area studies (personal correspondence between Bereday and Cronin,  
29 July 1957). Similarly, the contrastive analyses between education in 
the United States and the Soviet Union were placed at the center stage 
of the founding meeting of the Comparative Education Society (CES)

Before the heyday of language and area studies, U.S. comparative and

It is within this context of near obsession with Soviet studies and 
increasing emphasis on education development in the Third World (pri-
marily aimed at the study of education development in nonaligned coun-
tries) that Western European Education was established at the end of the 
1960s. The idea for the journal emerged at Teachers College, Columbia 
University as a result of a close collaboration between Professor George 
Bereday (who subsequently became one of the journal advisory board 
members) and Professor Ursula Springer (a former student of George 
Bereday and a founding editor of the journal). At the time of the journal’s 
establishment, Professor Springer taught at Brooklyn College and was 
also an active member of the Comparative and International Education 
Society, serving on its board of directors during 1969–71. Explaining the 
rationale for establishing the journal, she emphasized the need to revive 
interest in the study of education in Europe: 
The interest in Europe was remarkably limited. The interest in the Soviet 
realm was more lively, which had a lot to do with the political atmosphere. 
When I was working on the Western European Education journal, Europe 
was only of interest to students who wanted to travel there. . . . There 
was no genuine academic interest in European education. That interest 
existed in the heads of only a few people. (Personal communication,  
29 August 2008)

Berliner and a fluent speaker of German, English, Italian, and French,

From single country case-studies to multiple case-study 
comparison 
In addition to expanding the geographic scope of scholarship in com-
parative and international education during the cold war, Western Euro-
pean Education also attempted to make a statement on methodological 
debates in the comparative education field. In the 1960s, the debate on 
comparative methods revolved around the value of single country case 
studies versus cross-national comparative analysis, or historiography 
versus “scientific” approaches to comparative analysis. In the period 
1957–65, Comparative Education Review alone published more than 
twenty-five articles on the theory and method of comparative educa-
tion (Kazamias & Schwartz, 1977) and the Western European Educa-
tion journal aimed to make significant contributions to these debates. 
Crucial to the discussion of methodological issues were the following 
questions: Does comparative education have a unique methodology, 
which is distinct from other social science disciplines? Is comparative 
education a discipline and should it become one? What type of compari-
son will make comparative education more “scientific”? What type of

SPRiNG  2009 23
comparison will be most meaningful as comparative education matures 
as an academic field? 
Historically, the use of single country as the unit of analysis had been 
common in the field of comparative education. For example, Bray notes 
that the use of nation-state had been a “dominant unit of analysis since 
the emergence of the field as a distinct domain of inquiry” (2008, p. 305), 
dating back to early seminal works in the field (Hans, 1950; Kandel, 
1933a; Sadler, 1900). In the 1950s and 1960s, this tradition had found 
support among the group of prominent comparative education scholars, 
including Professor Isaac Kandel from Columbia University Teachers 
College, Professor Robert Ulich from Harvard University, Professor 
William Brickman from New york University, and many others. For 
example, Kandel perceived comparative education as the “continua-
tion of the study of the history of education into the present” (1933b, 
p. xix). In Ulich’s view, comparative education was “unapologetically 
and thoroughly historical,” because he believed that it was impossible 
to comprehend the nature of educational processes without understand-
ing its historical context (Nash, 1977, p. 148).
6 It was precisely the fear 
of “extreme historicism” that made some scholars anxious about the 
future of comparative education (Anderson, 1961, p. 4). In the words of 
Kazamias and Schwartz, the attempts to show that systems were “his-
torically determined, different from each other, and quite unique” would 
make comparison (and by extension comparative education) impossible 
(1977, p. 397). 
While the historical tradition of comparative education did not abandon 
comparative research but rather embedded it in contextual comparison, 
different perspectives emerged in the late 1950s and began to dominate 
the field in the 1960s. For example, the contours of comparative education 
were reshaped by the growing emphasis on international development, 
resulting in the increasing use of education as an instrument of political, 
economic, and social development. In the 1960s and 1970s, for example, 
many studies followed the modernization narrative to examine the prac-
tice of development (often from the perspective of structural function-
alism and economics’ human capital theory). While this has inevitably 
reinforced the use of the nation-state as a main unit of analysis in compara-
tive research, it shifted comparative education inquiry toward the issues of  
education efficiency and quantitative analysis rather than a broader understand-
ing of pedagogical aspects of schooling (Silova & Brehm, 2009).
7 
At the same time, the comparative education field became preoccupied

with the desire to become “scientific,” as epitomized by Harold Noah’s 
and Max Eckstein’s (1969) book, Toward a Science of Comparative Edu-
cation. Those who advocated for infusing comparative education with 
social science method and techniques conceived comparative education 
as “dealing with objectives, measurable and concrete levels of reality 
which, in principle, at least, existed independent of observer” (Kazamias 
& Schwartz, 1977, p. 167). The use of scientific, value-free approach 
is best exemplified by the research initiated in the early 1960s by the 
International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievements 
(IEA). Not only were the IEA studies perceived as value-free, empirical, 
and therefore “scientific,” but they were also based on a cross-national 
comparison, thus signaling a radical departure from the tradition emphasis 
on single case studies and historiography in comparative education. 
In a way, the debate on theory and method became polarized into two 
extremes, with “new” approaches on one end of the spectrum and “old” 
ones on the other. As Kazamias & Schwarz described, the methodological 
literature echoed “calls for an approach that would be more systemic than 
impressionistic, empirical and analytical than speculative and descrip-
tive, microcosmic than macrocosmic, and scientific than philosophical or 
historical” (1977, p. 163). Undeniably, some scholars (including George 
Bereday, Andreas Kazamias, Ursula Springer, and others) searched for a 
synthesis of these approaches, envisioning comparative education as an 
interdisciplinary area of study that would at once capture the importance 
of the historical context, while at the same time advance the use of social 
science methods in comparative education.
8 
Reluctant to abandon the historical dimensions of the field yet un-
derstanding the value of cross-national comparison, the founding editor 
(Ursula Springer) and the advisory board members (including George 
Bereday) of the Western European Education journal made a bold at-
tempt to reconceptualize the debate on comparative methods by using the 
newly created journal to advance another alternative–multiple case-study 
analysis in comparative education research. The idea was to publish the-
matic journal issues, which would feature qualitative country case-studies 
tightly embedded in their political, economic, and social contexts, yet 
allow an opportunity for the editor to compare educational issues across 
different contexts and cases. More specifically, Springer explained: 
The reason for having thematic issues was to provide a topic that could be 
used for practical functions. Since it was a comparative journal, it provided 
not only information on, for example, what the peasants do in southern 
=== PAGE 9 ===
SPRiNG  2009 25
Italy, but also information on peasant farmers in France and Germany. 
I, as the editor, introduced the topic and the comparisons. There would 
be a marvelous summary in a broader sense. This was really a terrific 
instrument to enrich the comparative method. (Personal communication, 
29 August 2008)
Since its establishment in 1969, the journal has attempted to adhere to 
its original goal of emphasizing the value of the historical context while 
encouraging cross-national and cross-case comparison. For example, 
the first issue focused on comparative analysis of curriculum design by 
introducing national case-studies from Sweden, West Germany, England, 
France, and Italy, which were preceded by an editorial introduction sum-
marizing and analyzing similarities and differences across cases. Most 
subsequent journal editors followed the same principle as they brought 
together contextually embedded case-studies on specific themes—ranging 
from education policy and university autonomy to teacher education 
reform—to create opportunities to engage in a comparative analysis that is 
both cross-national and contextual (Silova & Brehm, 2009). While some 
editors experimented with different organization of journal issues, the 
overall commitment to embed multiple case-study analysis contextually 
remained consistent throughout the four decades. For example, William 
Brickman occasionally wrote individual introductions to each article 
instead of an overall introduction to the journal issue, and attempted to 
structure journal issues in a way that would allow the readers to make 
their own comparative analyses. As Springer eloquently put it, the aspira-
tions in this context were those of “broad understanding and pragmatic 
utility, rather than scientific elegance” (1977, p. 361).
Opening up “a gateway to the world” for European scholarship
One of the main goals and major contributions of Western European Edu-
cation was to open the field of comparative and international education to 
more faculty and students in the United States and Europe, especially to 
those who may not have the necessary language skills to conduct research 
in original languages. In fact, Western European Education was originally 
established as “the journal of translations” (Springer, 1969a, p. 4), which 
provided access to original sources (translated from foreign languages) 
for an English-speaking audience. For example, William Brickman (1981) 
explained how language remained a barrier to disseminating scholarship 
on European education because “the countless educational readers . . .

English” (1981, p. 4). To address this gap, one of the journal’s goals was 
to provide European and American scholars with access to “an informa-

Springer explained: “Europeans have become accustomed to look be-

stated that “Americans, too, may want to extend their views beyond their

(1969a, p. 3). The idea of opening up the field of comparative educa-
tion was echoed by the advisory board members. For example, George 
Bereday (1969) emphasized that the journal was “a vital addition to the 
library of students in comparative education who cannot read in the origi-

with primary sources” (1969, p. 6). Similarly, Saul Robinsohn, a former

The idea of acquainting American educators with developments in Eu-

and of research procedures within large sections of the social—and espe-
cially the educational—sciences might now give way to a more balanced 
exchange. Such an exchange, to judge from earlier experience, may well 
prove a source of critical assessment and, hopefully, even of inspiration

While opening up opportunities for U.S. scholars to get acquainted 
with European research, the journal also aimed to provide opportunities 
for non-English-speaking European academics to make their research 
available to the English-speaking audience in the United States and in-
ternationally. The journal promised “a gateway to the world,” as Philip 
Idenburg, a Dutch educator and advisory board member, stated:
When I mention “a gateway to the world,” I was thinking more of the fact 
that too little of what is going on in the Netherlands reaches the outside 
world. . . . In general it may be said that fairly considerable but rather un-

In other words, the journal’s establishment opened up comparative 
education to more academics and students by helping them overcome 
the linguistic and geographic obstacles. Harold Noah, who served 
as the editor of Soviet Education and observed the development of  
M.E. Sharpe’s education journals over the decades, explained, “the  
[M.E. Sharpe] journals definitely have a niche: they give those who can-
not read Russian, French, German, and other languages a glimpse into 
what is being done in the way of research about youth and education in 
some other countries” (personal communication, 19 January 2009). It 
provided access to relevant primary sources, documents, and research that 
were otherwise not available through bibliographic channels (Springer, 
1969b). At the same time, the journal provided an opportunity for Eu-
ropean academics to share their research beyond Europe’s borders and, 
more importantly, created a bridge between academics in the United 
States and Europe as they engaged in the debates about the past, present, 
and future of European education. 
A prospective: Continuity and change in European Education
European Education emerged at a time when comparative educators in 
the United States were engaged in a complex process of reexamining 
historical foundations of the field, exploring its methodological and 
theoretical directions, and expanding its geographic boundaries. Forty 
years later, the academic debate on comparative theory and method is far 
from over, as comparative educators continue to ponder the importance 
of history and context, as well as quantitative and qualitative research 
methodology, in explaining education phenomena in Europe and beyond 
its borders. The onset of globalization (whether real or imagined) has 
further intensified the debate about the importance of local agency and 
context in comparative research, and European Education has remained 
an active participant in the ongoing conversation about the past and future 
of comparative education.

As the journal celebrates its fortieth anniversary, the theoretical and 
methodological frontiers of comparative education continue to change 
as rapidly as the geographic borders of Europe itself. In this context, the 
journal’s contributions to an increasingly complex debate on comparative 
theory and method could be characterized by both continuity and change. 
On the one hand, the journal continues to adhere to its original mission of 
envisioning comparative education as an interdisciplinary area of study 
that encourages scholars to engage in a comparative analysis that is both 
cross-national and contextual. This original commitment of emphasizing 
the value of the historical context while encouraging comparative analysis 
has remained key to the journal’s mission. What has changed, however, 
is that the journal seeks actively to engage in an ongoing dialogue by 
providing an intellectual space to explore alternative theories and meth-
odologies that would capture the nuances of global and local contexts 
through both qualitative and quantitative comparative research. 
Furthermore, the context itself has become increasingly complex as 
Europe’s borders are expanding and its education systems experience the 
pressures of globalization. While the journal continues to provide what 
Philip J. Idenburg (1969) called “gateways to the world” for the sharing 
of knowledge and insight across national contexts, it is also expanding its 
geographic reach and analytic scope. In the next decades, the journal aims 
to extend its scope beyond the “old Europe” to all forty-seven member 
states of the Council of Europe to capture the complexity of transnational 
factors and global imprints on particular educational policies and practices 
in different contexts. As one of the advisory board members, Noah Sobe 
(2009), points out in his introductory greetings to this anniversary issue, 
“instead of merely trying to ascertain what the world can learn from Eu-
rope, our present task is to explore what we can learn from Europe about 
the world.” The idea of extending our analyses beyond Europe’s borders 
is also echoed by Guntars Catlaks (2009) who encourages the journal to 
“remain fully aware of our continent’s education roots, and increasingly 
be able to place education issues in the real context of time—that of the 
world.” It is in this dynamic and increasingly interdependent context of 
the world that European Education sets out to explore the new academic 
frontiers in comparative scholarship on European education.
