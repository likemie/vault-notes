ABSTRACT  
The peidu trend has become prevalent in rural China in the last 
decade, with many rural children seen studying in urban schools 
accompanied by their adult family members in county capitals. 
Essentially, peidu is a practice of migration for school choice. 
However, unlike in the international context, this school choice 
phenomenon in rural China is not driven by marketisation reform, 
but by a strategy of the local government to achieve 
urbanisation. Based on questionnaires and interviews, this study 
reveals that peidu is stratified by family socioeconomic status. As 
the rural middle and top strata migrate out of the countryside by 
choosing urban schools for their children, rural schools further 
deteriorate and children of the most disadvantaged families are 
left behind in deeper struggles. Similar to school choice effects in 
other countries, peidu reinforces the rural-urban educational 
disparities and calls into question the urbanisation strategy in 
China.

School choice – whereby parents choose school for their children – has long been 
common across the global (Ball 2003; Wu 2012). In rural China, however, it was not 
prevalent until the 2010s, when village schools were closed in large number (Zhang 
2019) and a strong educational desire boosted rural families’ investment in education

(Wang and Teng 2022). Over the past decade, school choice has spread to rural China with 
the emergence of a rural-to-urban peidu trend.
In rural China, an increasing number of rural families choose urban schools in county 
capitals for their children to receive education. Disappointed with the poor academic 
achievement of rural schools, many rural families abandon village schools and enrol 
their children in better performing urban schools in county capitals (Lei 2018; Pang 
et al. 2017). Indeed, urban schools still outperform rural schools because of better 
human resources and more effective school processes (Li, Shi, and Xue 2020; Wang 
et al. 2017), despite the significantly narrowed gaps in budget and school facilities 
(Ministry of Education 2020). Urban schools attract better teaching staff, who are 
more experienced and motivated and with formal training (Li, Shi, and Xue 2020). 
While gaps in hardware keep reducing between rural and urban schools, the essential 
disparities in personnel and expertise continue to persist, hence the sharp differences 
in student learning outcomes (Zhang et al. 2018). No doubt many rural families tend 
to choose urban schools in county capitals, hoping to give their children a higher start­
ing point.
Peidu is the result of this parental school choice. The long distance between the home 
village and the chosen urban school makes daily commute impossible. Therefore, the 
rural family needs to either buy or rent an apartment near the urban school and spare 
at least one adult, most often the mother or a grandparent, to stay with the child in 
the county capital. Hence the term, peidu, which literally means ‘accompanying children 
to study’, involves rural adults relocating to urban areas to accommodate their children’s 
education (Chen and Liang 2015; Pang et al. 2017). Due to the relocation from rural vil­
lages to urban areas, this phenomenon appears to be largely a new form of school 
choice through migration conducted by rural parents.
Peidu has spread widely across rural China over the past decade. One-fourth of rural 
children in Yulin, Shanxi Province, were peidu children studying in urban primary 
schools in 2013 (Pang et al. 2017). In Xinyi City, Jiangsu Province (located in economically 
developed eastern China), 69.21% of primary students studied in urban areas in 2017 
(Ding, Luo, and Wang 2019). In the village of Gang in Ping, Jiangxi, two thirds of rural 
families in 2020 had decided to choose the primary school in the county capital 
instead of the village school for their children (Zhang and Zhu 2021). In short, the litera­
ture suggests that some rural schools have lost between two fifths and two thirds of the 
school-aged children in their catchment areas to urban schools.
Research into peidu remains scarce, and the limited empirical studies of this topic have 
mainly discussed its impact on individuals and families, such as the effects on children’s 
academic performance (Long et al. 2020); on peidu adults’ social adaptation and parents’ 
marital relationships (Chen and Liang 2015; Ding, Luo, and Wang 2019); and on the 
families’ economic burdens (Lei 2018). The current study takes a different approach by 
treating peidu phenomenon as a form of school choice, which, as found in the inter­
national literature, is a socially stratified practice (Ball 2003; Debs and Cheung 2021). Illu­
minated by the school choice literature, this study investigates how family economic, 
cultural and social capital influence rural parents’ school choice in the form of peidu, 
and how this type of school choice further affects the educational disparities between 
rural and urban schools.

School choice in the international context: marketisation and social 
stratification
School choice has remained a popular phenomenon across the world for decades. In 
many countries, both developed (e.g. the United States, the United Kingdom, Sweden) 
and developing (e.g. Chile, Colombia, Bangladesh), school choice is an important ‘top- 
down’ educational policy under neoliberal reform (Adnett and Davies 2003; Cookson 
and Shapira 1997). School choice policies reflect government attempts to create a com­
petitive educational market to replace the monopolistic state educational system, a strat­
egy claimed to improve students’ academic performance, reduce inequality and increase 
educational diversity (Friedman 1962; Levin and Belfield 2003). In many parts of the world, 
school choice is an outcome of marketisation in public education (Adnett and Davies 
2003; Friedman 1962).
Contrary to the original policy intentions, empirical studies have criticised the market- 
based school choice reform for undermining educational equality and reinforcing social 
stratification, because family economic, social and cultural capital is found to be crucial 
in school choice (Ball 2003; Debs and Cheung 2021).
Family economic capital significantly contributes to the process and results of school 
choice. Economic concerns, such as uniform requirements and the cost of long commute 
to and from school, are important considerations influencing working-class parents’ 
choice of schools (Lareau and Goyette 2014). More importantly, economic capital has 
been found to enable well-off parents to gain access to a chosen school by purchasing 
a nearby residence (Gabay-Egozi 2016). In contrast, low-income parents are trapped in 
schools within their neighbourhood (Lareau and Goyette 2014). A stronger financial 
condition also enables parents to send their children to extracurricular classes or 
arrange private tutoring to promote their children’s special talents or academic test 
scores, in preparation for admission to talent programmes or dream schools (Roda 
2017).
Cultural capital also plays critical roles in school choice. Parents who are better edu­
cated are more familiar with the educational system and the school choice market, 
which benefits their children’s school selection (Reay 2004). Children from families with 
higher levels of cultural capital are more likely to pass school selection procedures, 
such as interviews and entrance exams (Carrasco, Gutierrez, and Flores 2017). Compared 
with working-class parents, upper- and middle-class parents are found more likely to 
choose schools that offer abundant curricula in languages, music, art and sport and 
talent programmes to improve their children’s cultural capital for obtaining upper- or 
middle-class careers and lifestyles (Bereményi and Carrasco 2018; Smala, Paz, and 
Lingard 2013).
Social capital, i.e. family’s social connections and networks, also affects likelihood of 
success in school choice. Studies conducted in the United Kingdom and the United 
States have shown that middle-class parents can obtain intimate knowledge and infor­
mation from their large and higher status social circles to increase their children’s 
success in the education market (Ball 2003; Bell 2009). In contrast, working-class 
parents lack the effective social networks through which to obtain accurate data (such 
as class sizes and test scores) that are important for school choice, thus restricting their 
children to a limited number of schools (Alegre and Benito 2012).

The literature suggests that school choice is a socially stratified practice with its out­
comes largely influenced by family economic, social and cultural capital. Children from 
wealthier families are more likely to enrol in high-performing schools, leaving children 
from working-class or poor families concentrated in struggling schools (Bell 2009). 
School choice is therefore likely to deepen the social divide among schools and reinforce 
class inequality (Ball 2003).
Peidu, the rural school choice in China, exhibits similar inclination in class stratification 
since studying and living in county capitals obviously engender extra costs. However, 
unlike school choice in other parts of the world, peidu in China is not prompted by a 
market-driven educational policy. Instead, it is induced by the state-led social engineering 
of urbanisation.
School choice in rural China: Peidu for urbanisation
Unlike the market-based reform of school choice in international background, school 
choice during the compulsory education period is not permitted by the Chinese govern­
ment. Under the national policy, Chinese children are required to attend neighbourhood 
schools according to residence registration, i.e. the widely known hukou system (National 
People’s Congress 1986). Since implementation in 1950s, the hukou system has restricted 
rural-urban migration and secured a rural-urban segregation in job opportunities and 
welfare service (education included) for decades (Lin 2006). In the early years, the 
hukou system used to be an insurmountable barrier for rural children to attend urban 
public schools. However, in 2014, a hukou reform was launched by the Chinese govern­
ment to allow migrants to obtain a local hukou in all but a few of the country’s megacities 
(China’s State Council 2014). The purpose of the reform was to promote the new urban­
isation, which aims at reducing rural population and turning them into urban residents 
(Zhan 2017).
Hukou still matters, but its importance varies tremendously among different levels of 
urbanities. Megacities like Beijing and Shanghai continue with harsh controls over house­
hold registration. A variety of ‘point systems’ (jifenzhi) are introduced to evaluate qualifi­
cations of applicants for local hukou, which implicitly favour the middle- or upper-classes 
with higher education and stable employment and put rural migrants in disadvantage 
(Chen and Wang 2021; Wan and Vickers 2022). In other words, the role of hukou in popu­
lation control has changed. In megacities, the rural population is subject to dual discrimi­
nation, overt discrimination by hukou status and tacit discrimination by socioeconomic 
standing. On the contrary, small cities welcome the rural population and thresholds to 
access local urban hukou are much lower. Public facilities such as schools, hospitals, 
libraries, etc. are open to residents in small cities disregarding the hukou status. For the 
rural population, access to quality public facilities in small cities depends more on the 
affordability of urban living than on hukou registration.
This change of hukou policy has reshaped the educational landscape of the rural popu­
lation. On the one hand, rural migrant workers, shut out by public schools in large metro­
polis, decide to send their children back to small cities (county capitals) in their birth 
province, where quality public schools welcome them (Han and Yu 2020). On the other 
hand, rural left-behind children also flock into county capitals because of rural school con­
solidation and expansion of urban schools in the county. Rural population rapidly

concentrates in county urban districts; education becomes a tool to achieve the goal of 
urbanisation.
Rural school consolidation started in early 2000s and has resulted in massive closure of 
rural primary schools. From 2000 to 2022, the number of rural primary schools decreased 
by 83% from 440,284 to 76,093 (Ministry of Education 2022). Rural children were often 
assigned to nearby township schools, most of which were far from students’ homes 
and poor in academic performance. Some better-off rural families abandoned township 
schools and found ways to enter county capital schools, triggering the peidu trend 
(Chen and Liang 2015; Pang et al. 2017).
Earlier school consolidation might be stimulated by concerns about resource 
efficiency. However, soon local governments realised that the influx of these better-off 
peidu families benefits the urbanisation of small cities. Both Ye (2017) and Zhang 
(2019) argued that the mass closure of rural schools was intentionally conducted by 
the local government to push rural people into small cities. Meanwhile, education 
parks were established in county capitals to accommodate rural students (Zhang 2019). 
Rural school closure coupled with expansion of county urban schools became an impor­
tant strategy to achieve the urbanisation goal in small cities since one child studying in 
urban schools would bring at least one adult or even the whole family into the city 
(Hillman 2013; Zhang 2019). For example, the officials in Dapu, Guangdong province, 
strongly embraced the strategy in the remark that ‘To develop Dapu, you should bring 
all rural children into the county capital’ (Zhang 2019, 71). Urbanisation by concentration 
of schools has become the template for local governments and is supported by the 
central government’s policy where it clearly states that ‘school consolidation should be 
in coordinated with the urbanisation plan’ (China’s State Council 2001).
From the perspective of rural families, the urbanisation strategy has opened the 
window for school choice. Rural children with means now have the opportunity to 
enter county capital schools where subjects are offered in full, school activities richer, 
and instructional quality higher (Wang et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2018). Such school 
choice of rural population inevitably entails the phenomenon of peidu due to the vast 
space in rural areas that often makes daily home-school commute impossible.
Apparently, peidu, school choice in rural China, takes place in an entirely different 
policy context from that documented in the international literature. It is urbanisation 
rather than marketisation that drives school choice in rural China. Despite the radically 
different rationale, policymakers in both contexts are similar in taking faith in the 
program for addressing the inequality issues. In the West, market competition is believed 
to be a silver bullet to boost quality of low-performing schools (Chubb and Moe 1990; 
Friedman 1962). Similarly, in China government officials believe that urbanisation of edu­
cation reduces rural-urban inequality because rural students can enjoy an equal start by 
enrolling in better urban schools (Zhang 2019). However, the international literature has 
reminded us that school choice is a socially stratified practice and tends to reinforce 
inequality among schools (Ball 2003; Bell 2009). We suspect that similar outcomes may 
arise in rural China, where peidu draws away more affluent rural families, plunging rural 
schools into deeper crises. Empirical research on this topic is scarce if not entirely 
absent. This study aims to fill in this gap by examining peidu in relation to rural social stra­
tification and exploring the consequences of peidu on rural schools.

Data from County G
County G is located in Hubei province of central China, with a population of 358,109 in 
2018, 75% of whom were rural residents (Prefecture City Statistics Bureau 2019). School 
choice is widespread in County G (Prefecture City Evening News 2014), making the 
peidu phenomenon in this county typical of rural China.
The study focuses on primary schools. Migration for school choice, or peidu is more 
common at the primary school level, compared with later school levels, because children 
of this age need more care. In 2018, twelve primary schools were selected to participate 
upon their principals’ acceptance. The selected schools comprised all three public schools 
in the county capital, three in townships and six village schools (five full-grade schools and 
one teaching point) spread across the three towns. We revisited the field site in 2023, and 
included in the sample the fourth public school in the county capital, which was newly 
established in 2021 to offer more places for the increasing peidu children. Township 
schools were included because rural children were often assigned to township schools 
when village schools were closed down. Township schools were usually considered 
better than village schools but inferior to county capital schools.
Teachers were recruited from the selected schools on a voluntary basis. With teachers’ help 
and through snowball sampling, three groups of rural families were then selected: peidu 
families in the county capital, peidu families in townships and non-peidu families in villages. 
Qualitative and quantitative data were collected from questionnaires and interviews, sup­
plemented by information from school archives. Table 1 provides the sample information.

Meanwhile, the local government actively shut down more small-scale village schools. 
For instance, Village School 8 in our sample was closed by the government in 2022, and 
most of the students were assigned to School X. The government openly admits and pro­
motes the school-closure-for-urbanisation strategy because it is believed to be a ben­
eficial cause for rural families: 
The government encourages rural residents to move to urban areas … Through peidu, they 
can easily come to urban areas. Our county seat is expanding. Education should meet the 
needs of urbanisation and establish more schools for those coming to urban areas. 
(O-Gong, the vice director of the local Education Bureau)
We told the parents in School 8 about the advantages of the new county seat school [School 
X]. Teacher quality of the new school is much better than School 8. The parents were happy 
that School 8 was closed so their children can study in a better school. (O-Long)
Indeed, school quality was the top reason for peidu. 72% of peidu families in our ques­
tionnaire survey selected ‘education quality’ as the reason for relocation (82% of peidu 
families in the county capital and 48% of those in the townships). Five out of 100 inter­
viewed peidu adults even transferred their children from poor-performing schools in 
large cities like Shenzhen to the county capital school in County G. This move came as 
their children were excluded from reputable public schools in megacities. Due to the 
long home-school distance, coupled with the absence of a school bus or boarding 
service, almost all rural families who opt for urban schools arrange for one adult to accom­
pany their children. The mothers or grandparents usually became peidu adults, while the 
father or in many cases both the parents worked as migrant workers in large cities to 
support the living expenses. The questionnaire indicated that mothers and grandparents 
accounted for 42% (206) and 53% (260) of the 494 peidu adults, respectively.
Nevertheless, not all rural children could enjoy this newly granted privilege. Although the 
restriction on household registration (hukou) is relaxed, access to urban schools hinges more 
on family capital. It is especially so if one would like to enter the elite urban school (key 
school). Thus the urbanisation project tends to attract into urban areas rural families with 
higher financial and social resources, as the next section will show.
Peidu and rural social stratification
Echoing the international literature, peidu as school choice was also found to be a socially 
stratified practice in County G. Family’s economic and social capital played pivotal roles in 
determining if and where to peidu. In general, peidu families in the county capital pos­
sessed more resources than those in township schools, leaving the most needy families 
in village schools.
Economic capital
A significant financial investment was necessary to accompany children in the county 
capital or township, to cover rent, daily living expenses and, in some cases, extracurricular 
tutoring.
Housing was one of the most important costs for peidu families, with an average annual 
rent in 2018 of RMB4,035 in the county capital and RMB2,133 in the townships. Apart from

housing, the annual expenditure on food and necessities averaged RMB17,290 for the 
peidu families in the county capital and RMB10,500 for those in townships. In total, the 
mean annual cost in 2018 was RMB21,325 for a peidu family in the county capital and 
RMB12,633 for a peidu family in townships (see Figure 1 in Supplementary Materials). 
When we revisit County G in 2023, the annual rent increased to a minimum of 
RMB7,000 in the county capital.
The questionnaire data revealed that 49% of peidu families in the county capital and 
17% of those in the townships sent their children to extracurricular classes. Specifically, 
29% of peidu families in the county capital and 15% of those in the townships sent 
their children to academic tutoring to help their children finish homework and improve 
academic performance. The cost for one academic tutoring for one semester ranged 
from RMB1,000 to RMB2,000 in the county capital and from RMB600 to RMB1,200 in 
the townships. Peidu parents in the county capital, especially the young and better edu­
cated ones, also put children in non-academic after-school classes. These peidu adults had 
already subscribed to the middle-class concept of ‘whole-person development’ and were 
willing to make investment in their children. According to the questionnaire survey, 30% 
of peidu adults in the county capital had enrolled their children in hobby classes, such as 
drawing and dance, whereas only four of the 141 peidu adults in the townships and none 
in the village had done so. The cost for one hobby class was between RMB1,200 and 
RMB2,800 per semester. A few peidu adults sent their children to two or more hobby 
classes. For example, S-Lirui’s mother chose three interest classes including basketball, 
table tennis and swimming for her son, costing a total of around RMB3000 one semester. 
This family also sent the child to the English tutoring class, costing RMB1500 half a year. 
Thus, the total cost on extracurricular classes for this family reached to RMB9000 every 
year.
In contrast, the average annual income of rural residents in County G was merely 
RMB10,340 per capita in 2018, inclusive of remittances from migrant workers in large 
cities (Prefecture City Statistics Bureau 2019). Therefore, the high cost of urban living 
and extracurricular classes was a deterrent for poor rural families choosing schools in 
townships and the county capital. The questionnaire data supported this, showing that 
financial stringency had impeded 60% of rural non-peidu families from choosing an 
urban school. Evidently, the impoverished rural families had no choice but to accept 
the village schools.
Social capital
Economic capital decided if peidu would be financially viable for rural families to live in 
urban districts, but social capital played a vital role in the choice of schools. Most rural 
school choice took place within the public school system with free tuition so the 
financial barrier to access urban schools was low. Nonetheless, the quality of public 
schools also varied, with the prestigious key school (School 1) being much more selective 
than the recently built Schools 3 and X. Hence, which urban school peidu children could 
enter heavily depended on family’s social connections.
In the county capital, forty-two of the 48 interviewed peidu families from School 1, 2 
and 3 had mobilised their social connections with target school staff for accessing their 
desired schools. For the choice of township school, 16 of the 22 interviewed families

My aunt-in-law teaches at School 2. I told her that I wanted to send my son to the county 
capital school in the summer holiday and asked her for help. Before the registration day, 
she told me to register the next day. Then I successfully chose School 2 for my son. It was 
easy. (S-Jun’s mother)
Indirect ties could work as well. Another 14 peidu families (12 in the county capital and 
two in the townships) who had relatives or friends who happened to know the adminis­
trative staff in the target schools also managed to win the school places for their children.
However, only families with strong and powerful connections could secure a school 
place in the most selective, prestigious School 1. For instance, a direct tie with a distant 
relative teaching in School 1 might not be considered strong enough. Teacher Cheng 
in School 1 was begged by one of her distant relatives for admission into School 1. She 
told us that ‘I did not want to trouble my principal … [because] the child was not my 
nephew or niece’. So she referred the child to the vice-principal of School 2, a non-key 
school where the child was accepted.
In contrast, an indirect tie through a powerful intermediary with a wide network of 
authorities was perceived to be most effective. For example, a peidu parent in School 1 
stated: 
My elder brother works in the Forestry Bureau. He knows many people including the principal 
of School 1. Before the summer break I told him that I wanted to choose School 1 for my son. 
Then my son was admitted into School 1. (S-Ke’s mother)
Social connections like in this case with public sector employees or authorities were con­
sidered strong and powerful enough to secure a place in the renowned School 1 in the 
county capital, as confirmed by both teachers and peidu parents.
Obviously, rural families with different social circles were stratified along the varied 
qualities of schools. Families well connected with powerful authorities were likely concen­
trated in the most selective key school, while others with some social ties were streamed 
into less reputable urban schools, leaving behind the families without financial means or 
social capital in least desired township or village schools.
Verification of family capital effects
The above findings from interviews were further verified with statistical models drawing 
from the questionnaire data. First, a comparison of family socioeconomic status (SES) was 
COMPARATIVE EDUCATION 
309
conducted among the three groups: peidu families in the county capital, peidu families in 
townships, and non-peidu families. Family SES was a composite index measured by family 
income, highest education level and highest occupational status of parents. The findings 
confirmed that peidu families overall had a significantly higher SES score (n = 494, M =  
0.181, SD = 0.961) than non-peidu families (n = 320, M = −0.279, SD = 0.996), t(812) =  
6.566, p < 0.001. More nuanced comparison of the three groups showed that the peidu 
families in the county capital (n = 353, M = 0.280, SD = 0.982) had a higher SES than 
those in the townships (n = 141, M = −0.069, SD = 0.861), t(492) = 3.690, p < 0.001, who 
in turn had a higher SES than the non-peidu families in villages, t(459) = 2.172, p < 0.05. 
Also, the peidu families with children attending the top school, School 1 (n = 167, M =  
0.572, SD = 0.984), had a significantly higher SES than those with children attending 
non-key schools in the county capital (n = 186, M = 0.018, SD = 0.906), t(351) = 5.506, 
p < 0.001. The comparisons depicted the ladder of social strata in the peidu phenomenon: 
family SES in the key school > in non-key schools in the county capital > in township 
schools > in village schools (see Figure 2 in Supplementary Materials).
Second, binary logistic regression models were run to specify the effects of family capital 
on rural parents’ school choice (see Table 4). Model 1 compared peidu families with non-peidu 
families. It found that peidu families tended to have a higher annual income (over RMB80,000), 
higher parental education (post-compulsory level), higher occupational status (non-farming), 
and more social connections with public sector employees.
Model 2 compared peidu families in the townships with those in the county capital. 
Compared with Model 1, the effects of income and occupational status were muted. 
Instead, cultural (education) and social capital were most important in accessing 
county capital schools.
Model 3 predicted the probability of getting into the key primary school for the peidu 
families in the county capital. Again, family income, parents’ post-compulsory education 
and social connections all exhibited significant effects.
Interestingly, parental cultural knowledge was not found to be a decisive factor in the 
rural school choice process, contrary to its influential role identified in the international 
literature (Bereményi and Carrasco 2018). Neither did the interviewees mention knowl­
edge about school preferences, school interview skills, or personal tastes in the school 
selection process. There were possibly two reasons. For one, the school admission 
process was simple, as it did not involve interview or other evaluative measures. There­
fore, subtle cultural know-how was unnecessary for entering schools in the county 
capital. Secondly, the ranking of all the four public schools in the county capital was trans­
parent information known to all. Hence, rural parents did not need complex cultural 
knowledge to distinguish among schools. The different outcomes of school choice 
were less a result from disparity in cultural knowledge, but were instead influenced by 
the varying strengthen of social connections.
In summary, peidu, or rural school choice, was highly contingent on family socioeco­
nomic standing. The rural families with the highest SES chose a quality school in the 
county capital or even the best school in the county for their children, the middle socio­
economic group chose township schools, while the children of the most disadvantaged 
languished in impoverished rural schools. As a result, rural schools suffered the loss of 
families with valuable economic, cultural and social resources.

Impact of rural parental school choice on rural schools
Similar with the findings in international literature, peidu, parental school choice in rural 
China, took away the cream of the crop and left the rural school in deeper struggles. 
Losing 40%–60% of the students to urban schools, village schools ran into crisis in all 
fronts, including funding, staffing and class instruction.
First, since school funding was determined based on the number of enrolled students, 
any decline in student enrolment significantly reduced the financial and physical 
resources allotted to rural schools. 
We have only 49 primary school students [other 16 are preschool students]. The funding is 
allocated based on RMB650 per student every academic year. Thus, we don’t have enough 
funding. The electricity even costs more than RMB2,000 a month. Our school still has 
debts. A lot of the teaching equipment is broken and we have no money to pay for 
repairs. (P-Yao)

Educational officers confirmed that the government prioritised the resource allocation for 
large schools. Rural schools with shrinking scales were positioned at disadvantage.
Second, the continual loss of students increased teacher turnover in rural schools. The 
expansion of urban schools to accommodate the influx of rural peidu students created a 
surge of demand for teachers. The vacancies in the new urban schools were usually filled 
by young and capable teachers from township and village schools. This resulted in a loss 
of quality teachers in rural schools, just as T-Jiang said, ‘In recent years, teachers who can 
and want to transfer to urban areas all have gone. Only the old teachers stay’. Meanwhile, 
the plummeting enrolment also made the government reluctant to assign new teachers 
to village schools, as confirmed by all six rural principals. 
My school has not received any new teachers in 5 to 6 years, but three teachers have retired in 
the past 5 years. No new teachers were assigned to our school to replace the retired teachers. 
Why? Because our school size is decreasing and they [the government] think that it is not 
worth allocating teachers to our school. (T-Wang)
Third, the loss of positive peers and the tiny class sizes due to peidu impeded rural schools’ 
teaching and learning. According to 83% of rural teachers who participated in our survey, stu­
dents who opted to leave rural schools typically exhibited superior academic performance 
and behaviour compared to those who stayed. The mini class size also dampened teachers’ 
enthusiasm in teaching, because ‘no students respond to me’ (T-Tian) and ‘the reading voice 
is too low, resulting in a bad teaching climate’ (T-Hu). Moreover, the loss of students hindered 
the implementation of classroom activities, because ‘it was difficult to organise group discus­
sion with a total of five students in the class’ (T-Hu). Also, the loss of positive peers lowered 
students’ sense of competitiveness and weakened students’ motivation for learning, accord­
ing to Principal of School 9. Eleven teachers indicated that the learning environment in rural 
schools had deteriorated following the advent of peidu, consequently impacting students’ 
academic performance negatively.
Worse still, the outflow of children and of teachers coupled by the subpar academic 
performance led to diminishing trust in rural schools among rural parents, resulting in 
more families leaving for urban schools. This distrust perpetuated the notion that rural 
schools were irredeemable and that urbanisation was the sole viable solution for rural 
education. Consequently, the remaining rural schools were caught in a vicious cycle, 
heightening the risk of school closures with each leaving child. Left behind families in 
rural schools suffered from anxiety about their future. One grandfather in a village 
school complained: 
Most children in our village are studying in urban schools. There are only a few students in 
this school now … If the school is closed, it will be difficult for the remaining children to 
go to school. The rich can send their children to the township. The poor will be deprived 
of education. (S-Hao’s grandfather)
Ultimately it was the most disadvantaged families low in both financial and social capital 
who bore the brunt of consequences stemming from the socially stratified peidu trend.
Discussion and conclusion
This study confirms that the prevalent trend of peidu in rural China represents a form of 
school choice exercised by rural parents. Rural families abandon village schools and move

children into urban schools in the county for better academic achievement. However, the 
policy background of this rural school choice is markedly distinct from that found in the 
international literature. While in the West and in some other developing countries school 
choice of various types is often driven by market-oriented reforms (Adnett and Davies 
2003; Cookson and Shapira 1997), in China the upsurge of rural school choice has been 
a component of the government’s urbanisation strategy.
The case of County G replicates the urbanisation model already implemented in other 
parts of China (see Zhang 2019), using schools to entice rural families into urban districts, 
especially the county capital, to fuel urban economy and increase urban population. The 
closing of village schools together with the expansion of urban schools creates a push– 
pull dynamic that drives rural families to relocate to urban areas. Similar to proponents 
who argue that market-driven school choice programs can alleviate educational inequal­
ity, local officials in China also regard urbanisation as a solution to rural-urban disparities. 
Findings from the current study, however, cast doubt on this claim.
Although emerged in different contexts, peidu, the school choice in rural China, is also a 
stratified practice along class lines, similar to school choice in international contexts (Ball 
2003; Debs and Cheung 2021). Rural households with best income, higher parental edu­
cation, and strong social networks manage to obtain places in county capital schools, 
while the middle stratum of rural families choose township schools and the most disadvan­
taged families stranded in village schools. Access to urban schools in small cities is much less 
restricted by household registration (hukou) than by family socioeconomic status. The most 
affluent and well-connected families manage to secure admissions into elite key schools, 
while families in poverty are barred from urban schools because of the high cost of urban 
living. The administrative discrimination based on hukou is increasingly and implicitly 
being replaced by market discrimination based on family socioeconomic standings.
This stratified peidu phenomenon serves to correct the common urban stereotype that 
regards all rural population as poor and backward, ignorant about the diversity and differ­
ences among rural households. In fact, as the current research indicates, the rural popu­
lation has developed considerable heterogeneity in socioeconomic conditions. Rural 
society has become increasingly differentiated with the rising market economy since 
China’s reform and opening up (Zhang 2015). For example, the income of the top 20% 
of rural income earners was 8.2 times higher than that of the lowest 20% in 2012, with 
the gap having grown by 1.8 times since 1990 (Yan and Chen 2015). Blind to these 
nuanced inequalities within the rural society, the urbanisation project through schooling 
functions to further divide the rural society, defeating its goal of reducing rural-urban 
inequality.
Obviously, the relaxation on hukou status does not signal the end of discrimination. 
Rather, it shifts from identity-based to class-based discrimination. The ‘visible hand’ is 
replaced by the covert ‘invisible hand’ of the market to achieve population screening, 
which ultimately serves urban economic development (urbanisation).
As the result of peidu, the school system, like in other school choice programs, con­
tinues to be stratified by social class, hence unequal. Specifically, the inequality 
between rural and urban schools is not diminished but rather intensified by peidu, con­
trary to what urbanisation advocates argue. The departure of rural children subjects 
village schools to decreased funding, accelerated teacher turnover, worsening academic 
performance, and an ultimately increased risk of school closures. These findings confirm 
COMPARATIVE EDUCATION 
313
the international literature that school choice is detrimental to disadvantaged schools, 
defeating its promise of educational equality (Ball 2003; Imberman 2011).
In conclusion, school choice, whether driven by marketisation or urbanisation, merely 
addresses the surface issues when it comes to educational inequality. The disparities 
among schools are rooted in the social stratification of school enrolment. Transferring 
the upper echelon of disadvantaged children and/or families to better schools does 
not reduce the privileges of elite schools or enhance the struggling ones; instead, it 
reinforces class-based stratification in education. Moreover, in the case of rural China, 
peidu goes beyond choice of schools and also involve considerable depopulation of 
rural communities (Wang and Teng 2022). Therefore, rural school choice in China not 
only jeopardises the well-being of rural schools, but also poses a threat to the sustainabil­
ity of agriculture and the rural society as a whole. These negative impacts call for an 
urgent reassessment of China’s current urbanisation strategy in small cities, particularly 
regarding its manipulation of schools to encourage peidu, i.e. the rural school choice.
